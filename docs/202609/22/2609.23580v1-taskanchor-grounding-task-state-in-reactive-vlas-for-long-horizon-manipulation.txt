## ABSTRACT 

Reactive vision–language–action (VLA) models struggle with long-horizon ma-nipulation when visually similar observations can correspond to different actions depending on the task stage or interaction history. We refer to this ambiguity as task-state aliasing and introduce T ASK ANCHOR , a lightweight adapter that grounds pretrained VLAs in execution history. T ASK ANCHOR combines history-conditioned visual refinement with a milestone-supervised task-state coordinate, a scalar representing the semantic stage of execution. These signals are injected through the native visual and language interfaces, respectively, without intro-ducing an explicit planner or modifying the action-generation mechanism. On RMBench, T ASK ANCHOR achieves approximately 4.9–5.5 × the average suc-cess rates of the published π0.5 and X-VLA baselines, with consistent gains on RoboMemArena and real robots. The added latency is only 2.08 ms per action chunk for π0.5.

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Vision–language–action models (VLAs) (Zheng et al., 2026; Black et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026; Guo et al., 2026) combine vision–language pretraining with action priors learned from large-scale robot trajectory data to generate effective manipulation actions. Most operate as reactive policies that predict short action chunks from the current observation and instruction. However, this formulation can struggle with long-horizon tasks (Chen et al., 2026; Lei et al., 2026) when the current observation does not provide the stage information or historical evidence needed for action selection. Visually similar observations may then correspond to distinct underlying task states and call for different actions, an ambiguity we term task-state aliasing . As illustrated in Fig. 1(a), this ambiguity takes two complementary forms. The first concerns subtask-stage identification : similar scenes may recur across stages but call for different subsequent actions. Without reliable stage information, a reactive policy may repeat com-pleted operations, omit necessary steps, or terminate prematurely. The second concerns within-stage action-branch selection : knowing the stage alone may not determine the appropriate action. Similar observations within a stage may call for different actions depending on earlier evidence, such as the initial object configuration or the outcome of a previous interaction. When this evidence is no longer visible, it must be recovered from history; otherwise, the policy may select an action that is locally plausible but inconsistent with the preceding execution. These two forms of aliasing call for complementary information: the current execution stage and the historical evidence needed to distinguish action branches within that stage. Existing methods typically address these needs through history modeling or high-level planning. History-aware poli-cies (Shi et al., 2026b; Koo et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2026a; Li et al., 2026) preserve detailed interaction evidence but often encode the execution stage only implicitly in high-dimensional temporal features. We hypothesize that pretrained VLAs already encode many of the task-consistent behaviors needed for long-horizon execution. Although optimized for local action prediction, these models are trained on temporally coherent trajectories spanning diverse task stages and interaction outcomes. A key limitation may therefore be the difficulty of selecting among suitable behaviors under ambiguous observations, rather than a lack of those behaviors. An effective task-state interface could thus enable long-horizon control through lightweight adaptation, without explicit subtask decomposition or high-level planning. Guided by this hypothesis, we introduce T ASK ANCHOR , a lightweight task-state adaptation frame-work for pretrained reactive VLAs. For within-stage action-branch selection, it retrieves execution-relevant evidence from interaction history and uses it to refine the current visual tokens through a residual update. For subtask-stage identification, it estimates a task-state coordinate: a continuous scalar that represents the semantic stage of task execution rather than a spatial position. Grounded in semantic milestones, the coordinate is encoded as a concise textual prompt appended to the orig-inal instruction. Both signals condition the pretrained VLA through its native visual and language interfaces, without modifying the architecture of the downstream vision–language backbone or ac-tion generator. This design enables T ASK ANCHOR to adapt pretrained policies through lightweight post-training, without an independent planner or explicit subtask decomposition. We evaluate T ASK ANCHOR on RMBench (Chen et al., 2026) and RoboMemArena (Lei et al., 2026). As shown in Fig. 1(b), T ASK ANCHOR -π0.5 improves the average success rate on RMBench by 

+53 .2 percentage points and the average CSR on RoboMemArena by +21 .8 percentage points, while adding only +2 .08 ms of latency per action chunk. Further analyses show that the inferred task-state signals systematically modulate action selection by the pretrained policy, providing evi-dence consistent with our central hypothesis. ## 2 RELATED WORK 

Pretrained Vision–Language–Action Models. Vision–language–action models combine vision– language pretraining with robot trajectories to unify semantic grounding and visuomotor control. RT-2 represents actions as language-model tokens to transfer Internet-scale visual–semantic knowl-edge to robotics (Brohan et al., 2023), while Octo, OpenVLA, and XVLA learn generalist policies from large-scale cross-embodiment data (Team et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2026). The π series instead couples a pretrained vision–language backbone with a flow-matching action expert for continuous control (Black et al., 2024; 2025). Despite their rich semantic, spatial, and be-havioral priors, these models are typically deployed reactively without explicit task progress, making visually similar stages prone to repeated actions or premature termination. T ASK ANCHOR addresses this limitation through lightweight post-training that conditions action generation on task state while preserving the pretrained policy. 

Temporal Context for Long-Horizon Control. Prior work incorporates temporal context through frame stacking, attention, recurrent states, or compact memory. RoboFlamingo uses sequential vi-sual context (Li et al., 2024); HAMLET introduces moment tokens and lightweight memory (Koo et al., 2026); MemoryVLA selectively retrieves task-relevant observations from a perceptual-cognitive memory bank (Shi et al., 2026b); and Long-VLA exploits task phases through phase-aware input masking (Fan et al., 2025). These methods establish the importance of history for non-Markovian dependencies and long-horizon skill composition. Rather than directly exposing long histories to the action generator, T ASK ANCHOR distills task-relevant evidence into a residual correction of the current visual representation and a compact global progress coordinate, targeting stage-level observation aliasing rather than merely expanding the temporal receptive field. 

High-Level Task Planning and Explicit Reasoning. Another line of work represents long-horizon structure through planning or semantic reasoning. SayCan and Inner Monologue generate skill se-quences or intermediate decisions using language models and low-level policies (Ahn et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2022). More recent methods, including π0.5, LoHoVLA, VLA-OS, Mem-0, Pred-iMem, and CoT-VLA, explore semantic subtasks, hierarchical execution, visual plans, or interme-diate reasoning traces (Black et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026; Lei 3et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2025). These approaches organize behavior around explicit subtask or goal representations. In contrast, T ASK ANCHOR generates neither free-form subtasks, future trajecto-ries, nor candidate plans, and introduces no separate planning loop. Instead, it investigates whether a low-bandwidth indication of task position can select stage-consistent behavior from a pretrained VLA, thereby activating existing behavioral priors without specifying the next action semantically. 

Task Progress Estimation and Progress-Aware Policies. Task progress has supported policy supervision, action guidance, reward modeling, and failure recovery. PALM models continuous within-subtask progress (Liu et al., 2026); ProgressVLA uses normalized trajectory time to guide diffusion-based action sampling (Yan et al., 2026); ProgVLA reweights flow-matching imitation learning using progress predictions (Kim et al., 2026); and See–Plan–Rewind grounds progress in spatial milestones for stagnation detection and recovery (Dai et al., 2026). T ASK ANCHOR instead defines global progress as the fraction of completed semantic milestones, reducing sensitivity to execution speed and stage duration. Progress serves neither as a reward, critic, sampling objective, nor recovery rule; it is converted into a concise textual condition and passed through the pretrained VLA’s language interface. Combined with context-conditioned visual adaptation, this provides a lightweight task-state interface connecting interaction history to pretrained behavioral priors with-out free-form planning, a latent world model, or a separate high-level controller.

4.3 ROBO MEM ARENA EVALUATION 

Benchmark and protocol. RoboMemArena (Lei et al., 2026) contains 26 tasks with an average length of 1,076 steps; 104 of 151 annotated subtasks require historical information. Counting and sequential execution emphasize task-stage tracking, while occlusion and transfer require retaining earlier states and outcomes. Following the standard protocol, we use 100 demonstrations per task. T ASK ANCHOR -π0.5predicts 10-action chunks, samples historical observations every five environment steps, and uses a single configuration across all tasks. Both task-state signals are inferred from observation history without additional annotations. We compare against π0.5 (Black et al., 2025), HiF-VLA (Lin et al., 2026), MemoryVLA (Shi et al., 2026b), MemER (Sridhar et al., 2026), and PrediMem (Lei et al., 2026). 

Results. As shown in Table 2, T ASK ANCHOR -π0.5achieves 44.4% average TSR and 60.5% aver-age CSR, improving over reactive π0.5 by 22.9 and 21.8 points, respectively. Compared with Pred-iMem, T ASK ANCHOR -π0.5further improves average TSR and CSR by 5.9 and 5.3 points. These results demonstrate that task-state information and historical evidence improve long-horizon execu-tion beyond reactive policies and memory-based approaches. The improvements are notable in transfer and counting tasks, where T ASK ANCHOR -π0.5achieves

## 5 CONCLUSION 

We presented T ASK ANCHOR , a lightweight task-state adaptation framework that improves pre-trained reactive VLAs on long-horizon manipulation. It addresses task-state aliasing by retrieving historical evidence for action-branch selection and “representing the execution stage through a task-state coordinate for subtask-stage identification.. These complementary signals are injected through the native visual and language interfaces of the pretrained policy without introducing a separate planning loop. Experiments on RMBench and RoboMemArena demonstrate substantial improve-ments in long-horizon performance with only 3.35% additional latency. Ablations and controlled 9interventions further show that the two pathways provide complementary information and that the progress coordinate actively redirects stage-specific behavior. These results suggest that lightweight task-state conditioning can unlock task-consistent long-horizon behaviors from pretrained reactive VLAs without explicit planning.
