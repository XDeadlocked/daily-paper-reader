## Abstract 

Autoregressive (AR) video generation degrades over long horizons due to an over-looked train-inference discrepancy we term KV eviction mismatch : models train on short clips where all context frames reside in the KV cache, but at inference, memory constraints force distant frames to be evicted from the KV cache – re-moving context the model was conditioned on. Rather than simulating eviction via context truncation – which discards temporal information the model still needs and degrades motion coherence – we keep the context but while progressively reducing the influence of distant frames, making their eventual eviction negligi-ble. To guide this design, we introduce the positional response R(∆ , t denoise ),a perturbation-based sensitivity measure revealing that context influence decays steeply with temporal distance and varies systematically across denoising steps. Motivated by this analysis, we propose Recency Forcing , which applies a non-positive, timestep-dependent bias, termed Temporal Response Bias (TRB) , on pre-softmax attention logits derived directly from R, closing the train–inference gap without modifying context length or training objectives. We further introduce 

Biased Attention Reparameterization (BAR) , an exact reformulation that moves the bias outside the softmax, making TRB a standard FlashAttention call at zero overhead. Recency Forcing operates in both training-free mode and training-based 

mode. Experiments on VBench and VBench-Long demonstrate state-of-the-art long-horizon generation quality at no additional inference cost. 

## 1 Introduction 

Autoregressive (AR) video generation predicts each frame from past context, enabling streaming and interactive applications with unbounded temporal rollouts, such as world modeling [18, 34, 40], game simulation [4, 41, 42, 56, 59], and robotics [27, 48]—settings where the bidirectional attention used by most state-of-the-art models [23, 43, 51] is fundamentally incompatible. Recent distillation-based methods (CausVid [55], Self-Forcing [19], Causal Forcing [63]) enable fast few-step causal generation via distribution matching, but suffer from a key limitation: they are trained on short clips (e.g., 5 seconds, 21 latent frames in Wan2.1) and degrade rapidly when rolled out to long horizons. Recent work extends these models to longer videos by adding an attention sink [47] as a persistent anchor, then layering additional mechanisms on top. These efforts cluster into three families: 

(1) KV-cache compression and pruning controls memory growth by selectively pruning past context [7, 35, 53, 57, 62]; (2) RoPE-based methods modify positional encoding for better length

We identify an aspect that prior work does not address: a train–test discrepancy we call KV eviction mismatch . During training, all context frames are accessible; at inference, memory constraints force distant frames to be evicted [19, 55], removing context the model was trained to depend on. The most direct fix – truncating context during training [33, 49] – discards the temporal information needed for consistent motion, and we observe degraded motion fidelity as a result (empirically analyzed in Sec. 4.2 - Fig. 3). We instead keep the full context but progressively diminish the influence of distant frames so that by eviction time, their contribution is negligible. The key question is what shape this decay should take. We introduce the positional response 

R(∆ , t denoise ), a perturbation-based sensitivity score measuring how the model’s output changes when the context frame at temporal distance ∆ from the current denoising frame is perturbed at denoising step tdenoise . The measured response reveals a two-axis structure: early timesteps focus sharply on nearby frames, while later timesteps draw information from a broader context window. Any fixed decay schedule cannot capture this, motivating a timestep-dependent design. We propose Recency Forcing , whose core component is Temporal Response Bias (TRB) : an additive softmax bias decaying with temporal distance at a rate that varies with the denoising timestep – steep early, gentle late – mirroring R. Since distant frames already receive negligible weight at the eviction boundary, their removal becomes information-preserving, closing the train–test gap by design. Recency Forcing targets attention weighting, a layer none of the three prior families modifies, and composes cleanly with all of them. To make TRB practical, we introduce Biased Attention Reparameterization (BAR) , an exact reformulation that moves the bias outside the softmax, making TRB a standard FlashAttention [10] call with no kernel modifications and zero inference overhead. We evaluate on VBench [20] and VBench-Long [21], generating videos up to 60 seconds— 12 × longer than the training horizon. The approach supports two modes: training-free , requiring no modification to the base model, where our method serves as a plug-in enhancement to Self-Forcing [19] and improves the VBench-Long quality score to 82.63 with no additional inference overhead ; and 

training-based , requiring only ∼12 hours of DMD retraining, further improving the score to 84.02, achieving state-of-the-art performance and surpassing prior methods on several metrics. Video generation models have evolved from U-Net diffusion models [5, 15] to scalable Diffusion Transformers (DiT) [36], with recent models such as CogVideoX [51], HunyuanVideo [23], Wan [43], Cosmos [1], and LTX-Video [16] achieving high-quality synthesis via transformer architectures over spatiotemporal tokens. These models generate all frames jointly using bidirectional attention, serving as the backbone for many AR models. Among them, Wan [43] has emerged as the dominant open-source base for AR video generation [19, 55, 63]; we likewise build on Wan2.1. 

Autoregressive video generation. A growing line of work extends bidirectional video diffusion to autoregressive generation [17, 28, 30, 39, 45, 58, 60]. Early AR methods such as NOVA [11], SkyReels [6], and PyramidFlow [24] generate chunk-by-chunk but require many sampling steps. More recent distillation-based methods achieve real-time generation: CausVid [55] distills a bidirectional DiT into a few-step causal generator; Self-Forcing [19] reduces exposure bias by conditioning on the model’s own outputs; Causal Forcing [63] further improves the ODE pretraining stage. All of these are trained on short clips ( ∼5 seconds) and degrade over longer horizons — the regime we target. 

Non-autoregressive long video generation. Most DiT-based models are limited to 5–10 second clips, and extending them is non-trivial. Hierarchical and sliding-window approaches such as NUWA-XL [54], RIFLEx [61], and FIFO-Diffusion [22] extend video length but do not exploit causal structure, making them less suitable for streaming settings. 

Autoregressive long video generation. The autoregressive paradigm is a natural fit for long-horizon generation, but AR models alone are insufficient: trained on short clips, they suffer from drift when extended, as observed in [19, 63]. Recent work addresses this by extending distilled AR frameworks, introducing an attention sink [47] as an anchor for long-term consistency, and further incorporating additional mechanisms. These efforts cluster into three families. (1) KV-cache compression and pruning controls memory growth by selectively discarding or compressing past context. DeepForc-ing [53] uses participative compression to preserve important tokens while discarding redundant ones, reducing drift; related methods include PackForcing [35], RelaxForcing [62], and MALT [57]. 

(2) RoPE-based methods modify positional encoding for better length extrapolation, including 

∞-RoPE [52], Rolling Forcing [31], LoL [9], and Anchor variants [26, 50]. (3) Correction-based methods expose the model to its own degraded outputs or use RL feedback to stabilize rollout. Self-Resampling [14] feeds the model its own generated errors, BAgger [37] constructs corrective trajectories, Pathwise [46] aligns rollout trajectories with teacher distributions, and Self-Forcing++ [8] simulates long rollouts and optimizes with RL-based feedback [33]. These approaches improve long-horizon AR generation from different angles – memory, positional generalization, and training robustness – all aiming to extend context capacity. In contrast, our method addresses the train–inference mismatch by modulating attention to past frames based on temporal distance and denoising timestep.

Context partition. Efficiency. BAR requires three lightweight modifications to a standard attention call: (i) rescale each value vector vj by its decay weight wj = exp( Bi,j ), (ii) append a scalar wj to vj to form ˜vj , and (iii) normalize the output by its final channel. The attention computation itself is an unmodified FlashAtten-tion call, inheriting all of its memory efficiency and speed. Training and inference modes. Recency Forcing supports two operating modes: training-free and 

training-based . In both modes, the decay weights wj = exp( Bi,j ) are computed using the power-law function Eq. (8). In the training-based mode, the model is further fine-tuned during a short DMD retraining stage ( ∼12 hours on 4 H100s), yielding additional gains. Importantly, BAR introduces no additional computational cost at inference in either mode. 

## 4 Experiments 

Implementation details. We build Recency Forcing on Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B [43], replacing standard SDPA with BAR-based TRB in all self-attention layers. The training-free variant is applied directly to chunk-wise Self-Forcing [19], while the training-based variant fine-tunes only the DMD stage of Causal Forcing [63]. We use Lattention = 21 and Lglobal = Lrecent = 3 . Additional training and implementation details are provided in Appendix- Sec. B. 

Evaluation. We evaluate on VBench [20] for short-video quality and VBench-Long [21] for long-horizon consistency, using 200 MovieGen [38] prompts following Rolling Forcing [31], with a disjoint 50-prompt set for ablations. All videos are 60 seconds at 16 fps, 832 ×480 . We additionally conduct a user study with 23 participants across four aspects: color consistency, motion dynamics, subject consistency, and overall quality. Details are in Appendix-Sec. C. 

Baselines. We compare against recent autoregressive video generation methods across two settings. For short video generation (VBench), we include SkyReelsV2 [6], MAGI-1 [2], NOVA [11], Pyramid Flow [24], CausVid [55], Self-Forcing [19], LongLive [49], Causal Forcing [63], and Rolling Forcing [31]. For long video generation (VBench-Long), we compare against both training-free methods – Infinity RoPE [52] and Deep Forcing [53] – and training-based AR methods — Self-Forcing [19], Causal Forcing [63], Rolling Forcing [31], and LongLive [49]. 

4.1 Comparison with prior approaches Quantitative results. Tab. 2 reports VBench results for short video quality, and Tab. 3 reports VBench-Long results for long-horizon generation. We highlight three takeaways. First, short video quality is preserved. Our method achieves the best overall score on VBench, demonstrating that integrating TRB introduces no degradation in short-video quality – the bias has negligible effect when all context frames fit within the training window. 4.2 Ablation Studies 

We conduct ablation studies on a held-out set of 50 prompts as the validation set, evaluating on VBench-Long with 60-second videos. All metrics are higher-is-better . Unless stated otherwise, ablations use the training-based variant with power-law decay. 

Context truncation vs. temporal decay. We first compare the two design directions in Sec. 3.2 for mitigating KV eviction mismatch in Fig. 3 and Tab. 4. Context truncation (9-frame context) partially reduces the mismatch but removes useful temporal context, causing unnatural motion and unwanted scene change. This increases the dynamic degree (88.58) while harming the overall quality. In contrast, our temporal decay suppresses distant-frame influence smoothly, achieving better VBench-Long performance than both truncation and Causal Forcing. 

Choice of bias function. We compare linear, logarithmic, and power-law decay forms (Eqs. (6) to (8)). As shown in Tab. 5, power-law decay performs best overall, consistent with its convex profile better matching the steep drop-off observed in the positional response curves ˜R. Linear and logarithmic decay both under-suppress distant frames relative to the empirical response, leading to residual drift. We adopt power-law decay for all subsequent experiments.

## 5 Conclusion 

We propose Recency Forcing , whose core component is Temporal Response Bias (TRB) : an additive softmax bias decaying with temporal distance at a rate that varies with the denoising timestep 9– steep early, gentle late – mirroring R. Since distant frames already receive negligible weight at the eviction boundary, their removal becomes information-preserving, closing the train–test gap by design. Recency Forcing targets attention weighting, a layer none of the three prior families modifies, and composes cleanly with all of them. To make TRB practical, we introduce Biased Attention Reparameterization (BAR) , an exact reformulation that moves the bias outside the softmax, making TRB a standard FlashAttention [10] call with no kernel modifications and zero inference overhead. 10 References

## B Implementation Details 

We implement Recency Forcing on top of a 1.3B autoregressive video diffusion model following Causal Forcing [63], built on the Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B backbone [43]. We replace standard SDPA with BAR-based TRB in all self-attention layers. The model generates videos at a resolution of 832 × 480 ,

Lattention = 21 latent frames, Lglobal = Lrecent = Lcurrent = 3 latent frames, with TRB applied to the remaining history frames. 

Analysis. The positional response R(∆ , t dempose ) analysis is conducted on both chunk-wise and frame-wise Causal Forcing [63], only frame-wise version is reported in Sec. 3.2 for conceptual clarity. 

Training. Our method can be directly applied to pretrained distilled autoregressive video models [19, 63]. For the training-based variant, we initialize from a pretrained causal ODE checkpoint of Causal Forcing [63] and fine-tune only the DMD stage with TRB following the Self-Forcing [19] training protocol. Training is conducted on short clips (5 seconds) sampled from a filtered and LLM-augmented version of VidProM [44] for 3,500 steps with batch size 4 on H100 GPUs. During training, we sample αbase ∼ U (0 , 2) and γbase ∼ U (0 , 1) , and set β = Lhistory .

Inference. During inference, KV-cache eviction is applied as in prior work, while TRB adaptively reduces reliance on evicted frames. All experiments use chunk-wise Causal Forcing [63] as the base model, with a chunk size of 3 latent frames, temporal distance ∆ is calculated between chunks. For the training-free setting, we apply our method to Self-Forcing [19] to ensure a fair comparison with other training-free methods (e.g., Deep Forcing [53] and ∞-RoPE [52]). For long video generation, we adopt relative RoPE [31, 53, 62], where the RoPE indices of the global context are shifted to align with the immediately preceding history frames. For fair comparison, we also incorporate the Causal Forcing baseline with both global sink tokens and relative RoPE. 

## C User Study 

We conduct a human evaluation using a one-versus-other protocol. 

Setup. We conduct a user study with 23 participants and evaluate on randomly sampled 20 prompts. For each prompt, we generate videos from four methods: Self Forcing [19], Rolling Forcing [25], Longlive [49], and our Recency Forcing. Each participant is assigned up to 20 pairwise comparisons randomly sampled from four methods. 

Protocol. We adopt a Two-Alternative Forced Choice (2AFC) protocol. Participants are shown two videos at a time and asked to select the better one according to the following criteria: • Color consistency : which video has more consistent colors across frames? • Dynamic motion : which video show more natural and convincing motion dynamics? • Subject consistency : which video keep the subject more consistent over time? • Overall quality : Which video has better overall quality? The evaluation is conducted via a web-based interface with randomized ordering to avoid bias. 

Statistics. In total, we collected over 1620 responses. Results are reported as preference percentages. 

Results. As shown in Fig. 6, participants consistently prefer Recency Forcing across all four evalu-ation aspects. The largest margin appears in motion dynamics, suggesting that TRB’s suppression of distant-frame drift improves perceptual identity preservation — an effect not fully captured by automatic metrics.
