## Abstract 

Camera-conditioned video generation requires positional encoding that remains reliable under changes in camera motion, lens configuration, and scene structure. However, existing attention-level camera encodings either provide ray-only camera signals or rely on pinhole camera geometry, limiting their applicability to gen-eral camera control under the Unified Camera Model, including wide-angle and fisheye lenses. To address this limitation, we propose Curved Ray Expectation Positional Encoding (CRePE) . CRePE represents each image token as a depth-aware positional distribution along its source ray, providing a Unified Camera Model-compatible positional encoding that captures the projected-path geometry induced by wide-angle and fisheye cameras. CRePE is implemented through a 

Geometric Attention Adapter added to frozen video DiTs, injecting token-wise scene-distance information into selected attention layers and stabilizing it with pseudo supervision from a monocular geometry foundation model. This design leads to more stable camera control and improves several geometry-aware and perceptual-quality metrics, while remaining competitive on video-quality metrics. Controlled positional-encoding ablations show a better overall average rank than a RayRoPE-style endpoint PE baseline, demonstrating the effectiveness of UCM-aware projected-path integration across diverse camera models. Furthermore, by extending the same positional-encoding pathway to external geometry control through Radial MixForcing , CRePE supports external radial-map control for scene-geometry-conditioned generation and source-video motion transfer beyond camera control. 

## 1 Introduction 

Camera-conditioned video generation [ 1 ] requires models to synthesize videos that follow specified camera trajectories and lens configurations. Recent studies have expanded the scope of camera conditioning from direct camera conditioning to attention-level positional encoding [ 2, 3 ]. By incorporating camera geometry directly into positional encoding, video diffusion transformers [ 4, 5]can more explicitly model the relative positional relationships among latent tokens under view changes. In particular, UCPE [ 3] encodes the viewing ray of each token based on a unified central-camera representation, demonstrating the possibility of camera control under the Unified Camera Model (UCM), including wide-angle and fisheye cameras. This marks an important step toward handling diverse lens models within a unified positional-encoding framework. However, relative-ray-based positional encoding still has a limitation. A viewing ray indicates the direction associated with a token, but it does not specify where the underlying scene content lies along that ray. When the camera moves, token correspondences depend not only on ray direction but also on the scene position along the ray. Thus, while ray-only encoding provides a stable way to inject camera geometry, it remains limited in representing more refined positional relationships coupled with scene geometry. This limitation becomes more pronounced for wide-angle and fisheye cameras, where projection geometry changes nonlinearly. To address this problem, we propose Curved Ray Expectation Positional Encoding (CRePE) .Instead of representing each token only by its viewing ray, CRePE models it as a depth-aware positional distribution along the source ray, reflecting possible scene positions associated with the token. CRePE is designed to be compatible with the Unified Camera Model, naturally capturing the nonlinear projection geometry that arises under diverse lens configurations. This allows the positional encoding to account for how tokens may align with the underlying scene structure and to refine token relationships under view changes. To apply CRePE to camera-conditioned video generation, we introduce a Geometric Attention Adapter for frozen video DiTs. The adapter predicts token-wise scene distance and incorporates it into the positional relationships used in attention. Pseudo radial-distance supervision from a monocular geometry foundation model serves as an auxiliary geometric anchor, helping the predicted geometry signal remain stable during training. Through this design, CRePE extends ray-only positional encoding into a scene-geometry-aware positional encoding while preserving the adapter-based camera-conditioning structure. 2We evaluate CRePE against camera-conditioned video generation baselines [ 6, 3 ] and analyze the effect of the positional encoding itself through controlled ablations. CRePE improves camera-control, lens, orientation, and several perceptual-quality metrics under the Unified Camera Model, while remaining competitive on video-quality metrics and showing a favorable average-rank trend over a RayRoPE-style endpoint PE baseline in the controlled setting. Furthermore, inspired by YonoSplat [7], we extend the same positional-encoding pathway toward external geometry control via Radial MixForcing . By aligning tokens with geometry-aware positional information in a compatible representation, CRePE suggests a promising route for external radial-map control in scene-geometry-conditioned generation and source-video motion transfer. Figure 1 illustrates representative wide-angle and fisheye camera-conditioned generations together with CRePE’s internal radial-distance maps. Table 1 summarizes how CRePE differs from UCPE and RayRoPE-style endpoint PE along camera-model support, ray-position modeling, geometric grounding, and external-control capability. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose Curved Ray Expectation Positional Encoding , a Unified Camera Model-compatible positional encoding that considers not only ray direction but also possible scene positions along the ray, enabling depth-aware positional encoding for curved projected-path geometry. • We apply CRePE to camera-conditioned video generation through a Geometric Attention Adapter for frozen video DiTs, incorporating token-wise scene distance into attention and stabilizing geometry prediction with pseudo radial-distance supervision. • We provide controlled comparisons showing that CRePE improves camera-control, lens/orientation, and perceptual metrics over camera-conditioned baselines, and achieves a better average rank than a RayRoPE-style endpoint PE baseline. We further demonstrate that the same pathway can be extended through Radial MixForcing toward external radial-map control for scene-geometry-conditioned generation and source-video motion transfer. 

## 2 Related Work 

Camera-conditioned video generation. Camera-conditioned video models control generated mo-tion using pose embeddings, camera trajectories, rendered geometric guidance, or dedicated camera branches [ 8 –11 , 6 ]. ReCamMaster [ 6] is a strong external baseline for trajectory-conditioned genera-tion, but its formulation is tied to a pinhole setting. UCPE [ 3 ] instead conditions video diffusion with unified-camera rays and is therefore the closest baseline for our setting. CRePE builds on the UCPE interface but adds a token-wise ray-position variable inside attention. 

Geometric positional encoding. Rotary positional encoding [ 12 ] makes attention depend on relative position. Multi-view variants replace image-grid position with camera-aware quantities: CaPE [ 13 ], GTA [ 14 ], and PRoPE [ 15 ] encode relative camera geometry; raymap methods concatenate ray origins or directions to features [ 16 – 19 ]; RayRoPE [ 2 ] predicts a token-wise ray-position interval and applies expected RoPE after projection. Our RayRoPE-style endpoint PE ablation follows this endpoint-interval approximation. CRePE differs by using UCM projection for unified central cameras and by integrating the phasor over a curved projected path rather than a single endpoint-defined interval. 

Central-camera geometry and radial distance. Wide-angle and fisheye videos are better described by central-camera models such as UCM than by pinhole intrinsics. Radial distance—distance from the camera center along the viewing ray—is a natural scalar for such cameras because it remains defined regardless of lens distortion. Recent geometric foundation models provide dense monocular scene geometry across diverse cameras [ 20 – 23 ]. We use these predictions only as pseudo radial-distance anchors for CRePE’s latent ray-position head. 3.4 Radial MixForcing 

Radial MixForcing is a training-time teacher-substitution algorithm for the CRePE ray-position pathway. Its purpose is to teach the adapter to accept externally supplied radial maps while preserving a safe camera-only fallback. be a sampled teacher-substitution mask. We define pseudo-GT validity on the raw metric output as 

Vgt = 1 [valid UniK3D (ˆ rm) ∧ 0 < ˆrm ≤ rmax ] , rmax = 20 m . (6) Distances above the validity threshold are therefore treated as unknown, not as valid targets clipped to 20 m . CRePE consumes the effective radial distribution

The second branch covers every invalid ray: if UniK3D fails, the distance is non-finite or non-positive, the validity filters mark it unreliable or outside the supported range, or the raw metric pseudo target is above 20 m , no teacher geometry is injected and that ray uses the model prediction. The auxiliary loss in Eq. (4) is still applied only on valid tokens, so the head learns from reliable pseudo radial distances while invalid regions remain self-predicted. At inference, an external radial map follows the same rule after validity filtering and scale normalization: valid external rays may replace the predicted tensor, while invalid or missing rays fall back to (μpred , σ pred ). We use the supervised CRePE model for the main camera-only benchmark and use the MixForcing-trained model for external-radial-map qualitative examples. The schedule and teacher-width details are in Appendix F. 

3.5 Layer Placement 

Applying CRePE to every layer is unnecessary and can degrade visual quality. We probe the frozen DiT layers for recoverable radial-distance information and find that middle layers provide the best tradeoff between geometric control and generation quality. Our default model applies CRePE to the middle 10 blocks and retains UCPE modulation elsewhere. The probing curve and placement ablation are in Appendix C. 

## 4 Experiments 

4.1 Setup Backbone and training. We use Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B [ 5] as the video diffusion backbone and keep its weights frozen. CRePE is trained through the camera-conditioning adapter for 10K steps using AdamW. The default setting applies CRePE to the middle 10 DiT blocks, uses K = 5 radial-distance breakpoints, and supervises the radial-distance head at every CRePE layer. Full hyperparameters are in Appendix B. 6Dataset. We train and evaluate on PanShot, a multi-camera video dataset covering panning shots with pinhole, wide-angle, and fisheye camera settings, following [ 3]. Each clip has a camera trajectory and UCM lens parameters. Pseudo radial-distance maps are produced offline with UniK3D [ 23 ] and used only for training supervision or optional external-radial-map interventions. Raw metric pseudo distances are filtered before normalization: outputs that fail UniK3D validity checks, are non-finite or non-positive, fall outside the supported distance range, or exceed 20 m are marked invalid rather than clipped into supervision. Dataset and evaluation details are listed in Appendix G. 

Metrics. We evaluate camera controllability using recovered rotation error (RotErr), scale-aligned translation error (TransErr), and the aggregate CamMC score. We evaluate generation quality with FVD, FVD-C, FID, Inception Score, VBench metrics, and CLIP-based image/text scores. Lens and orientation diagnostics include radial-distortion-coefficient errors (k1, k 2), pitch error, gravity error, and up-vector error, following [3] 

4.2 Baselines 

The main evaluation compares against ReCamMaster [ 6 ] and UCPE [ 3 ]. Because ReCamMaster is originally formulated for video re-rendering, we use it as an external camera-control baseline under the same recovered-camera evaluation protocol; UCPE remains the architecture-matched baseline with the same Wan backbone and adapter interface but without CRePE or radial-distance supervision. We evaluate RayRoPE-style endpoint PE [ 2] in a separate positional-encoding ablation, where it isolates the difference between endpoint-based expected RoPE and CRePE’s UCM-compatible curved projected-path integration.

4.5 External Radial-Map Conditioning and Motion Transfer 

CRePE exposes the ray-position variable through the positional-encoding pathway. Although our primary benchmark is camera-conditioned generation, this interface also allows intervention: at inference, CRePE-Mix can replace the predicted radial-distance tensor with an external radial map after the same validity filtering and scale normalization used for pseudo targets. We show two qualitative uses in Figure 4. First, the same prompt and camera trajectory can be generated with different radial maps, changing scene layout through the PE pathway. Second, a source video can provide a sequence of radial maps, allowing motion and scene-geometry transfer while using a new 8text prompt and target camera trajectory. Invalid or missing external radial values do not overwrite the predicted tensor; those rays continue to use the model prediction according to Eq. (7) . 4.6 Limitations 

CRePE improves geometry-aware camera conditioning, but does not solve all camera-control chal-lenges. Camera recovery from generated videos is itself noisy, so pose and geometry metrics should be interpreted together with qualitative examples. The radial-distance supervision depends on the quality and scale normalization of monocular geometry predictions, which may introduce errors when the estimated geometry is unreliable or poorly calibrated. In addition, CRePE currently focuses on central-camera models represented by UCM, and extending the formulation to more general non-central or rolling-shutter camera models remains future work. Finally, external-radial-map conditioning and motion transfer are presented as qualitative extensions; a dedicated benchmark for those tasks is left for future work. 

## 5 Conclusion 

We presented CRePE, a curved-ray expectation positional encoding for unified-camera-controlled video generation. CRePE represents each token as a depth-aware positional distribution along its source ray and integrates the expected RoPE phasor along the UCM-projected curved path, extending ray-only camera conditioning with token-wise scene-geometry awareness while remaining compatible with non-pinhole lenses. A Geometric Attention Adapter realizes this design on a frozen video DiT, with pseudo radial-distance supervision from a monocular geometry foundation model stabilizing the ray-position channel and probe-selected middle-layer placement keeping the adapter compact. 9In experiments, CRePE improves camera-control, lens/orientation, and several perceptual-quality metrics over ReCamMaster and UCPE across pinhole and non-pinhole lenses, while remaining competitive on video-quality metrics. Controlled positional-encoding ablations show that CRePE attains a better average rank than RayRoPE-style endpoint PE, isolating the benefit of UCM-aware curved projected-path integration over endpoint-based approximations. Beyond camera-only conditioning, the same positional-encoding pathway exposes a controllable interface for external scene geometry. Through Radial MixForcing, CRePE-Mix learns to accept externally supplied radial maps under the same validity filtering and scale normalization used during training, while falling back to model-predicted geometry on rays where external supervision is missing or unreliable. This unifies camera and scene-geometry control under a single adapter and enables qualitative scene-geometry-conditioned generation and source-video motion transfer with new prompts and target trajectories. We hope CRePE serves as a useful building block for geometry-aware, camera-controllable video generation under diverse lens configurations.

## C Layer Placement Ablation 

Probe protocol. For each frozen Wan2.1 block, we train an independent lightweight probe with the same input features used by the adapter to predict the valid, normalized UniK3D radial-distance targets on the token grid. The backbone and video diffusion objective are not updated during probing; 12 the probe is optimized only with the radial-distance supervision loss and evaluated on held-out Panshot clips. Lower probing error indicates that the layer exposes recoverable ray-position information to a small adapter head, not that the final generator has been trained with CRePE at that layer. 

Placement selection. The probe curve is used as a selection guide rather than as the sole criterion. We evaluate contiguous CRePE windows on the first 10, middle 10, last 10, and all 30 DiT blocks. Middle-layer placement provides the best overall rank and the strongest pose/lens/orientation tradeoff, while retaining UCPE-style relative-ray modulation in the remaining blocks. This is why the default model applies CRePE to blocks [10 , 20) and uses UCPE modulation elsewhere. 

How to read the appendix metrics. Across the appendix tables, arrows indicate the preferred direc-tion for each metric. Pose metrics measure whether the generated video follows the requested camera trajectory: rotation error and scale-aligned translation error are reported separately, while CamMC summarizes camera-motion consistency. Lens metrics evaluate the recovered radial-distortion param-eters (k1, k 2), which are especially important for wide-angle and fisheye settings. Orientation metrics measure whether the generated video preserves the requested camera orientation, including pitch, roll, gravity, lateral, and up-vector consistency. Text/image alignment metrics report CLIP-based image and text scores, while FID, FVD, FVD-C, and IS measure visual and temporal generation quality. VBench metrics further evaluate perceptual quality and temporal consistency. Because these metrics capture different aspects of the generated video, we use average rank to summarize the overall tradeoff rather than selecting a model based on a single metric. ## E Radial-Distance Supervision Details 

Pseudo radial-distance maps are generated by UniK3D [ 23 ]. We apply validity filtering before normalization. A raw metric pseudo target ˆrm is invalid if UniK3D fails, is marked unreliable by our validity filters, is non-finite or non-positive, falls outside the supported distance range, or exceeds 15 20 m . These far-field targets are not clipped to 20 m ; they are excluded from the loss and, under Radial MixForcing, fall back to the model prediction for that ray. We divide only valid metric maps by a per-clip near-distance statistic to obtain normalized targets, pool them to the token grid, and mask invalid regions. From (μ, σ ), the normalized prediction is ¯r = exp( μ). The uncertainty scale s is derived from the variance of a uniform distribution over the normalized interval [exp( μ − | σ|), exp( μ + |σ|)] :

Var( r) = (exp( μ + |σ|) − exp( μ − | σ|)) 2

12 . (13) We use s = pmax(Var( r), 10 −6) with a ceiling of 10, α = 1 .0, and λrad = 10 −3. Supervision is gated off for the noisiest 3% of diffusion timesteps. 

## F Additional Radial MixForcing Details 

Section 3.4 gives the main Radial MixForcing algorithm. Here we keep only the implementation choices that are too detailed for the main text. 

Teacher interval width. When a valid pseudo radial target is substituted, we use σT = 0 .1. Invalid pseudo targets never create a teacher interval: if the pseudo distance is unavailable, marked unreliable by the validity filters, non-finite or non-positive, outside the supported range, or above 20 m in raw metric units, the effective CRePE geometry for that ray remains (μpred , σ pred ).

Schedule. We use a piecewise-linear schedule that starts at full substitution probability, decays between steps 1000 and 7000, and then holds a small floor. For block-frame sampling, the floor is 0.1; for video-level sampling, the floor is 0.5. The mask is sampled once per forward pass and shared across all CRePE layers. 

External-radial-map inference. For qualitative scene-geometry conditioning and source-video motion transfer, we intentionally provide an external radial map at inference and route it through the same CRePE pathway. The same validity and scale-normalization rule is used as in training: valid external radial values can overwrite the predicted radial tensor after normalization, while invalid or missing values use the model prediction for the corresponding ray. This changes only the positional coefficients, not the video backbone. 

## G Dataset and Evaluation Details 

Panshot contains panning videos with camera trajectories and UCM lens parameters. The train/test split prevents scene overlap. The dataset spans pinhole, wide-angle, and fisheye settings with horizontal FoV and UCM distortion; the main tables report pinhole and non-pinhole results separately where appropriate. Pseudo radial-distance maps are generated offline and are used only for training-time supervision or the qualitative external-radial-map interventions. Camera controllability is measured by recovering camera motion from generated videos and comparing it to the requested trajectory after scale alignment for translation. We emphasize that all 340 clips in the PanShot test split are used for evaluation. In particular, we do not remove the high-motion tail of the distribution (e.g., the top 20% by camera-motion magnitude [ 3 ]), since large-trajectory clips are precisely the regime in which camera controllability is hardest to achieve and where curved-ray modeling is expected to matter most. We report representative metrics in Table 2 and the merged full metric suite in Table 5.
