1 Introduction 

The last decade has seen major developments in the capture, post-processing, encoding, transmission, and display of High Dynamic Range (HDR) images and videos [Miller et al . 2013]. HDR displays are now standard on consumer devices such as smartphones and laptops [Banterle et al . 2017]. These displays offer immersive experi-ences by approaching contrast ratios of real-world scenes and emit-ting luminance from 0.01 to 1000 cd/ 𝑚 2 (nits) [Reinhard 2020]. More-over, by leveraging developments in capture and post-production, the television and cinema industries already produce a large amount of content intended for HDR display [Quero et al. 2025]. Against this backdrop of rapid HDR adoption, the up-conversion of existing SDR video poses a major challenge: SDR videos have been produced for many decades; they often contain clipped highlight de-tails and crushed shadows; and have been encoded and compressed with SDR reproduction in mind. Naïve upconversion of such videos to HDR can amplify the appearance of such artifacts — clipped highlights appearing very bright, shadowed regions lacking detail, intensity quantization becoming much more apparent — resulting in a low-quality visual experience. Despite considerable efforts to overcome these issues using classical methods [Akyüz et al . 2007; Didyk et al . 2008], CNNs [Eilertsen et al . 2017; Endo et al . 2017; Marnerides et al . 2018; Santos et al . 2020], transformers [Wang et al .2025a], GANs [Wang et al . 2023], and image diffusion models [Wang et al . 2025b], convincing results for this problem remain elusive, particularly in the context of video. In this work, we revisit the problem of generating HDR video from casual SDR footage by harnessing the power of large video generation models. These models have been trained on billions of images and millions of videos and thus provide very strong priors about dynamic appearance. Repurposing such off-the-shelf mod-els for HDR upconversion is non-trivial because these models are trained almost exclusively on compressed, SDR-display-encoded content with 8-bit intensities. As a result, the autoencoders these models are built on are poorly suited to represent HDR video frames (Figure 3) and retraining them for HDR video generation is currently infeasible due to the lack of large-scale HDR video data [Froehlich et al. 2014; Wang et al. 2025b]. To overcome these challenges, we drew inspiration from one of the earliest photography techniques: exposure bracketing [Adams 1983; Debevec and Malik 1997; Le Gray 1856]. Our key insight is that the task of generating an exposure-bracketed image sequence is inherently compatible with the latent space of pre-trained SDR video models because each image in the sequence is an SDR image. Thus, instead of attempting to generate HDR videos directly, we finetune the video model to generate a short bracket sequence for every frame of an SDR video. The output of the model can be thought of as 𝑁 videos recorded simultaneously by 𝑁 synchronized cam-eras with different exposure settings. With these videos on hand, HDR upconversion reduces to the classical problem of converting a bracketed sequence of 𝑁 SDR images into a single HDR frame. We show that this two-stage approach is effective in inpainting under-exposed and over-exposed regions of the SDR source video and enables robust HDR video upconversion without specialized HDR capture protocols or large-scale HDR video training datasets. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows: 

• We present, to our knowledge, the first bracketed-generation approach for SDR-to-HDR conversion of casual videos. Our framework can also be seamlessly combined with off-the-shelf text or image-conditioned SDR video generation models to form a complete HDR generation pipeline. 

• We propose a Multi-Exposure Video Model (MEVM) that leverages large-scale SDR generative video pretraining to predict linear exposure-bracketed SDR videos, and a learn-able Video Merging Model (VMM) that merges generated exposure-bracketed SDR videos into HDR videos. 

• Extensive experiments demonstrate that our method achieves state-of-the-art perceptual quality for SDR-to-HDR video con-version and HDR video content generation. 

2 Related Work 

We organize prior work by the capture protocol it requires, then turn to the generative video models we build on, and finally to the most relevant SDR-to-HDR conversion literature. 

2.1 HDR Capture Techniques 

Most HDR pipelines obtain dynamic range through capture-time exposure coding —deliberate exposure variation across time, space, or both. The classical approach captures multiple LDR frames at different shutter times and merges them into scene-referred linear radiance [Debevec and Malik 1997; Mann and Picard 1995]. For dynamic scenes, a substantial line of work suppresses ghosting via patch matching and optical flow [Kalantari et al . 2017; Sen et al .2012], UNets [Wu et al . 2018], attention or transformer-based align-ment [Tel et al . 2023; Yan et al . 2019], lightweight architectures [Kong et al . 2024], intrinsic decompositions [Dille et al . 2024], and genera-tive merging that hallucinates occluded content [Anand et al . 2021; Niu et al . 2021]. Spatial coding instead multiplexes exposures across pixels in a single shot via neutral-density arrays [Nayar and Mit-sunaga 2000], coded shutters [Cho et al . 2014], generalized assorted pixels [Yasuma et al . 2010], learned [Nguyen et al . 2022] or jointly demosaicked [Qu et al . 2024; Xu et al . 2022] patterns, and optical randomization [Dai et al . 2025]; specialized rigs and dual-readout sensors generalize this idea spatio-temporally [Froehlich et al . 2014; Yue et al . 2023]. A distinct family obtains dynamic range without ex-posure coding: HDR+ [Hasinoff et al . 2016] and its successors [Chen and Hasinoff 2020; Manfred Ernst 2021] capture constant-exposure bursts and recover radiance through raw-domain denoising and tone mapping. Extending these ideas to video, alternating-exposure pipelines cycle the shutter between exposures and align neighbor-ing frames via flow or deformable attention [Chen et al . 2021a; Chung and Cho 2023; Kalantari et al . 2017; Xu et al . 2024], joint interpolation [Khan et al. 2022], or exposure completing [Cui et al. 2024]; a closely related line decodes HDR from naturally occurring auto-exposure variations using self-supervised learning [Banterle et al. 2024] or temporal propagation [Ye et al. 2024]. None of these methods applies to arbitrary, casually recorded SDR video, but the bracket-and-merge structure they pioneered directly informs our generation-time formulation. 3frame 1

2.2 Generative Video Models 

Large-scale generative video models [Brooks et al . 2024; Li et al .2024; Wan et al . 2025] model not only motion but implicitly as-pects of the image-formation pipeline—exposure, focus, and lens geometry. Generative Photography [Yuan et al . 2025] makes camera intrinsics an explicit controllable input, enabling applications such as post-capture refocusing [Tedla et al . 2025a], deblurring [Tedla et al . 2025b], relighting [Bharadwaj et al . 2025], and cinematic edit-ing [Sun et al . 2025]. Building on this line, we observe that Wan 2.2-I2V-5B [Wan et al . 2025] responds to exposure-related prompts such as “make brighter”/“make darker” without any fine-tuning (Figure 2). However, these prompt-driven exposure shifts are not metrically calibrated, not aligned across frames, and do not follow a linear exposure ladder—all properties required for HDR merging. Our fine-tuning scheme converts this implicit sensitivity into a calibrated, aligned linear exposure-bracket generator. 

2.3 Learning SDR-to-HDR Conversion 

Single-image methods. With only a single SDR image, recovery is severely ill-posed and clipped highlights and crushed shadows must be plausibly hallucinated. The literature splits into Inverse Tone Map-ping (ITM)—which targets display-referred HDR (e.g., 8-bit BT.709 to 10-bit BT.2020/PQ) via global tone operators [Akyüz et al . 2007; Didyk et al . 2008], CNN highlight reconstruction [Eilertsen et al .2017], multi-branch networks [Marnerides et al . 2018], mask guid-ance [Santos et al . 2020], transformers [Wang et al . 2025a], embedded MLPs [Liu et al . 2024], and gain-map representations [Canham et al .2025; Liao et al . 2025]—and HDR image inference , which predicts scene-referred linear radiance. The most relevant inference line, con-ceptually closest to ours, reformulates the task as generation-time multi-exposure synthesis followed by HDR merging: pseudo-bracket prediction by regression [Endo et al . 2017; Lee et al . 2018; Zhang et al . 2023] or GANs [Wang et al . 2023], and diffusion-based bracket consistency [Bemana et al . 2024] couples multiple SDR denoising original HDR frame

Video methods. The setting most relevant to ours—HDR recov-ery from arbitrary, casually recorded SDR video—has only very recently been addressed. Concurrent to our work, DiffHDR [Yu et al . 2026] (no public code) and LumiVid [Korem et al . 2026] both adapt video foundation models by re-encoding HDR into a single-channel-stretched space (Log-Gamma and LogC3, respectively) and performing radiance inpainting. We instead generate bracketed SDR video that is natively compatible with the video model’s latent space. As shown in Figure 3, alternative encoding spaces produce artifacts in the VAE round-trip, whereas our multi-exposure bracketing strat-egy yields higher-fidelity HDR reconstruction.

3.1.4 Learnable rotary positional embedding. Our input, consist-ing of multiple frames at different exposure levels, forms a set of tokens that must attend not only across space and time, but also across exposure. However, the pre-trained axial rotary positional embedding (RoPE) [Heo et al . 2024] within WanVideo [2025] only represents spatial and temporal indices, and therefore provides no explicit notion of exposure or cross-exposure correspondence. As a result, we find that the pre-trained RoPE is unable to encode this information effectively (see the ablation study in Section 5). To ad-dress this, we propose a learnable embedding that modulates the pre-trained RoPE, enabling it to encode both exposure level and cross-exposure interactions. RoPE [Heo et al . 2024] encodes spatio-temporal patch position by rotating query and key vectors in the attention block with a position-dependent angle 𝜃 derived from predefined frequencies (see Supplementary Section S3 for details). To maintain minimal changes to the pretrained model, we introduce a learnable exposure embedding as an offset to the RoPE rotation vector 𝜃 to further embed exposure and relative time information. Specifically, we let each embedding encode three components: the exposure index 𝑒 ,the binary CRF indicator 𝑐 , and the relative frame index 𝑟 . We set the exposure index equal to the frame’s exposure value, so frames with identical exposure share the same index. The CRF indicator is a binary value used to distinguish CRF-encoded input frames from linearly generated output frames that share the same exposure level. The relative index groups frames across exposures occurring at the same temporal position. 4 Merging Exposure-Bracketed Video 

After generating exposure brackets, we require a method to merge the generated exposure-bracketed video frames into HDR frames. We found that using classical merging methods [Debevec and Malik 1997] resulted in suboptimal performance (see Section 5.2.1), as the VAE introduces small value changes during frame encoding and decoding. Thus, we propose a learnable merging method using a simple and effective video merging model (VMM).

5 Evaluation 5.1 Training Details 

5.1.1 MEVM fine-tuning. We finetune MEVM’s flow-matching trans-former by using all available 17-frame sequences (11,336 sequences) from 26 HDR videos (avg. 453 frames per video) of the Stuttgart dataset [Froehlich et al . 2014]. We train with a batch size of 64 and a learning rate of 0.00003. Training includes two stages: the first 15 epochs use a lower resolution of 512 ×768 , and the final 15 epochs use the full resolution of 704 ×1280 . Each training example consists of a target linear multi-exposure sequence and a corresponding SDR input video, both derived from the same HDR source. 

Sampling linear multi-exposure videos. From each HDR training video, we sample multi-exposure sequences in the linear domain with a fixed 4 EV spacing between exposures. The middle (reference) exposure is drawn uniformly at random from a per-scene range 

[𝑒 min , 𝑒 max ], where 𝑒 min is the exposure at which 10% of pixels are clipped to black (0) and 𝑒 max is the exposure at which 30% of pixels are clipped to white (1). 

Sampling SDR input videos. The SDR input to the model is gener-ated from the reference exposure video by applying sensor noise, a random CRF, clipping, and quantization. Details of this procedure are given in Supplementary Section 5.1. 

5.1.2 VMM training. The VMM is trained for 5 epochs on the same Stuttgart training split with the same multi-exposure sampling scheme as the MEVM. Because the VMM assumes clean, linearized (HDR) input video, noise and CRF simulation are not applied during VMM training. 

5.2 SDR-to-HDR Video Reconstruction 

We evaluate the performance of our method on 17-frame videos derived from 9 held-out test scenes in Stuttgart [Froehlich et al .2014] and 10 scenes from UBC [Azimi et al. 2014]. 

Input generation. From the ground-truth HDR frames, we gen-erate SDR input videos under two exposure conditions: over- and under-exposure. For over-exposure, a single fixed exposure is de-rived from the first frame, with its mean luminance set to 0.70, and this exposure is held constant across all frames. For under-exposure, the same per-scene fixed scheme is used, but targeting a mean lu-minance of 0.01 on the first frame. Then, the scaled linear values are clipped to [0, 1], gamma-encoded with 𝛾 = 2.2, and quantized to 8-bit SDR frames. We additionally explore a third auto-exposed input in Supplementary S2. 

Baselines. We compared our method against the recent deep-learning-based HDR inverse tone mapping operators: HDRCNN [Eilertsen et al . 2019], SingleHDR [Liu et al . 2020], LEDiff [Wang et al . 2025b], X2HDR [Wu et al . 2026], and LumiVid [Korem et al .2026]. HDRCNN and LumiVid are video-based methods that explic-itly enforce temporal coherence, whereas the remaining methods are applied independently to each frame. For SingleHDR, LEDiff, X2HDR, and LumiVid, we use the official pre-trained weights. HDR-CNN was retrained following the original protocol [Eilertsen et al .2019], excluding frames from Stuttgart and UBC datasets to prevent data leakage between training and evaluation. For methods without an explicit linearization stage (HDRCNN and X2HDR), we apply the SingleHDR linearization network [Liu et al . 2020] to estimate and invert the CRF. For a given video, the CRF is estimated from the most well-exposed frame selected by the criterion of Mertens [2007]. Additional quantitative comparisons with HDRTV [Chen et al .2021b], DVITMO [Xu et al . 2019], and ZHDRV [Banterle et al . 2024] are provided in Supplementary Section S2.1. 

Metrics. We follow the calibration protocol of Hanji [2022] and IntrinsicHDR [Dille et al . 2024]. Ground-truth frames are prepro-cessed following Liu et al. [2020] to map values into [1, 1000 ]. Pre-dictions are then affine-aligned to the calibrated ground truth via least-squares fitting on pixels whose ground-truth luminance lies between the 10th and 90th percentiles, followed by the Hanji et al. [2022] CRF correction. Following similar work [Korem et al .2026; Wu et al . 2026], we report CVVDP [Mantiuk et al . 2024] as our primary quality metric, computed on the CRF-corrected sequences with the following display configuration: 30-inch 4K HDR, BT.709-linear, 𝐿 peak = 1000 cd/m 2, 10 6:1 contrast, 10 lux ambient. We also compute PU-PSNR after the CRF-corrected prediction and ground truth are PU21-encoded [Azimi et al . 2021]. PU-PIQE is computed on the affine-aligned (pre-CRF) prediction as a no-reference quality 7

estimate. Following LEDiff [2025b], we apply the Drago [2003] and Mantiuk [2008] tonemappers directly to the affine-aligned predic-tion and ground truth (no CRF correction) and compute FID [Heusel et al. 2017] on the resulting SDR frames (D-FID and M-FID). 

Discussion. Our method achieves the highest CVVDP scores across nearly all exposure conditions and both datasets (Table 1), while maintaining competitive D-FID and M-FID scores, indicating that our outputs are both perceptually faithful to the ground truth and visually realistic. We outperform all single-image baselines, con-firming the benefit of generating temporally coherent exposure brackets. Compared to the video baseline LumiVid, our method con-sistently achieves higher CVVDP; as shown in Figure 5, LumiVid produces prominent high-frequency artifacts (see Supp. Fig. 2) due to its encoding strategy, which degrades perceptual quality. While our method does not achieve the highest PU-PSNR, we attribute this to the fact that large portions of the output are inpainted, and our generative approach deliberately trades distortion for perceptual plausibility [Cohen et al . 2023]. Please see Supplementary Section S2 for additional results, comparisons, and analysis. 

User study. We conducted a perceptual quality study on an HDR monitor following the ITU-T BT.500-13 single stimulus continuous quality evaluation (SSCQE) protocol [2012]. Sixteen observers rated video quality, on a 5-point impairment scale, of outputs from our method and from HDRCNN [Eilertsen et al . 2019], LEDiff [Wang et al . 2025b], and LumiVid [Korem et al . 2026]. We selected 5 scenes from the Stuttgart dataset [Froehlich et al . 2014] and 5 from the UBC dataset [Azimi et al . 2014], evaluating outputs from both over-exposed and under-exposed input conditions. Each participant rated 80 videos (4 methods × 10 scenes × 2 conditions) in random-ized order. Additional details are in Supplementary Section S5. Ta-ble 2 reports mean SSCQE scores; two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests [2007] (paired on observer × scene) find our method scores higher than every baseline under both conditions ( 𝑝 < 0.005 ). 

5.2.1 Ablation. In Supplementary Table 2, we conduct an ablation of our exposure-aware RoPE embedding, the use of VMM instead of Debevec [1997] merging, and the use of an L1 flow-matching loss instead of the standard L2 loss. We find each of these components is beneficial for increased temporal coherence and perceptual quality. 

5.3 Towards Fully Generative HDR Video 

Our pipeline can be chained with an off-the-shelf SDR video model to form a fully generative HDR video pipeline. We prompt a text-to-video model [Wan et al . 2025] with exposure-related keywords such as “very bright”, “sunny day”, “very dark”, and “no lights” to produce an input SDR video that can be lifted to HDR with our model. Figure 5(b) and Figure 6(a, top-left) show examples of this application. Across these qualitatively different generative scenarios, our method produces physically plausible dynamic-range expansion in both highlight and shadow regions while preserving the temporal coherence of the upstream generative video.

6 Conclusion 

We have shown that a reframing of HDR generation—generating bracketed video at multiple exposures—provides a robust path for converting SDR video to HDR. Remarkably, our model, fine-tuned on fewer than 30 HDR videos , synthesizes plausible HDR content even on challenging in-the-wild footage, demonstrating the strength of the video model’s prior for exposure-conditioned generation. Our method maintains high performance across benchmarks, generalizes to a wide variety of in-the-wild scenes, including iconic cinema footage, and can be paired with standard SDR video generation models to create a full generative HDR pipeline. 

Limitations and future work. We now highlight a few limitations of our approach and promising directions for future work. Our design choice to generate exposure brackets comes at the cost of re-duced video length. Additionally, our fixed three-bracket exposure strategy cannot always recover the full dynamic range of high-contrast scenes, leaving some outputs with residual clipping after generation. We explored diffusion forcing [Chen et al . 2025] to address both these issues through temporal and exposure autore-gressive generation, but observed compounding artifacts when con-ditioning on model-generated content—a problem of active interest in the community [Po et al . 2025; Song et al . 2025]. Additionally, we observed latent compression produces pixel-level misalignment 8

between brackets, which we expect to improve with advances in video VAEs [Wan et al . 2025] or pixel-space models [Lei et al . 2026]. We visualize and discuss these cases in Supplementary Section S2.4. Our work belongs to a growing class of methods that remap classical computational photography problems as tasks for large video generation models, exploiting the strong camera-physics pri-ors these models implicitly learn from internet-scale data [Sun et al .2025; Yuan et al . 2025]. Our findings open the door to end-to-end system design, in which camera auto-exposure algorithms could be designed knowing that a strong generative prior will reconstruct HDR in post-processing. With HDR displays now standard on con-sumer devices, the demand for HDR-native generative models is a present need—and our results suggest that generation-time brack-eting offers a practical path forward, without requiring large-scale HDR training data. 9LumiVid HDRCNN input (video frame 1) LEDiff proposed a)

S2.3 Additional Comparisons 

Figure 2 provides additional qualitative comparisons against Lu-miVid [Korem et al . 2026], the most closely related baseline. The comparisons highlight several conditions where the two methods differ in output quality. We believe a potential cause of these dif-ferences is that our method generates exposure-bracketed SDR se-quences that reside natively within the pre-trained latent space of the underlying video model, whereas LumiVid encodes HDR in LogC3 — a space that is not inherently compatible with the SDR model it fine-tunes. 

High-frequency motion (a). In scenes with fast-moving, high-frequency patterns, such as waterfalls, our method preserves spatial structure, whereas LumiVid exhibits irregular patterns in its output. We hypothesize that this difference arises from our approach oper-ating natively in SDR space, whereas LumiVid must generalize its LogC3 HDR encoding to rapidly changing texture statistics. 

Scene fidelity (b). Our method reconstructs scene content faith-fully under challenging conditions. In contrast, LumiVid can produce outputs that diverge significantly from the input scene, suggesting that its fine-tuned diffusion model occasionally decouples from the conditioning signal. 

Temporal consistency (c). In some scenes, where our method pro-duces consistent output, LumiVid outputs videos with perceptible color shifts. 

Low-light performance (d). In dark regions, our method recovers shadow detail from the generated high-exposure bracket. LumiVid introduces prominent pink chrominance artifacts in the same con-ditions, consistent with unreliable color estimation in low-signal regimes. 

Scene-linear output (e). Our pipeline learns to output linear SDR frames before merging brackets into HDR. In some scenes, Lu-miVid’s output retains residual tone-mapping, indicating the CRF was not fully inverted and the output is not truly scene-linear. That said, our design decisions have other limitations, which we discuss in Section S2.4.

S5 User study details 

We conducted a user study following the ITU-T BT.500-13 single stimulus continuous quality evaluation (SSCQE) protocol [2012]. Sixteen observers rated video quality on a 5-point impairment scale (1 = Bad, 5 = Excellent), preceded by two practice trials. Videos were displayed on a Macbook built-in liquid retina XDR display at 1,600 cd /m2 peak brightness via a custom WebGPU shader equipped with exposure and tone mapping controls. Tone mapping was im-plemented via a Naka-Rushton equation [Banić and Lončarić 2018] applied to exposure-adjusted scene-linear luminance. Tone mapped luminance is then applied to scene linear RGB channels via the color correction method of Mantiuk et al. [Mantiuk et al . 2009], leaving the saturation parameter open for observers to adjust. Via the ex-perimental interface shown in Figure 3, observers could modulate controls for exposure, contrast and pivot, or the image intensity where the contrast parameter is most impactful. This enabled users to display the image at their desired exposure and contrast and 5

Fig. 3. User Study Interface. Tone mapping and rating sliders are shown at an enlarged size. 

evaluate the image as editing source material. Videos were played on loop so that observers had as much time as they liked to adjust and assess them.
