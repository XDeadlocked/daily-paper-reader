## ABSTRACT 

Instruction following is central to language-conditioned robot policies: language should determine what to do when the same scene permits multiple valid ac-tions. Yet successful execution alone cannot establish whether a policy follows the instruction or infers the task from the scene. We study this ambiguity through 

scene-preserving instruction interventions , using valid target substitutions, arbi-trary nouns, and unrelated sentences while holding the scene fixed. We evaluate vision-language-action (VLA) policies and world-action models (WAMs) in sim-ulation and in real-world experiments. Our analysis addresses three questions: (a) 

Does task success imply instruction following? When instructions request a dif-ferent visible object, all evaluated policies predominantly approach and pick up the incorrect original target associated with the scene. (b) Is this failure caused by language insensitivity? Instruction perturbations affect task performance. A layerwise action lens shows intermediate action predictions respond to these per-turbations. Linear probes accurately recover instructed targets, indicating modi-fied instructions are encoded despite rarely determining target selection. (c) Why does encoded language fail to control action? Attention analysis indicates weak instruction-token contributions to action generation. Target-token attention can re-main focused on the original object, revealing a mismatch between target encod-ing and visual grounding. UMAP and shared non-negative matrix factorization show target information remains accessible within representations increasingly organized by scene identity. Our findings expose a grounding gap concealed by nominal success and provide a diagnostic framework. They further establish a concrete criterion for progress: policies should reliably follow valid changes in user intent, even when they conflict with scene-favored behavior. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Language-conditioned robot policies promise a natural interface for specifying what a robot should do in an environment. Recent VLAs (Intelligence et al., 2025; Bjorck et al., 2025) and WAMs (Yuan et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026b) demonstrate strong performance across manipulation tasks, embodi-ments, and visual environments, commonly evaluated by task success under a given instruction and environment. However, task success alone does not establish that the instruction determined the behavior. In many robot datasets, a scene is repeatedly paired with one target object or task, so the scene alone may suffice to infer the demonstrated behavior without resolving the requested behav-ior. A policy can consequently achieve high success by exploiting scene–task correlations while its ability to follow language remains unclear. Instruction following requires evidence that changing a valid instruction in a fixed scene redirects behavior toward the newly specified target. Evaluations using linguistic perturbations and modality ablations reveal brittleness and task-dependent language sensitivity in VLA policies (Fei et al., 2025;

More directly, LIBERO-CF and LangGap expose instruction-following failures under alternative feasible instructions in fixed scenes (Fang et al., 2026; Hou & Zhao, 2026), while InstructMove constructs scenes where lan-guage must distinguish among multiple feasible actions (Zhao et al., 2026). Mechanistic analyses further find that prompt information can remain decodable despite limited behavioral use, with ac-tion generation relying strongly on visual inputs (Grant et al., 2026). Building on these findings, we use scene-preserving instruction interventions to investigate how target-selection failures relate to the encoding, visual grounding, and contribution of instruction information to action generation, linking behavioral failures to action-level responses and internal representations across VLA and world-action models. 

Part I: Does nominal task success imply instruction following? We revisit this question across VLA and WAM policies, with additional real-world experiments. When a valid instruction requests another object in the same scene, the policies predominantly approach and manipulate the original target. We refer to this directional preference as a scene-conditioned behavioral prior . It reveals a failure that standard success rates cannot expose: a policy may successfully reproduce the expected behavior for a familiar scene without allowing language to determine which behavior it executes. 

Part II: Is target-conflict failure caused by language insensitivity? This failure cannot be at-tributed simply to absent linguistic influence. Controlled instruction perturbations produce system-atic, task-dependent changes in policy performance. A layerwise action lens shows that intermediate action predictions respond differently to target substitutions and random sentences, evidencing lan-guage sensitivity in action generation. Linear probes also recover target and scene identities from fine-tuned representations with high accuracy, so target information remains available despite incor-rect object selection. Policies thus respond to language at behavioral and representational levels, yet the failure persists even when the modified instruction affects action predictions and its target remains encoded—motivating a closer look at how this information contributes to action selection. 

Part III: If target information is encoded, why does it fail to control action? Attention contribu-tion analysis shows instruction tokens contribute little to action generation relative to visual inputs, and target-token attention can stay fixed on the original object even after the instruction names a different target. UMAP reveals a shift from object-based to scene-based clustering after fine-tuning; shared non-negative matrix factorization likewise identifies transitions from object-dominated to scene-dominated components in both π0.5 and GR00T N1.7. High target decodability thus coexists with limited instruction contribution, misaligned visual attention, and increased scene dependence, characterizing why encoded language does not reliably guide target selection. We describe this dissociation as a representation-to-control grounding gap : linguistic information can be represented within a policy without reliably governing action selection. Our analyses dis-tinguish sensitivity to language, accessibility of target information, and behavioral adherence to the instruction. These target-selection failures also extend to the evaluated world-action models, show-ing that visual prediction objectives alone do not ensure instruction following. Our findings motivate a stricter grounding criterion: a valid change in instruction should reliably select a different behavior within the same scene. We hope this work encourages evaluations that test such control directly and provides a diagnostic basis for investigating where encoded instructions fail to guide action. Ulti-mately, progress should be measured by how reliably robots act on the user’s current intent, even when it conflicts with familiar scene-task associations. 2Preprint 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Language-conditioned robot policies. Vision-language-action (VLA) models map visual obser-vations and language instructions to robot actions. Recent models such as π0.5 couple a pretrained vision-language backbone with a dedicated action expert that generates continuous action chunks through flow matching (Intelligence et al., 2025). GR00T N1 adopts a dual-system architecture in which a vision-language module interprets visual observations and language instructions, followed by a diffusion transformer that generates continuous motor actions (Bjorck et al., 2025). World action models (WAMs) instead incorporate explicit visual-dynamics modeling into policy learning. LingBot-VA adopts an autoregressive diffusion framework that jointly learns future-frame predic-tion and policy execution, using a shared latent space and a Mixture-of-Transformers architecture to integrate visual and action tokens (Li et al., 2026b). Fast-WAM retains video co-training dur-ing training but removes explicit future prediction at test time, directly producing actions from the learned world representations (Yuan et al., 2026). These complementary architectures motivate our evaluation of π0.5, GR00T N1.7, LingBot-VA, and FastWAM, allowing us to examine whether instruction-grounding failures persist across both VLA and world-action modeling paradigms. 

Evaluating and improving instruction following. Recent studies investigate if task success reli-ably reflects language grounding. LIBERO-PRO reports persistent behaviors under corrupted in-structions (Zhou et al., 2025b), while LIBERO-Plus evaluates robustness to linguistic, visual, and physical perturbations (Fei et al., 2025). Multilingual evaluation further reveals step-dependent language sensitivity (Dong et al., 2026). To test semantic control more directly, LIBERO-CF and LangGap vary instructions within existing scene layouts (Fang et al., 2026; Hou & Zhao, 2026), while InstructMove introduces semantic distractors that require language-dependent action selec-tion (Zhao et al., 2026). Complementary methods strengthen language conditioning through coun-terfactual language–action augmentation in navigation (Glossop et al., 2026), Bayesian training ob-jectives (Lian et al., 2026), counterfactual action guidance (Fang et al., 2026), and instruction aug-mentation coupled with guidance against a vision-only prior (Zhan et al., 2026). Building on these studies, we connect behavior under scene-preserving instruction interventions to internal policy rep-resentations. Target substitutions test semantic redirection, whereas arbitrary nouns and random sentences diagnose language dependence without necessarily specifying executable alternatives. 

Language representations and behavioral influence. Probing studies distinguish decodable in-formation from its use by a model, emphasizing the need for controls when interpreting probe accuracy (Belinkov, 2022; Hewitt & Liang, 2019). In robotics, Lu et al. (2025) decode symbolic object and action states from OpenVLA for monitoring within the cognitive architecture. Using counterfactual prompts, activation injection, sparse autoencoders, and probes, Grant et al. (2026) identify distinct VLM and action-expert roles and report decodable prompt categories despite weak behavioral sensitivity in π0.5. VLA-Trace combines representation analysis, attention knockouts, and behavioral interventions to identify model-dependent control pathways and limitations in fine-grained semantic following (Shi et al., 2026). We build on these findings by pairing instruction interventions with layer-wise analyses of target and scene information across VLA and WAM poli-cies, examining how instructed-target representations relate to action selection when language and scene-associated behavior conflict.

> Guiding question Q1. When the instruction requests another valid target in the same scene, does the policy follow the current instruction or retain the original target?

Successful execution cannot distinguish instruction following from task inference when the scene and instruction suggest the same behavior. Following prior evaluations of alternative feasible in-structions in fixed scenes (Fang et al., 2026; Hou & Zhao, 2026), we revisit this ambiguity through controlled target replacement. ## 6 WHY DOES ENCODED LANGUAGE FAIL TO CONTROL ACTION ?

Guiding question Q3. If the current target remains encoded, how can visual attention and action gener-ation remain misaligned with the instruction, and what changes in representational organization accom-pany this failure?

We now examine how this information participates in action generation and relates to visual grounding. We first quantify the relative contribution of instruction tokens to action generation. This helps reconcile high probe accuracy with instruction-following failures and is consis-tent with the preference for the original target, without establishing a causal link. 

Answer to Q3. Target information remains encoded, but its participation in action generation and alignment with the instructed object are limited. Instruction tokens account for a relatively small share of the measured attention contribution to action generation, target-token visual attention can remain focused on the original object, and fine-tuning shifts representational organization toward scene identity. These findings characterize how preserved target information coexists with unreliable instruction following, without establishing a causal mechanism for the failure.

## 7 CONCLUSION 

We investigate instruction grounding through a progression from behavioral failures to language sensitivity, target encoding, and participation in action generation. Across the evaluated policies, changed instructions affect computation and preserve recoverable target information, yet often fail to redirect target selection. The internal analyses reveal weak instruction contributions, persistent attention to the original object, and a shift toward scene-dependent representational organization. This evidence places the use of encoded language at the center of the grounding problem. It mo-tivates evaluation and training that explicitly require instructions to select among competing valid behaviors within the same scene.

## A GENERAL EXPERIMENTAL SETTINGS 

A.1 MODEL SOURCES AND IMPLEMENTATIONS 

We evaluate four language-conditioned robot policies two model families: two vision-language-action (VLA) policies, π0.5 (Intelligence et al., 2025) and GR00T N1.7 (Bjorck et al., 2025), and two world-action models (WAMs), FastWAM (Yuan et al., 2026) and LingBot-VA (Li et al., 2026b). All behavioral evaluations are conducted in closed loop with frozen policy parameters. We retain the observation preprocessing, action representation, and inference interface of each public imple-mentation rather than converting all models into a shared policy architecture. 

Source repositories. The model implementations are based on the following public repositories: • π0.5: Physical Intelligence OpenPI (GitHub). • GR00T N1.7: NVIDIA Isaac-GR00T (GitHub). • FastWAM (GitHub). • LingBot-VA (GitHub). • XPolicyLab (GitHub), used for our RoboTwin training pipeline for π0.5 and GR00T N1.7. A.2 LIBERO B ENCHMARK 

LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023) is a language-conditioned manipulation benchmark designed to study knowledge transfer across manipulation tasks. We evaluate the four commonly used ten-task suites: LIBERO-S PATIAL , LIBERO-O BJECT , LIBERO-G OAL , and LIBERO-L ONG .The suites emphasize different forms of generalization. SPATIAL varies spatial relations while largely preserving object semantics; O BJECT varies the manipulated objects; G OAL varies task 17 Preprint goals; and L ONG contains longer-horizon, multi-stage manipulation tasks. Each task is associated with a natural-language instruction and a benchmark-defined success predicate. The LIBERO simulator uses a Franka Panda robot and provides RGB observations from an exter-nal agent-view camera and an eye-in-hand wrist camera together with robot proprioception. The low-level control action is seven-dimensional, consisting of end-effector translation, end-effector rotation, and gripper control. We use the benchmark-provided initial states and native task-success predicates. For all instruction-intervention experiments, the initial environment state and robot state are held fixed across instruction conditions. Thus, the O RIGINAL , N OUN REPLACEMENT , and R ANDOM 

SENTENCE conditions differ only in the language input. A.3 LIBERO E VALUATION PROTOCOLS 

Because the four policies expose different action-chunking and inference interfaces, we retain the evaluation protocol associated with each public implementation. For each model, we use a check-point fine-tuned on the full LIBERO benchmark. When such a checkpoint is not publicly available, we fine-tune the model ourselves following the official training guidelines and recommended set-tings. Importantly, the evaluation protocol for a given model is kept identical across all instruction conditions. A.4 ROBO TWIN 2.0 B ENCHMARK 

RoboTwin 2.0 (Chen et al., 2025) is a simulation benchmark for generalizable bimanual robotic manipulation. We evaluate all four policies on the 50-task benchmark using the ALOHA-AgileX dual-arm embodiment. We consider two evaluation conditions. The C LEAN condition uses the nominal task configura-tion, whereas the R ANDOMIZED condition introduces benchmark-defined domain randomization. RoboTwin 2.0 randomizes multiple visual and physical factors, including background and tabletop textures, scene clutter, illumination, tabletop height, and camera viewpoint. These variations test whether a policy trained on demonstrated manipulation behaviors remains robust to changes in the surrounding scene. The RoboTwin environment provides observations from a global head camera and two wrist cameras together with the bimanual robot state. Policies predict actions for the ALOHA-AgileX embodiment through their corresponding RoboTwin policy adapters. A.5 ROBO TWIN TRAINING AND EVALUATION PROTOCOL 

For π0.5 and GR00T N1.7, the RoboTwin checkpoints used in this paper are trained by us following the default training pipelines provided by XPolicyLab. FastWAM and LingBot-VA are evaluated through their corresponding public RoboTwin implementations. RoboTwin 2.0 uses task-specific evaluation horizons rather than a single global maximum episode length. In evaluation mode, each task reads its control-step limit from the benchmark configuration. We retain these benchmark-defined limits. Following the benchmark evaluation protocol, each task is evaluated with 100 rollouts. An episode terminates when either the task-specific success condition is satisfied or its corresponding control-step limit is reached. The benchmark success predicate is used directly without introducing an additional task-completion heuristic. Within each model, the same simulator seeds, task configurations, episode limits, observation pre-processing, and action-execution parameters are used for the O RIGINAL , N OUN REPLACEMENT ,and R ANDOM SENTENCE conditions. Only the language instruction is modified. A.6 MATCHED EVALUATION UNDER INSTRUCTION INTERVENTIONS

## H DETAILED LITERATURE REVIEW 

Recent advances in robot learning have expanded the ways perception is translated into action. This section organizes the literature into four complementary paradigms. Vision–Action (VA) mod-els learn direct mappings from visual observations to motor commands, with an emphasis on pre-cise sensorimotor control. Vision–Language–Action (VLA) models condition behavior on natural-language instructions, enabling a single policy to address a broader range of tasks. World–Action Models (WAMs) couple action generation with predictions of how the environment will evolve, providing a basis for anticipating action outcomes. H.1 VISION –A CTION MODELS .Vision–action (VA) models learn visuomotor policies that map visual observations and robot states to actions without requiring language instructions. Their development has centered on three related challenges: representing spatial information, generating coherent action sequences, and maintaining robust control during execution. Diffusion-based policies have become a prominent approach to modeling multimodal action distri-butions. Diffusion Policy (Chi et al., 2025) established action-sequence denoising as an effective framework for visuomotor control. Subsequent work has strengthened its spatial representations: DP3 (Ze et al., 2024) conditions action generation on 3D point clouds, while iDP3 (Ze et al., 2025) extends this direction to humanoid manipulation learned from human demonstrations. Other meth-ods refine how visual information enters the policy. H 3DP (Lu et al., 2026) introduces hierarchies across observations, visual features, and action generation; 3D Diffuser Actor (Ke et al., 2024) uses tokenized 3D scene representations; and Render and Diffuse (R&D) (Vosylius et al., 2024) aligns observations and candidate actions by rendering robot configurations in image space. Mamba Pol-icy (Cao et al., 2025b) explores a more efficient sequence-modeling backbone, whereas MCDP (Cao et al., 2025a) combines diffusion policies trained on different visual modalities at inference time. GCP (Cao et al., 2026) further studies distribution-level policy composition across diffusion- and flow-based policies. A second line of work addresses the structure and reliability of generated actions. DexDif-fuser (Weng et al., 2024) targets dexterous grasp generation, while ALOHA Unleashed (Zhao et al., 2024) applies diffusion-based action generation to complex bimanual manipulation. ChainedDif-fuser (Xian et al., 2023) connects end-effector keyposes to executable trajectories, and HDP (Ma et al., 2024) incorporates kinematic structure into policy learning. For generalization across objects and scenes, S 2-Diffusion (Yang et al., 2025) investigates the transfer from instance-level demon-strations to category-level skills, and C3DM (Saxena et al., 2023) uses constrained visual context to reduce the influence of distractors. Crossway Diffusion (Li et al., 2024) improves learned repre-sentations through an auxiliary self-supervised objective, while Equivariant Diffusion Policy (Wang et al., 2024) builds geometric symmetry into action generation. Temporal consistency and responsiveness have motivated complementary advances. Streaming Dif-fusion Policy (Høeg et al., 2024) addresses continuous action generation during execution, and Bidi-rectional Decoding (BID) (Liu et al., 2025d) improves action-chunk execution through guided test-time sampling. Beyond diffusion, ACT (Zhao et al., 2023) predicts action chunks with a transformer-based policy, while Chunking Causal Transformer (Zhang et al., 2025) models dependencies across chunks. Act3D (Gervet et al., 2023) and Spatial Policy (Liu et al., 2025c) emphasize spatially grounded action prediction. Flow matching provides another route to generative control: Mani-Flow (Yan et al., 2025) and VITA (Gao et al., 2026a) develop flow-based visuomotor policies, while Flow Matching Policy Gradients (McAllister et al., 2026) connects flow-based policies with rein-forcement learning. Finally, DynaGuide (Du & Song, 2026) uses feedback from a dynamics model 27 Preprint to steer a diffusion policy at inference time, and Latent Policy Barrier (Sun & Song, 2026) uses learned latent boundaries to identify and respond to states outside the expert distribution. H.2 VISION -L ANGUAGE -A CTION MODELS (VLA) Vision-language-action (VLA) models extend visuomotor control by conditioning robot behavior on natural-language instructions. Their central challenge is to connect semantic information—such as the requested object, goal, or task—to actions that are executable in the observed scene. Exist-ing approaches differ in where this connection is made: some learn language-conditioned policies directly, while others adapt pretrained vision-language models for action prediction or use language models to guide a separate control policy. Early language-conditioned approaches explored several ways to ground goals in behavior. MDT (Reuss et al., 2024) learns a diffusion policy from language and visual goal specifications, while PlayFusion (Chen et al., 2023) learns skills from language-annotated play data. StructDif-fusion (Liu et al., 2022) uses language and object-centric 3D observations to generate physically plausible rearrangement goals. Rather than changing the policy architecture, ROSIE (Yu et al., 2023) uses text-guided image generation to augment robot training data. These works establish complementary routes from linguistic goals to visuomotor behavior. Scaling beyond individual tasks and platforms has motivated training on diverse robot data. RT-1 (Brohan et al., 2023) demonstrated large-scale instruction-conditioned robot learning, while RT-2 (Brohan et al., 2023) transferred knowledge from web-trained vision-language models into robotic action prediction. The Open X-Embodiment effort and RT-X models (O’Neill et al., 2024) extended this direction across datasets and embodiments; Octo (Team et al., 2024) likewise trains a generalist policy on heterogeneous robot demonstrations. Other methods address the scarcity or incompatibil-ity of action data. LAPA (Ye et al., 2025) learns latent action representations from videos without requiring ground-truth robot actions during pretraining. RDT-1B (Liu et al., 2025b) uses a diffusion transformer and a unified action representation for bimanual manipulation, while RDT2 (Liu et al., 2026) investigates cross-embodiment generalization through larger-scale UMI data. TinyVLA (Wen et al., 2025a) instead emphasizes a compact vision-language backbone and a diffusion action de-coder for efficient adaptation. A major architectural question is how to preserve semantic reasoning while generating precise continuous actions. DiffusionVLA (Wen et al., 2025b) and HybridVLA (Liu et al., 2025a) com-bine autoregressive modeling with diffusion-based action generation. The π0 family (Black et al., 2024; Intelligence et al., 2025; 2026) pairs a pretrained vision-language backbone with a flow-based action expert, with successive models extending its generalization and conditioning capabilities. ChatVLA (Zhou et al., 2025c) addresses the interaction between multimodal understanding and robot control through phased training and an expert-based architecture. Together, these models illustrate different ways to connect representations learned from vision and language to action gen-eration. Spatial reasoning and longer-horizon planning form a related direction. 3D-VLA (Zhen et al., 2024) connects 3D perception and language reasoning with generative predictions of future scenes, provid-ing a bridge to world-action modeling. SpatialBot (Cai et al., 2025) strengthens depth-aware spatial understanding in vision-language models. At the planning level, HiP (Ajay et al., 2023) composes models specialized for language, visual prediction, and control, while Plan Diffuser (Sharan et al., 2024) grounds language-model plans through diffusion-based generation. ReKep (Huang et al., 2024) expresses tasks as spatial and temporal keypoint constraints, whereas VoxPoser (Huang et al., 2023) converts language-guided affordances into 3D value maps. These planning systems connect semantic goals to robot execution and lead into the agent-based approaches discussed later. H.3 WORLD -A CTION MODELS (WAM) World-action models (WAMs) connect robot control with predictions of how the environment may change. This predictive signal can play different roles: a model may generate a visual plan before choosing actions, learn future observations and actions jointly, or use future prediction only as su-pervision during training. The distinction matters because forecasting a useful future representation does not necessarily require rendering future video at deployment. UniPi (Du et al., 2023) provides 28 Preprint an early example of planning through text-conditioned video generation, treating predicted visual trajectories as an intermediate representation for decision-making. One approach separates visual prediction from action execution. Gen2Act (Bharadhwaj et al., 2024) generates videos of human behavior in novel scenes and uses them to guide robot manipulation. Vidar (Feng et al., 2025a) combines video diffusion pretraining with a masked inverse dynamics model that recovers actions, while CoT-VLA (Zhao et al., 2025) autoregressively predicts future images as visual goals before generating action sequences. Act2Goal (Zhou et al., 2025a) connects a goal-conditioned visual world model to control at multiple temporal scales. Related work uses video models to expand training data or support visual planning: DreamGen (Jang et al., 2025) produces synthetic robot trajectories from generated videos, and RoboDreamer (Zhou et al., 2024) factorizes video generation to compose visual plans from learned elements. A second approach learns visual dynamics and actions within a shared model. GR-1 (Wu et al., 2024) predicts future images alongside robot actions, and GR-2 (Cheang et al., 2024) extends video pretraining to web-scale data before adapting to robot trajectories. WorldVLA (Cen et al., 2025b) and RynnVLA-002 (Cen et al., 2025a) further unify action prediction with world modeling. LingBot-VA (Li et al., 2026b) learns frame prediction and policy execution within an autoregressive diffusion framework. Unified World Models (UWM) (Zhu et al., 2025) couple video and action diffusion in a model that can be used for policy learning, visual prediction, and dynamics model-ing. DreamVLA (Zhang et al., 2026) instead predicts compact world knowledge to support inverse dynamics and action generation. Large video models offer another source of predictive representations. Cosmos Policy (Kim et al., 2026) adapts a pretrained video foundation model to visuomotor control through post-training, while DreamZero (Ye et al., 2026b) builds a joint video-action model on a pretrained video diffusion back-bone. Genie Envisioner (Liao et al., 2026) brings policy learning, visual generation, and evaluation into a video-generative framework. DreamDojo (Gao et al., 2026b) pretrains an action-controllable world model on large-scale egocentric human video before adapting it to robot data. At the level of model design, DUST (Won et al., 2025) uses separate but interacting diffusion streams for vi-sual futures and actions. Motus (Bi et al., 2026) and MotuBrain (Team et al., 2026) use multiple transformer streams to model understanding, video generation, and action within shared systems. LDA-1B (Lyu et al., 2026) scales joint learning of dynamics, policy, and visual forecasting across heterogeneous embodied data. Explicit future-image generation, however, adds computational cost and may place demands on a model that differ from those of action prediction. FLARE (Zheng et al., 2025) introduces future-latent prediction as an implicit world-modeling objective for policy learning. Fast-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026) retains video co-training but omits future generation at test time, allowing a direct comparison between the benefits of predictive training and inference-time imagination. GigaWorld-Policy (Ye et al., 2026a) likewise centers action prediction while using visual dynamics to inform training. Across these methods, the central design choice is not simply whether to predict the future, but what future information to learn, how to connect it to actions, and whether it must be generated during execution .H.4 AGENTIC ROBOTICS 

Agentic robotics studies how a robot can pursue a task through repeated observation, reasoning, ac-tion, and feedback. In these systems, a multimodal model may decompose a goal, select executable skills, inspect their outcomes, and revise its plan as the scene changes. The agent therefore coor-dinates perception and control over an entire task, rather than producing a single action from the current observation. Recent work has made this coordination more explicit. Gemini Robotics 1.5 (Team et al., 2025b;a) combines embodied reasoning with a multi-embodiment action model. Reflective Planning (Feng et al., 2025b) uses imagined future states to reconsider skill choices, while RoboAgent (Xu et al., 2026) learns to chain basic capabilities for embodied task planning. MoMaStage (Li et al., 2026a) grounds planning in a skill-state graph, and Physical Agency (Galanti et al., 2026) places a closed-loop orchestrator above existing policies and robot skills. RoboClaw (Li et al., 2026c) extends agent coordination across data collection, policy learning, and task execution. 29 Preprint A newer line of work asks how much of the policy itself a general-purpose agent can provide. Agent as Policy (Jia et al., 2026) lets an agent inspect observations, write programs, issue robot commands, and adapt from physical feedback. GPT-Policy (Cheng et al., 2026) uses demonstrations and interaction history as context for verified robot tool calls. Evaluation of GPT-6 Astra examine direct action selection and its combination with a pretrained policy (Su et al., 2026); their results also expose the remaining difficulty of precise and dynamic control. Finally, GaP (Chen et al., 2026) uses coding agents and structured feedback to improve robot policies or executable skill graphs. Together, these studies shift attention toward the interfaces and feedback loops that connect general reasoning to reliable physical execution. 30
