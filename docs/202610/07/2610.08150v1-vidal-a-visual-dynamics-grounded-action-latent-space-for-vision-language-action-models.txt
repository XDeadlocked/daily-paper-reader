## Abstract 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have become a central paradigm for robot policy learning, which predict actions in three forms: raw action chunks, discrete action tokens, or continuous action latents. However, existing action representa-tions primarily model action trajectories, with limited consideration of the visual dynamics induced by these actions. We introduce ViDAL, a Visual Dynamics-grounded Action Latent Space that anchors continuous action latents in the future visual dynamics of the scene. Specifically, ViDAL learns action latent space by training an Action Variational Autoencoder (Action VAE) to reconstruct action chunks while aligning its latent with future scene dynamics. When integrated into downstream robot policies, the proposed Action VAE serves as a plug-in action interface compatible with multiple VLA architectures and enables optional future-video prediction as an additional capability. Empirically, ViDAL outperforms competitive baselines on LIBERO with 98.1% average success, improves a multi-task π0.5 policy on RoboTwin 2.0 from 54.3% to 65.5% (Clean) and from 33.2% to 43.1% (Random) success rates over 50 dual-arm tasks, and yields 20.0% and 23.4% absolute success-rate gains on real-world single-arm Franka and dual-arm ARX robot platforms. 

## 1 Introduction 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models [1–6] have emerged as a popular paradigm for generalist robot manipulation, directly predicting actions from visual observations and language instructions. Since actions define the interface between the VLA policy and robot control, their representation determines both the learning target and the executable control output. In recent VLA systems, action representations usually take three predominant forms: raw action chunks regressed by a continuous head [4, 5, 7], discrete action tokens produced by a tokenizer [8–11], or continuous action latents produced by a learned autoencoder [12]. These representations primarily model action trajectories, but manipulation actions are meaningful through their effects on the scene, such as object motion, contact changes, and task-state transitions. Accordingly, as VLA gradually scales to long-horizon, multi-task and multi-scenario manipulation [13–16], an effective action representation should account for not only the action trajectory but also the visual dynamics induced by the action. Recent works have pursued two complementary directions: learning compact action representations and incorporating visual dynamics into VLA policies. As shown in Figure 1(a), early action to-kenizers or autoencoders compress action chunks into compact tokens or latents [8–12], yielding representations primarily optimized for action reconstruction, without modeling visual dynamics. Together, these directions suggest that visual dynamics benefit action learning, but leave open how they should shape the action representation. Since actions are visually contextual, the challenge is learning an executable, visual dynamics-grounded action latent space. We propose ViDAL (Visual Dynamics-grounded Action Latent Space, Figure 1(c)) to inject visual dynamics directly into the action latent during representation learning. In ViDAL, a frozen video VAE extracts the initial and future visual latents from each robot trajectory, and an Action VAE encodes the corresponding action chunk into a continuous action latent. A lightweight visual-dynamics predictor then maps the initial visual latent and the action latent to the future visual latent, training the action latent to preserve executable actions while explaining future scene dynamics conditioned on the current observation. For downstream policy learning, only the frozen Action VAE is required: its encoder produces action latent targets for VLA training, and its decoder maps VLA-predicted latents to executable robot actions. Thus, the decoupled design lets ViDAL plug into both regression (e.g., OpenVLA-OFT [23]) and flow-matching (e.g., π0.5 [5]) action experts without modifying the VLM backbone. The video VAE also enables future-video prediction for visualizing the learned dynamics. We evaluate ViDAL on two simulation benchmarks (LIBERO [24], RoboTwin 2.0 [25]) and two real-world platforms (single-arm Franka, dual-arm ARX), using both regression (OpenVLA-OFT [23], StarVLA-OFT [26]) and flow-matching ( π0.5 [5]) action experts as the downstream policy. Across all four settings, ViDAL consistently improves the underlying VLA policies and surpasses competitive action-representation baselines on LIBERO (Figure 1, right): on the higher-dimensional dual-arm RoboTwin 2.0, it lifts a multi-task π0.5 policy by 11 .2% Clean and 9.9% Random over 50 tasks, and on real robots it delivers 20 .0% absolute gain on Franka and 23 .4% on ARX. These significant gains across simulation and real, single- and dual-arm settings establish visual-dynamics grounding as a better design principle for action representations. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We introduce ViDAL, which, to our knowledge, is the first approach to ground action latents in future visual dynamics for VLA models, learned via an Action VAE jointly trained on action reconstruction and future visual latent prediction. • We propose a plug-in action-latent interface for downstream VLA policies, where the frozen Action VAE serves as the action representation for both regression and flow-matching action experts, requiring no changes to the VLM backbone. • We conduct extensive experiments in both simulation (LIBERO, RoboTwin 2.0) and real-world (single-arm Franka, dual-arm ARX) environments. Results show that ViDAL consis-tently improves both regression and flow-matching VLA policies and surpasses competitive action-representation methods. 

## 2 Related Work 

Vision-Language-Action Models. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models adapt pretrained vision-language backbones to predict robot actions from visual observations and language instructions [1– 3, 27, 28, 16, 6]. Recent VLAs differ mainly in how their action experts generate actions: flow-matching experts in π0 [4] and π0.5 [5] support high-frequency continuous control, while one-step 2regression experts in OpenVLA-OFT [23] and StarVLA-OFT [26] target faster inference. ViDAL is orthogonal to these backbones and experts: it changes the action target to a dynamics-grounded latent and decodes the predicted latent back to executable actions, without modifying the underlying VLA architecture. 

Action Representations for VLA Models. VLA action representations take three predominant forms, each shaped by a different supervision signal. Raw action chunks are regressed under behavior-cloning [4–7, 29]; discrete action tokens compress trajectories via quantization [8–11]; and continuous action latents use an autoencoder bottleneck without quantization [12]. All three are shaped only by reconstruction, token efficiency, or imitation losses, and rarely consider how the scene visually evolves under each action. ViDAL explicitly introduces visual dynamics as a direct supervision signal for the action latent, anchoring the representation to the visual effect of each action chunk. 

Visual Dynamics for VLA Policies. There is also a complementary line that uses visual dynamics to guide VLA policies. JEPA-style methods pretrain on video to provide dynamics-aware features that condition the policy [17, 30]. Future-prediction approaches inject future-frame or video-diffusion signals into the VLM backbone or the policy as auxiliary supervision [18, 31, 32]. Other methods use dynamics or world-model signals for action reasoning [19, 21, 20, 22]. These designs entangle dynamics learning with policy training and tie the visual signal to a VLA architecture, so the dynamics machinery must be retrained for every new backbone. ViDAL confines visual dynamics to the action latent during representation learning and freezes it before policy training, so the same dynamics-grounded Action VAE can be plugged into different VLM backbones and action experts. 

## 3 Method 

3.1 Overview 

ViDAL learns a reusable action representation whose continuous latent is grounded by visual dy-namics. As shown in Figure 2, given a robot trajectory with observation clip ot:t+H and action chunk at:t+H−1, an Action VAE encodes the action chunk into a continuous action latent that is jointly supervised by action reconstruction and visual-dynamics prediction based on video VAE model(Section 3.2). Then the frozen Action VAE serves as a plug-in action representation for downstream VLA policy learning (Section 3.3), where the video model is no longer required. 

3.2 Learning the Action Latent Space 

The central challenge in learning action latents is that action reconstruction alone does not determine which aspects of an action chunk are relevant to policy learning: a low-dimensional latent may reconstruct actions while failing to encode how those actions interact with the current scene. ViDAL addresses this by adding visual-dynamics supervision, encouraging the action latent to be informative for predicting the future visual latent conditioned on the initial observation.

3.3 Integrating ViDAL into VLA Policies 

After representation learning, ViDAL replaces the raw-action interface of a VLA policy with an interface based on action latents: during training, the policy predicts an encoded action latent, and at inference, the predicted latent is decoded into an executable action chunk.

## 4 Experiments 

We evaluate ViDAL as a plug-in action representation for VLA policies, demonstrating whether visual-dynamics grounding improves downstream success across simulation and real robots. Following the experimental setup (Section 4.1), the experiments are organized around three questions: 1. Does ViDAL consistently improve VLA policies on simulation benchmarks (Section 4.2), and do the gains transfer to real-robot platforms (Section 4.5)? 2. Which training-objective terms and action-latent design choices drive these improvements (Section 4.3), and how do they scale with tasks and demonstrations (Section 4.4)? 3. Are ViDAL action latents more structured than raw actions, and do they correspond to recognizable future visual dynamics (Section 4.6)? 

4.1 Experimental Setup Benchmarks. We evaluate on two simulation benchmarks and two real-world platforms. LIBERO [24] covers single-arm language-conditioned manipulation across four task suites (Object, Spatial, Goal, Long); RoboTwin 2.0 [25] covers dual-arm manipulation across 50 tasks under a Clean setting matched to training and a Random setting with randomized scene conditions (e.g., background, lighting, distractors, viewpoint). Real-world deployment uses a single-arm Franka and a dual-arm ARX, each with a small set of tabletop tasks; we report task success rates (%) throughout. 

VLA backbones and baselines. We pair ViDAL with both regression and flow-matching action experts: OpenVLA-OFT [23] on LIBERO and StarVLA-OFT [26] on RoboTwin 2.0 for regression, and π0.5 [5] on both benchmarks for flow matching; the primary comparison contrasts raw-action targets against ViDAL latent targets on each backbone. On LIBERO we additionally compare against action-tokenization VLAs ( π0-FAST [8], FASTer [11]) and recent VLAs that augment the VLM backbone with visual dynamics or world models [33, 19, 34–36]. 

Training and evaluation. We train ViDAL per benchmark on its demonstrations with a frozen Wan VAE [37] as the video encoder, and reuse it as a frozen plug-in action representation across all VLA backbones. The latent dimension is D=16 for the dual-arm settings (RoboTwin 2.0, ARX) and D=8 for the single-arm settings (LIBERO, Franka), with loss weights λdyna =1 and λKL =10 −4

throughout. Each VLA policy is trained as a multi-task policy across all tasks of its benchmark, following the backbone’s original recipe with the action target replaced by the ViDAL latent.

4.4 Further Analysis 

Beyond the latent-design ablations above, we further analyze categorical design choices of ViDAL and how its gain scales with data, under the same controlled π0.5/RoboTwin 2.0 protocol as Section 4.3. 

Visual-dynamics supervision. The visual-dynamics objective Ldyna is most effective as a dense L1 regression onto a frozen Wan VAE feature (Fig. 4(a,b)). In panel (a), Wan VAE yields the strongest target among the four frozen video encoders, with CogVideoX [38], SVD [39], and the self-supervised V-JEPA target [17] all narrowing the +ViDAL gain. In panel (b), L1 supervises the action latent with per-feature regression on the entire future video-VAE map, a denser signal than the contrastive InfoNCE objective [40] that only enforces global similarity; replacing L1 with InfoNCE or stacking the two both degrade performance. 

Policy prediction target. The frozen ViDAL latent alone is the most effective policy target (Fig. 4(c)). A raw-only head reproduces the π0.5 baseline [5], while a joint head sits in between regardless of which branch decodes at inference. Splitting head capacity between two targets dilutes the supervision on the dynamics-grounded latent, itself a compact target the flow-matching expert fits on its own. 

Scaling with tasks and demonstrations. ViDAL improves π0.5 at every |T | and N tested, and the +ViDAL gain widens as data scales up (Fig. 5). Since the eval subset varies with |T | , the comparable trend is read from ∆ in panel (b), where the gain widens monotonically; panel (d) shows the same widening of ∆ along N . 4.5 Real-Robot Experiments Setup. We deploy ViDAL on two real-robot platforms to test whether its simulation gains transfer to physical hardware (Fig. 6). Results. ViDAL consistently improves π0.5 on every task across both platforms, raising the average success rate by +20 .0% on the single-arm Franka and +23 .4% on the dual-arm ARX (Fig. 6). On the dual-arm side, both deformable manipulation ( Fold Towel ) and rigid bimanual coordination ( Wipe Plate , Stack Bowls (dual-arm) ) benefit, and the average gain even exceeds that of the single-arm setup, suggesting that the dynamics-grounded latent is especially useful when contact-rich, higher-DoF behavior must be coordinated across two arms. 4.6 Qualitative Visualization and Analysis 

We examine the learned action latent space qualitatively, asking whether ViDAL yields a richer latent geometry than raw actions and whether it retains enough visual-dynamics information to reconstruct future video frames. 

Action latent space geometry. ViDAL produces a less collapsed action latent space than raw actions (Fig. 7). The top principal component absorbs 40 .9% of total variance for raw action chunks but only 15 .6% for ViDAL, and reaching 95% cumulative variance needs 14 versus 40 components. The participation ratio [41], the effective number of significant latent directions, rises from 4.7 to 14 .6

(3.1×). The 2D PCA projections confirm this: raw chunks collapse onto a few low-dimensional curves, whereas ViDAL latents spread across more directions and separate tasks more uniformly. Such a well-regularized latent is the kind of representation that flow-matching action experts learn effectively from [42, 43]. 

Future video prediction. The same action latent the VLA policy predicts also drives a recognizable future video rollout (Fig. 8). Feeding (z0, za) through the future visual latent predictor Pθ and decoding ˆzf with the frozen video VAE decoder yields frames that track the ground truth in object location, gripper pose, and contact events. The action latent therefore encodes what the scene should look like next, not only what the robot should do. Both the predictor and video decoder are used only for analysis and are not required during policy inference. 

## 5 Conclusion 

We propose ViDAL, a method that grounds the action latent space in future visual dynamics by jointly training an Action VAE on action reconstruction and video latent prediction. The frozen Action VAE then serves as a plug-in action representation for both regression and flow-matching VLA policies without modifying the VLM backbone, and consistently improves performance across two simulation benchmarks (LIBERO, RoboTwin 2.0) and two real-robot platforms (single-arm Franka, dual-arm ARX), and further analysis confirms that the advantage widens as tasks and demonstrations scale up. Since ViDAL decouples action-representation learning from VLA policy training, extending it to diverse action spaces and embodiments requires only retraining the lightweight Action VAE; leveraging large-scale egocentric human videos as an additional source of visual-dynamics supervision is a natural next step toward a universal dynamics-grounded action latent space. 9References

12 A Method details 

Action VAE architecture. The Action VAE maps a normalized action chunk at:t+H−1 ∈ RH×da to a short sequence of continuous action-latent tokens. Its encoder is a temporal convolutional network with residual blocks and strided temporal downsampling, so the latent sequence preserves the temporal order of the action chunk while reducing its length. The encoder predicts a Gaussian posterior with mean and variance parameters, from which the action latent is sampled during representation learning. For downstream VLA supervision, we use the posterior mean as a deterministic action-latent target. The decoder mirrors the encoder with temporal upsampling and reconstructs the original action chunk from the latent sequence. This design keeps the action representation continuous, temporally structured, and directly decodable into executable robot actions. 

Frozen video-latent target. For each action chunk, we use the aligned observation clip ot:t+H ,which contains one more frame than the action horizon. A frozen video VAE encodes this clip into spatiotemporal latent tokens. For a clip with H+1 = 4 k+1 frames, image size h×w, and video-latent channel dimension Cv , the flattened video latent has shape 

zv ∈ RTv Ns×Cv , Tv = k + 1 , Ns = ( h/ 16)( w/ 16) . (8) We split this latent into the first-frame spatial tokens z0 ∈ RNs×Cv and the future target zf ∈

R(Tv −1) Ns×Cv . The first-frame tokens provide the current visual context, while the future tokens provide the visual-dynamics target induced by the action chunk. 

Future-latent predictor. The dynamics predictor learns to infer the future visual latent from the current visual context and the action latent. It represents the current and future visual positions as a shared token sequence, conditions the future queries on the action-latent tokens through cross-attention, and predicts the future visual-latent tokens jointly: 

ˆzf = Pθ (z0, za). (9) Conditioning on z0 disambiguates actions whose visual effects depend on the current scene, while conditioning on za encourages the action latent to encode the scene transition caused by the action chunk. The predictor is used only during representation learning and is not part of the deployed VLA policy. 

Training objective. The Action VAE is trained with four terms: 

LViDAL = Lrec + λfreq Lfreq + λKL LKL + λdyna Ldyna . (10) The reconstruction term is an L1 loss on normalized actions, Lrec = ∥ˆa−a∥1. The frequency-domain term applies a discrete cosine transform along the temporal axis and computes an L1 distance between reconstructed and target frequency coefficients. The KL term is the standard Gaussian-posterior regularizer. The visual-dynamics term is an L1 loss on frozen video-latent tokens, 

Ldyna = ∥Pθ (z0, za) − sg( zf )∥1, (11) where sg( ·) denotes that the video-latent target is held fixed. The weights and schedules are benchmark-specific and are listed in Section B. 

Downstream VLA interface. After representation learning, the video VAE and future-latent predictor are discarded. The frozen Action VAE defines the interface between a VLA policy and the robot: its encoder converts each action chunk into a deterministic latent target, and its decoder converts predicted latents back into executable action chunks. We normalize the action latents before policy training and invert the normalization before decoding at inference. Thus, downstream VLA training changes only the action target space; the VLM backbone and the policy action expert remain otherwise unchanged.
