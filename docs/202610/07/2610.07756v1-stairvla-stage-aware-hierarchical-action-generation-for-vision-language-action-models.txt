# StairVLA: Stage-Aware Hierarchical Action Generation for Vision-Language-Action Models 

Shangyuan Yuan 1, Xinda Qi 1,2, Yujiang Pu 1, Wenliang Guo 1, Xiaobo Tan 11Michigan State University 2Ant Group 

{yuanshan, qixinda, puyujian, guowenli, xbtan }@msu.edu 

Abstract 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models increasingly rely on diffusion- or flow-matching-based action heads to generate continuous robot actions. These action heads typically process the denoising trajectory in a largely uniform manner. However, we observe that the conditioning focus naturally shifts across denoising stages: early stages combine language instructions and visual observations to establish a coarse action trajectory, whereas later stages place greater emphasis on current visual observations for action alignment. Based on this insight, we introduce StairVLA, a stage-aware hierarchical action generation framework that uses partially denoised actions as a natural interface between coarse long-horizon action generation and local refinement. A high-level VLA performs early denoising to produce a reusable long-horizon partially denoised action trajectory, while a lightweight refiner operates at a higher frequency to refine local action chunks using the latest observations. This design amortizes expensive high-level VLA computation while preserving frequent closed-loop correction. On LIBERO, our GR00T-style instantiation improves average success from 96.5% to 97.8% while reducing amortized inference latency from 115.0 ms to 44.2 ms per action chunk. More broadly, across two VLA backbones, simulation benchmarks, and real-robot tasks, StairVLA consistently reduces inference cost while maintaining strong task performance. # 1 Introduction 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models have demonstrated strong performance across a wide range of robot manipulation tasks (Kim et al., 2024), combining vision-language understanding with generative action heads to translate multimodal context into continuous robot actions (Black et al., 2024; Bjorck et al., 2025). Specifically, these diffusion- or flow-matching-based action heads are conditioned on language instructions, visual observations, and robot states to progressively transform noise into executable action trajectories (Chi et al., 2025). Despite this progressive generation process, multimodal conditioning is typically applied throughout the entire denoising trajectory, leaving open whether different types of conditioning information should always be processed together during action generation. Intuitively, language instructions typically specify task intent that remains stable over many control steps, whereas visual observations and robot states continuously change during execution. Task-level in-formation therefore need not be processed as frequently as the feedback required for precise closed-loop control. Existing hierarchical VLAs exploit this temporal asymmetry by processing high-level information at a lower frequency and communicating it to higher-frequency controllers through subgoals, waypoints, macro-actions, or learned representations (Liu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2026; Du et al., 2026). Such hierarchies are typically introduced through externally designed representations or model components. This leads to our central question: can such a hierarchy arise directly from the generative action process itself?

In practice, we observe that the denoising trajectory itself already exhibits such a structure. As illus-trated in Fig. 1, substantial action structure emerges well before denoising is complete, while the interaction between evolving action and multimodal conditioning continues to change throughout denoising stages. Intermediate actions are therefore not merely noisy versions of the final prediction: they already encode meaningful structure, even as subsequent denoising continues to reshape them toward execution. This re-veals a natural stage dependence in generative VLA action formation, with different denoising stages serving distinct functional roles. This behavior is also consistent with the coarse-to-fine progression commonly ob-served in generative denoising (Balaji et al., 2022; Falck et al., 2025). This observation suggests a direct way to exploit the hierarchy already present in denoising: expensive task conditioning can be concentrated in less frequent early-stage generation, while later stages perform frequent local refinement using up-to-date observations. However, this separation raises a key challenge: how can the refiner preserve task information without repeatedly processing the full instruction? Our key insight is that partially denoised actions themselves can bridge the two stages. By the end of early denoising, they already encode task-relevant action structure while remaining open to further refinement. The low-level refiner can therefore operate directly on these intermediate actions together with the latest observation, preserving high-level task information without repeatedly processing the full instruction. Based on this insight, we introduce StairVLA , a stage-aware hierarchical action generation framework. A high-level VLA performs the early denoising stages over a long-horizon action trajectory and outputs par-tially denoised actions that are cached across multiple control steps. For each local chunk, a lightweight re-finer takes the corresponding local action segment together with the latest observation and cached high-level context, and completes the remaining refinement before execution. This allows expensive task-conditioned generation to be reused across multiple control steps while keeping local actions responsive to the evolving environment. Importantly, the two stages communicate directly through intermediate states of the generative action trajectory, rather than through an externally designed action abstraction. We evaluate our approach across two VLA backbones, simulation benchmarks, and real-robot manip-ulation tasks. On LIBERO, our GR00T-style instantiation improves average success from 96.5% to 97.8% while reducing amortized inference latency from 115.0 ms to 44.2 ms per action chunk, with consistent performance-efficiency gains on a π-style VLA backbone. Although performance gradually degrades as the reuse horizon increases, further analysis shows that StairVLA maintains strong performance over longer high-level reuse horizons than prior hierarchical VLA methods. # 2 Related work 

Action Chunking and Unified VLA Models. Unified VLA models typically map language instructions, visual observations, and robot states into action sequences within a single policy. Recent VLA models fur-ther adopt generative action heads based on diffusion or flow matching to produce continuous actions (Black et al., 2024; Bjorck et al., 2025; Chi et al., 2025). These methods commonly use action chunking, predicting a sequence of future actions rather than a single-step action to improve temporal consistency and reduce policy invocation frequency (Zhao et al., 2023; Chi et al., 2025). However, action chunking introduces an inherent horizon trade-off: short chunks require frequent invocation of computationally expensive VLMs, while long chunks weaken closed-loop feedback and real-time correction during execution (Zhang et al., 2026). Therefore, processing different information and generating actions at a single temporal scale may be unnecessarily restrictive. 

Hierarchical VLA Models. Recent works increasingly adopt hierarchical structures to bridge high-level task understanding and low-level robot control. Earlier methods use explicit intermediate goals to guide low-level policies, such as subgoals, waypoints, keypoints, or language sub-instructions (Hu et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2025). More recent works further integrate this hierarchy into the model architecture and explore different high-to-low-level interfaces, including VLM tokens, learned features, 3discrete macro-actions, and coarse action representations (Cui et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026). Other dual-system approaches focus more explicitly on temporal organization, using event-driven high-level updates or allowing different levels to operate at different frequencies (Chen et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026). Despite their different forms, these methods highlight a common design question in hierarchical VLA: how should high-level task intent be transferred to low-level action generation? Rather than using manually designed or learned interfaces, we directly leverage intermediate states formed during action generation as a natural high-to-low-level interface. 

Generative Action Models and Intermediate Denoising States. Diffusion- and flow-based generation progressively transforms noise into executable actions. In both generative modeling and robot policies, prior works assign different denoising experts to different sampling stages to better accommodate stage-specific generation requirements (Balaji et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024; Reuss et al., 2025; Du et al., 2026; Niu et al., 2026). Another line of work reuses partially denoised trajectories or constructs better warm starts to reduce redundant sampling and control latency (Høeg et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026). Our method further uses partially denoised actions to transfer task intent across stages, enabling high-level coarse action generation and local refinement to use different conditioning and operate at different frequencies. 

# 3 Method 

3.1 Overview 

As illustrated in Fig. 2, we decompose VLA action generation into two stages that operate at different frequencies. The high-level VLA is invoked at a lower frequency to perform early denoising and produce a long-horizon partially denoised action trajectory, which is reused across multiple local action chunks. The low-level refiner runs at a higher frequency, taking the corresponding local action segment and the latest visual observation to produce an executable action chunk. This design reduces repeated VLM computation while preserving closed-loop correction with updated observations. Our framework is designed to be applicable to flow-matching-based generative VLAs and is trained in two stages. The high-level VLA retains the original VLA architecture with an extended action horizon. The lightweight refiner is the main additional module and follows the design of the VLA action head. It starts from partially denoised actions produced by the trained high-level VLA and performs local refinement using current observations. The high-level generation, refiner architecture and training, and multi-rate inference procedures are detailed in the following subsections.

3.3 Observation-Conditioned Low-Level Refinement 

As illustrated in Figure 3, we introduce a lightweight refiner to further refine the partially denoised local action segments produced by the high-level VLA. The refiner uses a SigLIP-B/16 visual encoder to process the latest observation, which is jointly trained. Meanwhile, the hidden states from the last layer of the high-level VLM are compressed into a fixed number of tokens through adaptive average pooling, which are cached and reused across multiple refinement cycles. As illustrated on the right side of Figure 3, the refiner follows the overall architecture of the GR00T action head (Bjorck et al., 2025) and takes two types of inputs: action-side tokens and condition-side tokens. The action side includes the robot state token, learnable future tokens, and the current query action chunk, while the condition side consists of visual tokens, compressed VLM tokens, and temporal action context extracted from the cached high-level trajectory. Self-attention operates only on the action-side tokens to model dependencies within the action representation, whereas cross-attention uses the action-side tokens as queries to incorporate information from the condition-side tokens. These two attention layers are interleaved throughout the DiT, alternating between action modeling and conditional fusion. The refiner training does not directly follow the original flow-matching path of the high-level VLA. Due to prediction errors, its partially denoised actions may not lie exactly on the ideal generation path defined by the denoising origin and the ground-truth. Therefore, as illustrated in Figure 4, for each local query chunk, we reconstruct a refinement path toward the ground-truth. For clarity, we omit the local chunk indices in this subsection. 3.4 Multi-Rate Inference and Horizon Reuse 

During inference, each high-level VLA invocation gener-ates and caches a partially denoised trajectory of horizon 

H, which is reused across the following M low-level refinement cycles. At each cycle, the low-level refiner extracts the corresponding partially denoised segment of length h from the cached trajectory and further refines it. Let Thigh and Tref denote the inference time of one high-level invocation and one low-level refinement, respectively. The average inference cost per local action chunk is 

T (M ) = Tref + Thigh 

M . (9) As M increases, the high-level VLA cost is amortized over more local action chunks. However, a larger 

M also reuses the same high-level trajectory for a longer horizon, which may reduce its consistency with the latest environment state. Therefore, M controls the trade-off between computational efficiency and the freshness of high-level information. We evaluate this trade-off under different reuse horizons in Section 4.

Training Setup. We adopt a two-stage training strategy. On LIBERO, we first train the high-level VLA for 30k steps and then freeze the high-level policy to train the low-level refiner for another 30k steps. By default, the refiner is trained using high-level trajectories at denoising progress α = 0 .8. We use AdamW with a global batch size of 128, an initial learning rate of 5 × 10 −5, cosine learning-rate decay, 1k warmup steps, and a weight decay of 0.01. All models are trained with bf16 mixed precision on 8 NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs. 

Inference and Latency Measurement. Unless otherwise specified, we use high-level trajectories at α =0.97 and reuse each trajectory for M = 4 refinement cycles, with h = 5 actions per cycle. Latency is measured per executable action chunk and includes the amortized high-level inference cost and low-level refinement cost, excluding environment execution. All measurements are conducted on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU. Additional implementation, training, and preprocessing details are provided in Appendix A.

LIBERO-Plus. We further evaluate our method on LIBERO-Plus (Fei et al., 2026), which introduces seven types of perturbations to the original LIBERO tasks. In the zero-shot setting, the model trained on LIBERO is directly evaluated in the perturbed environments, while in the supervised setting, it is further fine-tuned on the LIBERO-Plus training set using the same hyperparameter configuration as in LIBERO. As shown in Fig. 5, even without any masking, the error rises sharply near the end of denoising, suggesting that a unified generation mechanism is not equally effective across all stages. Masking the image further causes a larger error increase at later stages, supporting the stronger dependence on visual observations during late-stage refinement. This effect decreases again near completion, possibly because the remaining residuals can be largely corrected through simple local smoothing. These results motivate our stage-wise generation design and observation-conditioned refiner. Additional details on the real-world platform, datasets, training and evaluation protocols, and per-task results are provided in Appendix C. 10 5 Conclusion 

We propose StairVLA, a stage-aware hierarchical action generation framework that combines low-frequency high-level generation with high-frequency observation-conditioned refinement to reduce inference cost while maintaining strong task performance. More importantly, our results suggest that VLA action generation is not a homogeneous denoising process: different stages exhibit different information dependencies, while partially denoised actions themselves already encode task-relevant information for subsequent refinement. Our method exploits this stage difference by explicitly separating early-stage generation from later-stage refinement. Further understanding the functional roles of different denoising stages, better utilizing the information encoded in intermediate actions, and learning such hierarchical structures adaptively remain important directions for future research.

# A Implementation Details 

Unless otherwise specified, all experiments use the model architecture, training protocol, and evaluation settings described in this section. 

A.1 Model Architecture 

High-level policy. The high-level policy uses Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct as the vision-language backbone, followed by a GR00T-style flow-matching action head. The action prediction horizon is set to H = 32 .

Low-level refiner. The low-level refiner adopts a DiT-style flow-matching architecture with 16 Trans-former blocks, hidden dimension 768, 12 attention heads, and FFN dimension 3072. Cross-attention and self-attention blocks are interleaved. The refiner predicts an action chunk of length h = 5 .

Visual and high-level conditioning. The refiner uses a trainable SigLIP-B/16 visual encoder initialized from google/siglip-base-patch16-224 , with RGB observations from both the primary and wrist cameras. The final-layer hidden states of the high-level VLM are first projected to dimension 768 and then compressed along the token dimension using adaptive average pooling, producing eight high-level latent tokens. These features are cached together with the high-level action trajectory and reused across multiple low-level refinement cycles. 

Local action context. For each query chunk, the refiner additionally uses five coarse actions before and five coarse actions after the query segment from the same high-level trajectory as local temporal context. The query chunk itself is excluded from the context. A learned padding token is used when the required context extends beyond the trajectory boundary. 

Multi-rate inference. The refiner is invoked once every h = 5 environment steps and returns five actions that are executed sequentially. The high-level policy is refreshed every M low-level refinement cycles, corresponding to 5M environment steps. Refined actions are not written back into the cached high-level trajectory; each local chunk is refined from the original high-level coarse trajectory. 14 A.2 Training Details 

High-level policy training. The high-level policy is trained on the combined LIBERO dataset for 30k steps with an action horizon of H = 32 . After training, both the high-level VLM and action head are frozen during refiner training. 

Refiner training. During refiner training, the frozen high-level policy remains active to generate interme-diate action trajectories. The high-level policy is run to a fixed denoising progress α = 0 .8, producing a partially denoised trajectory C.For each training sample, we then sample

The refiner predicts the remaining flow velocity toward the ground-truth action and is trained using a validity-masked mean-squared error. The refiner is trained for 30k steps. 

Optimization. The refiner is trained with AdamW using a learning rate of 5 × 10 −5 for the refiner head and a weight decay of 0.01 . We use a cosine learning-rate schedule with 1k warmup steps. Training uses bf16 mixed precision, DeepSpeed ZeRO-2, and gradient clipping, and is conducted on eight NVIDIA RTX A6000 48GB GPUs. The per-GPU batch size is 16 with no gradient accumulation, resulting in a global batch size of 128. 

Dataset and preprocessing. We train on the combined LIBERO dataset and evaluate on LIBERO-Spatial, LIBERO-Object, LIBERO-Goal, and LIBERO-Long. The action dimension is 7 and the high-level action horizon is 32. Continuous action dimensions are min–max normalized to [−1, 1] . Action sequences extend-ing beyond episode boundaries are zero-padded, and invalid padded steps are excluded from the training loss using a validity mask. # B Additional Experimental Results and Analysis 

B.1 Denoising-Stage Conditioning Analysis 

We further analyze how generative VLA action heads use different conditioning information throughout the denoising process. Specifically, we examine how action queries allocate cross-attention to different conditioning sources across denoising stages in both GR00T and π0. This analysis is intended as a diagnostic of stage-dependent conditioning behavior rather than a strict causal attribution of individual token groups to the final action prediction. 

B.1.1 Experimental Setup 

Here, we use two trained StarVLA checkpoints based on GR00T and π0, and randomly sample 16 observation– instruction pairs from the LIBERO LeRobot dataset. All analyses are performed at inference time without additional training or parameter updates. 15 To examine how attention evolves throughout denoising, we densify the inference trajectory into 100 analysis stages and record the cross-attention probability from action queries to conditioning-token keys at each stage. This 100-stage trajectory is used only for analysis and visualization and does not correspond to the native inference setting of the models, whose default inference trajectory uses four denoising steps. For each model, we analyze both the first and last action-head layers, resulting in four cases: GR00T first layer, GR00T last layer, π0 first layer, and π0 last layer. At each denoising stage, we first average the attention probabilities over attention heads and action-query tokens to obtain a key-side attention distribution. We then sum the attention mass over tokens belonging to the same group and normalize it by the total attention mass over valid tokens. The reported curves represent the mean attention mass over the 16 sampled observation–instruction pairs. We divide the conditioning tokens into five groups: task instruction , wrapper prompt , grounding prompt ,

image tokens , and special tokens . The task instruction corresponds to the actual LIBERO command, such as “pick up the cream cheese and place it in the basket.” The wrapper prompt refers to template text preceding the task instruction, such as “Your task is.” The grounding prompt refers to localization-oriented template text following the task instruction, such as a request to identify key objects using bounding-box coordi-nates. Importantly, the grounding prompt specifies only the output format and does not contain ground-truth bounding boxes. Image tokens correspond to visual inputs, while special tokens include chat-template to-kens, image-boundary tokens, role-boundary tokens, padding tokens, and other special tokens. For compact visualization, wrapper-prompt, grounding-prompt, and special tokens can be grouped as “other prompt tokens.” In the analysis below, we keep the five groups separate to better examine the source of attention shifts across models. 

B.1.2 Cross-Attention Allocation across Denoising Stages 

The overall results are shown in Fig. 11. Both GR00T and π0 exhibit changes in cross-attention allocation throughout denoising, indicating that the action head does not read conditioning information in a completely static manner during generation. Compared with the last layer, these changes are more pronounced in the first action-head layer. The first-layer result of π0 provides the clearest example. As denoising approaches later stages, the attention mass allocated to image tokens increases substantially, while the proportion assigned to prompt-related tokens decreases. This suggests that late-stage action queries in π0 place greater explicit emphasis on visual conditioning, consistent with the interpretation that later refinement increasingly focuses on alignment with the current observation. GR00T exhibits a different surface trend. In its first action-head layer, image-token attention decreases toward later stages, while attention to grounding-prompt and other prompt-related positions increases. We attribute this difference primarily to the different conditioning architectures of the two models. π0 uses layer-wise VLM conditioning, such that its first action-head layer receives comparatively shallow VLM-contextualized representations. In contrast, GR00T conditions its action head on the final-layer hidden states of Qwen2.5-VL. Therefore, even at the first action-head layer, token positions corresponding to grounding or prompt text have already undergone multiple layers of visual-language contextualization. Consequently, the increase in attention to grounding-related token positions in GR00T should not be interpreted simply as an increased reliance on template text. Importantly, these grounding representations themselves encode image-derived spatial information, such as object bounding boxes. Their increased at-tention therefore more likely reflects a reallocation of cross-attention toward more explicit visual-spatial grounding representations during later denoising stages. Overall, although GR00T and π0 exhibit different attention patterns, both suggest a shift toward more visually grounded information in later denoising stages. B.3 Long-Horizon Action Generation and Reuse

Therefore, one high-level policy invocation corresponds to an effective execution horizon of 5M actions. For H = 48 and H = 64 , the maximum refinement horizons used during training are 5×9 = 45 and 5×12 = 60 actions, respectively. when the reuse horizon is extended to 40–60 actions. Notably, increasing the high-level prediction horizon from H = 48 to H = 64 does not provide a clear performance advantage under long reuse. When the high-level policy generates and caches an increasingly long future plan, the subsequent execution must continue to operate under an evolving closed-loop state. Although the low-level refiner can use the latest visual observation to locally adjust the executable actions, its refinement remains conditioned on the cached high-level trajectory. Consequently, when the upper-level plan spans an excessively long timescale and is reused without refresh, local refinement may no longer be sufficient to compensate for the growing mismatch between the cached plan and the actual closed-loop state. From this perspective, the efficiency benefit of hierarchical VLA inference cannot be obtained simply by continuously increasing the high-level prediction horizon or reuse length. A longer cached trajectory further amortizes the expensive high-level VLA invocation, but simultaneously reduces the frequency at which the high-level decision process can respond to newly observed states. Our results therefore suggest a practical trade-off between high-level reuse and closed-loop responsiveness: within a moderate reuse range, low-level refinement can effectively provide local closed-loop correction while reducing high-level inference frequency; beyond this range, accumulated uncertainty and plan–state mismatch may exceed what the local refiner can compensate for, leading to substantial performance degradation. Rather than refreshing the high-level trajectory after a fixed number of refinement cycles, an adaptive mechanism could trigger a new high-level VLA invocation according to the observed state deviation, action uncertainty, or consistency between the cached trajectory and the current observation. where the translational delta is defined in the robot world frame, the rotational delta is represented as an axis-angle increment in the local end-effector frame, and g∗ denotes the absolute gripper target. During execution, the predicted end-effector target pose is converted to joint targets through local inverse kinematics. For safe and consistent real-world evaluation, all methods use the same control constraints: the per-step translation and rotation are limited to 0.05 m and 0.20 rad , respectively, and the maximum joint velocity is limited to 25 ◦/s. Before each trial, the robot returns to a fixed initial configuration, the internal action cache is cleared, and the cameras are warmed up. Fruit25 and PushBlock are executed at control frequencies of 8 Hz and 20 Hz, respectively. Inference is synchronous: when the action buffer is empty, the control loop waits for a new action chunk and then executes the predicted actions sequentially at the corresponding control frequency. Independently of the policy control frequency, robot feedback is recorded at approximately 50 Hz for subsequent trajectory-quality and motion-smoothness analysis. All real-world experiments are conducted on the same NVIDIA RTX A4000 GPU. 

C.2 Datasets and Data Processing 

The two real-world environments are illustrated in Figs. 15 and 16. Fruit25 is collected through VR tele-operation and contains 25 tasks. Among them, 24 are single-fruit pick-and-place tasks formed by four fruit 21 categories—apple, orange, lemon, and lime—and six target locations, including five colored baskets and one metal tray. The final task is a long-horizon multi-object fruit-sorting task. We perform quality control on 1,220 collected episodes through trajectory inspection, video inspection, and manual replay review, remov-ing episodes with clear execution errors, corrupted recordings, or abnormal trajectory segments. This results in 1,186 valid demonstrations with 109,684 frames. PushBlock contains a single continuous pushing task in which the robot pushes a black block into a blue target region. The dataset contains 100 demonstrations with 32,776 frames. Both Fruit25 and PushBlock include top and wrist RGB observations, end-effector poses, gripper states, and language instructions. The two datasets use different sampling and control frequencies. Fruit25 mainly consists of relatively discrete and quasi-static manipulation behaviors such as grasping, transporting, and placing objects, for which 8 Hz provides sufficient temporal resolution. In contrast, PushBlock requires sustained contact with the object and more frequent closed-loop corrections during execution, so we use a higher frequency of 20 Hz to provide finer-grained motion control. All demonstrations are recorded only after robot initialization is completed, so initialization motion is excluded from the training trajectories. We do not apply a unified automatic trimming procedure to remove stationary segments at the beginning or end of trajectories; instead, low-quality episodes are removed as complete trajectories. C.3 Model Configuration and Training 

We compare StarVLA-GR00T, StarVLA-π, and our hierarchical model. All three methods use Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct as the vision-language backbone and are trained on the same data, language instructions, and state/action representations for each benchmark. StarVLA-GR00T and StarVLA-π are fully fine-tuned end-to-end and use an action horizon of 8. For our method, we set the high-level action horizon to H = 32 , the low-level chunk size to h = 8 , and the reuse length to M = 3 , corresponding to 24 executed actions between two high-level policy updates. During refiner training, the high-level partial-denoising progress is sampled from [0 .6, 1.0] , the low-level refinement scale from [0 .8, 1.0] , and the temporal-context trajectory is fully denoised. At inference time, we use a high-level denoising progress of 0.97 , two high-level inference steps, and one low-level refinement step, with the refiner operating from an assumed denoising progress of 0.97 .For Fruit25, all models are trained for 30k steps, while for PushBlock they are trained for 10k steps. On Fruit25, StarVLA-GR00T and both stages of our method use a global batch size of 64, while StarVLA-π

uses a global batch size of 32. On PushBlock, all three methods use an effective batch size of 8. We use a cosine learning-rate schedule with 1,000 warm-up steps, a main learning rate of 10 −4 for the action-related modules, and 2.5 × 10 −5 for the vision-language backbone.

C.5 Detailed Main Results 

We further report detailed success rates and latency results for the two benchmarks. Fruit25 is primarily composed of quasi-static pick-and-place tasks and is executed at 8 Hz; therefore, an 8-step action chunk corresponds to approximately 1 s of execution. As a result, inference is invoked relatively sparsely and the latency difference is less visually apparent during execution. In contrast, PushBlock runs at 20 Hz, where the same 8-step chunk covers only about 0.4 s, making inference latency more critical to continuous closed-loop control. In addition, real-world deployment introduces fixed overhead from image transmission, inverse kinematics, and robot control, which partially reduces the relative latency gain compared with simulation. The advantage of our method is more pronounced on PushBlock. StarVLA-GR00T and StarVLA-π

mainly succeed in simple cases where the block is approximately aligned below the target region and can be pushed along a near-straight trajectory, as illustrated in Fig. 17. With a longer high-level horizon, our method can form a more extended motion plan and succeeds repeatedly under more challenging initial layouts requiring larger directional corrections. C.6 Motion Smoothness and Execution Continuity 

We further analyze the motion smoothness and execution continuity of the real robot using independently recorded high-rate feedback. Since PushBlock contains only 10 evaluation trials per method, we restrict this analysis to Fruit25. Both successful and failed trials are included. Table 7 reports the median and interquartile range (IQR) over these trials. We use median-based statistics to reduce sensitivity to occasional outlier trajectories and long execution pauses. We use three complementary measures of motion quality. Translational SPARC and Rotational SPARC are spectral arc-length measures computed from the magnitudes of the end-effector linear and an-gular velocities (Balasubramanian et al., 2015), respectively; values closer to zero indicate smoother motion. 

Translational and Rotational dimensionless jerk measure the temporal variation of translational and rota-tional motion after normalization by trajectory duration and motion magnitude (Hogan and Sternad, 2009); we report the same log 10 dimensionless jerk cost used throughout our analysis, where lower values indicate smoother motion. Stop ratio is the fraction of the active-motion interval for which the end-effector linear velocity falls below 0.01 m /s, with lower values indicating fewer execution pauses.
