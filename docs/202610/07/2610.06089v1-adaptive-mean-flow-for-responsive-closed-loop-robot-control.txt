# Adaptive Mean Flow for Responsive Closed-Loop Robot Control 

Aksel Vaaler 1, Marco Job 1, Christian Holden 1, and Olav Egeland 1

Abstract —Diffusion- and flow-based robot policies have recently become widespread in robotic Imitation Learning (IL) due to their high performance and ability to model continuous and multimodal distributions. However, the iterative denoising procedure used by these models introduces significant prediction latency, hindering high-frequency closed-loop robot control and leading to jittery, unstable motion when frequent updates to the robot’s action predictions are used. Therefore, it is common practice to train models to predict chunks of actions that can be executed sequentially without feedback, even when this reduces responsiveness and may mean the most recent state information is not used. In this article, we present Adaptive Mean Flow (AMF), a flow-based IL method that enables smooth and responsive, fully closed-loop robot control. AMF uses Mean Flow, which is an accelerated form of Flow Matching (FM), to minimize prediction latency. To ensure smoothness and consistency across predictions, AMF uses a corrupted version of the trajectory from the previous step when predicting new robot actions, with the signal-to-noise ratio increasing over the time parameter of the trajectory. This discourages large changes in the prediction from one step to the next, while allowing freedom to adapt the predictions for future steps. We evaluate AMF across a wide range of simulated and real robot tasks and demonstrate significantly improved performance compared with baselines. Diffusion models [1], [2] and Flow Matching (FM) models [3] have recently become widespread in robotic Imitation Learning (IL) [4]. These models capture complex, high-dimensional distributions and are well-suited to model diverse, human-collected robot demonstration trajectories. Diffusion-and flow-based methods work by gradually transforming (typ-ically Gaussian) noise into approximate samples from the data distribution in a process referred to as denoising [1]. This iterative procedure yields high sample quality at the cost of slower inference speed. To take full advantage of the capacity afforded by these models while minimizing the impact of slow inference speed, it is proposed in [5] to predict sequences of future robot actions rather than just the action at the current timestep. This allows for faster execution of robot actions than the prediction speed of the policy, since action predictions from the previous control step can be sequentially passed to the robot while the policy calculates the next action sequence. This technique is called action chunking [6], where a “chunk” refers to the actions in a predicted sequence that the robot executes before replanning. Another benefit of action chunking is improved temporal consistency during deployment [5]. A. Speeding up diffusion and flow inference 

Diffusion and flow models rely on iterative refinement (de-noising) to produce high-quality output samples, which means that multiple forward passes of the network are needed per model prediction. Considerable research has been conducted to accelerate the inference process while maintaining quality. For applications with real-time demands, such as robotics, the deterministic Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models (DDIM) sampler proposed in [11] has largely been preferred in favor of the stochastic Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM) sampler originally proposed in [1], as it generally re-quires fewer denoising steps to produce good samples. Consis-tency Policy [12] can produce high-quality robot actions with very few denoising steps by utilizing the diffusion distillation framework developed in [13]. Similarly, One-Step Diffusion Policy [14] proposes a distillation scheme that enables training a student model in a single denoising step to mimic the action predictions of a pre-trained Diffusion Policy [5] network. Streaming Diffusion Policy [9] and Diffusion Forcing [10] train the networks using varying noise levels across the action sequence. This allows inference using a “stair-case” noise structure, where action predictions for near-future time steps are initialized with less noise and thus require fewer denoising steps. A flow model can itself be viewed as a faster alternative to dif-fusion, typically requiring far fewer integration steps compared with diffusion and DDPM sampling [3]. However, induced curvature in the probability flow field used to generate samples means that several ODE integration steps are still required [8]. Analogous to Consistency Policy [12], Consistency Flow Policy [15] trains the network with additional consistency constraints on the flow ODE [16] to enable action generation with fewer integration steps. Streaming Flow Policy (SFP) [17] takes a different approach by formulating action trajectories as flows. Starting from the last robot action, SFP treats a step along the flow ODE as a step along the forward robot trajectory, meaning the next robot action is “ready” after a single integration step. Our AMF method utilizes Mean Flow [8], which was also used in [18], and enables single-step action sequence prediction of high quality without the need for distillation, additional consistency losses, or complex inference algorithms. 

B. Consistency-reactivity trade-off in robotic Imitation Learn-ing 

The aforementioned works all address the problem of speeding up diffusion/flow inference, which offers immediate benefits in terms of smoother policy execution [17], [9]. However, they still use action chunking to ensure consistent robot motion and thus do not fully utilize the responsiveness enabled by fast inference. Comparatively few works directly address the consistency-reactivity trade-off between action chunking and fully closed-loop, single-step action execution. However, there is a growing interest, particularly in developing test-time al-gorithms to improve the performance of pre-trained chunking-based policies during deployment. Bidirectional Decoding [7] and Past-Token Prediction [19] introduce algorithms that select the next action sequence from a set of candidates, with selec-tion criteria that aim to balance consistency with the previous prediction and responsiveness to the most recent observation. Self-Guided Action Diffusion [20] and Real-Time Chunking [21] use weighted distances from the previous prediction to the current prediction to steer a diffusion/flow model during the denoising process, producing action sequences that more closely align with the trajectory prediction from the previous step. Temporal Action Selection [22] also employs a pre-trained IL policy, but uses online RL fine-tuning to train an action selector network to adaptively update the action trajectory based on current and previous predictions from the pre-trained policy. In contrast, our AMF method uses no fine-tuning or additional test-time compute. By training the model on action sequences with dynamic per-step signal-to-noise ratio, flexibility is re-tained with respect to controlling the behavior of the policy. During deployment, AMF predicts actions rapidly in closed-loop, maintaining consistency simply by initializing the noisy samples around the previous action trajectory. II. B ACKGROUND

IV. S IMULATION EXPERIMENTS 

In this section, we present a comprehensive set of simulated experiments to evaluate the overall efficacy of our proposed method and investigate key design choices. 

A. Simulation environments 

Metaworld: To assess each method’s capability to perform varied, precise, and smooth robot manipulation, we extract 8 challenging tasks from the widely used Metaworld object manipulation benchmark [26]. For each task, we collect 100 expert demonstrations using pre-trained RL policies included in the official repository of [26]. The action space consists of 3DOF end-effector velocities and gripper open/close. To test policy responsiveness and robustness, we conduct additional experiments in which correlated noise is added to the robot’s actions, requiring policies to adjust quickly to end-effector perturbations in order to solve the tasks. 

Push-T: Push-T is a 2D manipulation task where the agent must carefully align a T-shaped object within a goal region. The dataset contains 90 highly multimodal demonstrations collected via human teleoperation, so smoothness and temporal mode consistency are important for solving the task. 

Dynamic Block Push (Dyn-BP) : This is a dynamic and multimodal 2D manipulation where the agent must push a red and a green block into either of 2 goal zones that are drifting randomly within the environment. The dataset contains 1000 episodes. 

D3IL: Lastly we include the Stack-1 and Stack-2 tasks from the D3IL benchmark [27]. D3IL tasks contain multiple so-lution strategies and the corresponding datasets consist of diverse, human-collected demonstrations. Stack-1 and Stack-2 evaluate the agent’s ability to successfully stack 1 block or 2 blocks, respectively. Dataset size: 1000 episodes. 

B. Baselines 

We compare AMF against a varied set of baseline policies. All presented policies use a transformer architecture based on [5] as the policy network. Noisy robot actions as represent the transformer input tokens, while observations o and flow indices 

s (and optionally r) are projected to the token dimension and incorporated using cross-attention. The transformer network has 8 layers, 4 heads, and an embedding size of 256. All policies use the past To = 9 observation frames and predict 

Tp = 10 future actions. For the chunking variants, we set the chunk length to 8. With a control frequency of 10Hz, this gives an open-loop horizon of 0.8s, which has been shown to work well for quasi-static tasks [5]. 

FM-8 , FM-1 and FM-RTC predict robot actions using Flow Matching [3], and are trained using Eq. 5. FM-8 uses a chunk length of Ta = 8 (replans every 8th step), while FM-1 uses single-step action execution. FM-RTC uses Real-Time Chunking [21] at inference time, an algorithm that leverages past action sequence predictions to improve consistency across subsequent model predictions. AFM is equivalent to AMF but with Flow Matching instead of Mean Flow. AFM uses the same architecture as AMF. All FM-based methods use 10 denoising iterations. 

MF-8 and MF-1 use Mean Flow [8] (Eq. 6), and predict action sequences directly from Gaussian noise in a single denoising step. MF-8 and MF-1 use chunk lengths of 8and 1, respectively. MF-WarmStart (MF-WS ) reuses past trajectories similar to AMF, but with a constant noise-level across the trajectory instead of increasing noise. MF-WS is evaluated with Ta = 1 and a corruption level of s = 0 .5.

SDP [9] uses variable-noise diffusion to reduce the number of denoising iterations needed to predict action sequences. We implemented SDP using the same transformer architecture and time-step conditioning (AdaLNZero) as AMF. Consistency Policy (CP) [12] distills a diffusion-based action policy into a student model using consistency-preserving objective [13], allowing inference with a single denoising step. We evaluate both SDP and CTM with Ta = 1 .

C. Results 

Table I summarizes the main simulation results. AMF per-forms stably across all tasks and achieves a 6% relative improvement over the best-performing baseline, AFM. The Mean Flow baselines MF-8 and MF-1 perform worse than the Flow Matching baselines, indicating that naively swap-ping Flow Matching for Mean Flow, while enabling much faster inference, reduces model prediction quality. Note that inference delay does not impact the simulation environments, so we defer this analysis to Section V. AMF (and AFM) performs significantly better than SDP, even though both meth-ods use adaptive noise schedules and the same architecture. AMF fully denoises the predicted trajectory at every step and subsequently corrupts it with fresh noise (according to the adaptive schedule). SDP, by contrast, partially denoises each action in the trajectory at each step, appending fresh noise only at the end (i.e., the farthest future point) of the action sequence. We hypothesize that the added variation provided by the AMF/AFM approach leads to better performance compared with SDP. CP performs relatively poorly, and the training of the student policy was unstable for some tasks. We used the same transformer-based architecture for all baselines, which differs from the Unet architecture used in [12]. However, training instability is a known drawback of consistency models [28]. Metaworld tasks with the largest performance discrepancies, such as Basketball, Box-close, and Pick-out-of-hole, gener-ally require very precise manipulation to succeed. Indeed, we observe that AMF produces smooth and accurate robot trajectories. Push-T, on the other hand, also requires occasional trajectory “switches” to solve reliably, e.g., switching from pushing on the side of the block to pushing from the top to align it vertically. If the policy is overly biased towards smooth motion, it is prone to getting stuck in a single task phase and eventually stalling. However, this is not the case for AMF, which we attribute to the increased corruption applied to future action predictions, providing sufficient flexibility to switch trajectories when needed.
