## ABSTRACT 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models increasingly rely on action experts that generate short action chunks under receding-horizon control. While chunk-level training is convenient across robot embodiments, it optimizes local action like-lihood without explicitly accounting for long-horizon task success. Sequence-level reinforcement learning can address this limitation, but typically requires policy rollouts and closed-loop interaction, which are costly for real-robot ma-nipulation. We introduce DriftOPD , a teacher-free, rollout-free framework for sequence-level on-policy distillation of continuous VLA action experts. We show that the sequence-level reverse Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence decomposes into a chunk-level reverse-KL term and a future-potential term that captures the long-horizon effect of the current action. DriftOPD optimizes these two terms using a one-step drifting objective and a Q-function critic learned from offline demonstrations, respectively, enabling sequence-level optimization with only of-fline data and one-step action generation. Across multiple VLA architectures in simulation and real-world manipulation, DriftOPD generally outperforms existing one-step distillation baselines while achieving task success performance compa-rable to multi-step teacher policies. These results demonstrate that long-horizon behavior can be effectively distilled into one-step VLA action experts without on-line interaction or a separate teacher. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have recently emerged as a promising paradigm for general-purpose robotic manipulation (Zitkovich et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Black et al., 2025a). As a robot manipulation system, a VLA leverages the reasoning and planning knowledge of Vision-Language Models (VLMs), corresponding to System 2, together with the intuitive behavior gen-eration of a flow-based action expert, corresponding to System 1 (Black et al., 2025b; Shi et al., 2025). This VLA architecture is designed to reflect the state (e.g., environment and proprioception) that change according to actions, and VLAs perform tasks through closed-loop execution (Brohan et al., 2023; Bjorck et al., 2025). Since replanning every low-level action at each control step is computationally inefficient, VLAs commonly adopt a receding-horizon control scheme, repeatedly predicting short action chunks from updated observations (Zhao et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2025). Action chunks provide a convenient common training unit for action experts across multiple embod-iments (Black et al., 2025a; Bjorck et al., 2025). However, under this chunk-level training paradigm, VLAs suffer from two problems (Figure 1a). First, because VLAs are trained on demonstration states at train time, these VLAs suffer from a test-time distribution mismatch and fail to generalize to model-generated states (Ross et al., 2011; Mehta et al., 2025). Second, within an action sequence composed of action chunks, the policy na¨ ıvely generates only locally likely action chunks at each step without considering the final task success (Zhang et al., 2025). On the other hand, while re-cent reinforcement learning methods improve VLA training using various forms of sequence-level 1arXiv:2610.00317v1 [cs.RO] 29 Sep 2026 Preprint (a) Chunk-level VLA training Execution Demonstration (b) Sequence-level VLA training (Ours) Execution Demonstration Success

They must execute the current policy, evaluate the resulting sequences, and often recollect new rollouts as the policy changes. This dependence on closed-loop interaction is particularly costly for real-robot manipulation (Zhu et al., 2026). To this end, we explore a scalable and simple sequence-level objective function for action-expert training (Figure 1b). Currently, due to the multi-step sampling of diffusion/flow action experts, re-search on the trajectory-level reverse Kullback-Leibler divergence (KLD) for continuous generative policies has remained at the level of chunk -level ODE trajectories (Li et al., 2026b; Fang et al., 2026). In this work, we formulate sequence -level on-policy distillation (OPD) through the reverse KLD over the action sequence leading to success. Our formulated sequence-level OPD consists of a chunk-level reverse KLD term and a future-potential term for the current action chunk, similar to MiniLLM (Gu et al., 2024). However, unlike MiniLLM, the clean logits of continuous action chunks required for the future-potential term remain intractable. Therefore, the sequence-level OPD formulation itself still relies on rollout data, is bounded by the performance of the teacher model, and incurs multi-step sampling latency. To address these challenges, we propose DriftOPD , a teacher-free sequence-level OPD method for one-step distillation. DriftOPD calculates the chunk-level reverse KLD term as a one-step distribution-matching drifting objective (Deng et al., 2026), while estimating the future-potential term with a critic-based neural network. In this way, DriftOPD achieves one-step inference while learning directly from human demonstrations, without a separate teacher model. In practice, we adapt VGAS (Xu et al., 2026), which provides a demonstration-trained Q-function critic, to achieve fully rollout-free sequence-level OPD. Consequently, because DriftOPD uses only demonstration data, it can be readily applied across multiple embodiments, while its generated-sample-based sequence-level OPD avoids the test-time mismatch. Moreover, one-step policy generation through the drifting objective reduces robot manipulation latency. We apply DriftOPD to a diverse set of VLA models spanning different architectures, fine-tuning strategies, and action-expert parameterizations (Black et al., 2025a; Bjorck et al., 2025; Shukor et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026a). We distill their multi-step sampling capability into one-step policy generation at the action-sequence level. Our experiments cover simulation and real-world execution across a wide range of robot manipulation tasks (Nasiriany et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026a; Liu et al., 2023a; Fei et al., 2025). Experiments show that DriftOPD outperforms existing one-step distillation methods for the VLA action expert, achieving task success rates comparable to those of multi-step teachers. Further analysis shows that DriftOPD provides more stable training in data-scarce VLA settings, remains effective across diverse VLA configurations (e.g., architecture, fine-tuning strategy, and parameterization), and enables faster execution through one-step policy generation, with negligible loss of action diversity. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We formulate sequence-level on-policy-distillation for VLAs by minimizing the reverse KLD over action-sequence distributions, decomposing it into a local action-chunk reverse-KL term and a future-potential term. • We propose DriftOPD to make sequence-level OPD tractable for continuous VLA action experts. It realizes the local reverse KLD term with a one-step drifting objective and approximates the 2Preprint future-potential term with a demonstration-trained Q-function critic, enabling teacher-free and rollout-free one-step distillation. • Across diverse VLA configurations in simulation and real-world manipulation, DriftOPD gener-ally outperforms existing one-step distillation baselines while achieving task success comparable to multi-step policies. It further provides stable training in data-scarce VLA settings and faster execution than multi-step policy generation. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 VISION -L ANGUAGE -A CTION (VLA) M ODELS 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have emerged as general-purpose robot policies that cou-ple vision-language representations with imitation learning from large-scale robot demonstra-tions (Zitkovich et al., 2023; Black et al., 2025b; Bjorck et al., 2025). Early models such as RT-1 (Brohan et al., 2023) and RT-2 (Zitkovich et al., 2023) established scalable language-conditioned robot policies. Recent models such as π0 series (Black et al., 2025a;b) use continuous action experts for diverse robot tasks. In parallel, ACT (Zhao et al., 2023) and Diffusion Policy (Chi et al., 2023) demonstrated the effectiveness of predicting short action sequences (i.e., chunk) rather than individ-ual actions. Action chunking has since become a common training and execution unit in continuous-control VLAs (Black et al., 2025b; Kim et al., 2025; 2026), and is adopted by recent models such as 

π0 series (Black et al., 2025a;b), RLDX (Kim et al., 2026), ABot (Yang et al., 2026a), and GR00T series (Bjorck et al., 2025). However, behavior-cloned policies remain vulnerable to test-time dis-tribution shift, where the pretrained model fail to generalize to model- generated state at the test time (Ross et al., 2011; Mehta et al., 2025). Moreover, chunk-level imitation objectives provide no direct supervision for final task success (Zhang et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2026). Recent reinforce-ment learning methods address these limitations through sequence-level feedback (Lu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026a; 2025). However, obtaining such feedback from physical rollouts requires costly closed-loop interaction on real robots (Zhu et al., 2026). 2.2 ONE -S TEP DISTILLATION OF DIFFUSION /F LOW MODELS 

Diffusion and flow-based models (Song & Ermon, 2019; Song et al., 2021b; Liu et al., 2023b) have been widely adopted as action experts in VLA models (Black et al., 2025a; Bjorck et al., 2025), but their iterative sampling incurs substantial inference latency (Song et al., 2021a; Lu et al., 2022). To reduce the number of sampling steps, prior work has explored progressive and consistency dis-tillation (Salimans & Ho, 2022; Song et al., 2023; Lu & Song, 2025), distribution matching (Yin et al., 2024), and more recently native one-step formulations such as consistency objective (Song et al., 2023; Song & Dhariwal, 2024; Lu & Song, 2025), MeanFlow (Geng et al., 2025; 2026), and distribution drifting (Deng et al., 2026). Similar ideas have been introduced for robotic policies, including consistency- and distribution-matching-based policy distillation (Prasad et al., 2024; Jia et al., 2024). For VLA models, Li et al. (2026c) employ self-distillation for one-step action gener-ation, while Chen et al. (2026b) and Gao et al. (2026) directly introduce native one-step generation objectives into VLA action experts. Despite their efficiency, existing approaches mainly optimize action-chunk-level ODE-trajectory or distribution matching, without directly distilling supervision from task success, which can leave a gap between chunk-level training objectives and closed-loop test-time behavior. 2.3 ON-P OLICY DISTILLATION 

Prevalent action-chunking VLAs can be viewed as autoregressive (AR) sequence models at the chunk level, where each action chunk is conditioned on states induced by previously executed chunks. This perspective naturally connects VLAs to knowledge distillation in large language mod-els (LLMs), where recent work minimizes the divergence between teacher and student distribu-tions (Gu et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2024). In particular, a common formulation of on-policy dis-tillation (OPD) minimizes the sequence-level reverse Kullback-Leibler divergence (KLD), thereby evaluating the student and teacher distributions at student-visited states (Gu et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2026b). This reverse KLD formulation alleviates the train-test distribution mismatch of supervised fine-tuning by training on student-generated sequences (Gu et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2024). Such 3Preprint on-policy supervision has been particularly effective for long-horizon generation and reasoning tasks, including mathematical reasoning (Zhao et al., 2026b;c). Recent works extend this principle to continuous generative models, where DiffusionOPD (Li et al., 2026b) and FlowOPD (Fang et al., 2026) distill teacher knowledge along student-generated ODE 

trajectories from noise to data. However, these ODE trajectory-level OPD still distills knowledge only within each action chunk. It does not explicitly capture the sequence of executed chunks that de-termines final task success. Moreover, flow-based action experts do not directly provide clean-action likelihoods πθ (· | st) (Lipman et al., 2023), making sequence-level OPD over action chunks diffi-cult to apply directly. VLA-OPD (Zhong et al., 2026) instead derives dense teacher-based distillation rewards on student rollouts, but requires closed-loop environment interaction and repeated teacher queries. This work therefore aims to overcome these challenges, thereby enabling task-success sig-nals to be incorporated across action-chunk sequences.

## 5 EXPERIMENT 

We experimentally evaluate and analyze the effectiveness of DriftOPD on robot manipulation tasks. We use the π0.5 (Black et al., 2025a), GR00T N1.5/N1.6 (Bjorck et al., 2025), and ABot-M0 (Yang et al., 2026a) vision language action models, covering fully finetuned models, LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) finetuned models, models finetuned on selected VLM layers, and a JiT (Li & He, 2026)-style model that directly predicts clean actions. For the given embodiment-specific finetuned models, we further train each VLA using the DriftOPD objective (Eq. (26)) for only 10k steps. For the action-value critic Qϕ(st, a t) in Section 4.3, we adapt VGAS (Xu et al., 2026), a Q-function trained solely on demonstration data to avoid requiring rollout data for critic training. To compare one-step distillation in terms of performance, stability, and applicability, we use the multi-step teacher, its one-step prediction, and existing one-step distillation methods as baselines, including sCD (Lu & Song, 2025), MFD (Zhao et al., 2026a), and Drift (Zhang et al., 2026). 5.1 SIMULATION EXPERIMENTS

Effect of critic guidance. To assess whether the critic provides a meaningful success-aware signal and its sensitivity to critic weighting, we analyze trajectory-level critic values and sweep λ. ## 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduce DriftOPD, a sequence-level one-step distillation framework for VLAs. Starting from the reverse KL divergence over action-sequence distributions, we decompose sequence-level OPD into a local action-distribution term and a future-sequence term. DriftOPD realizes the local term with a single-positive drifting objective and approximates the future gradient contribution with a demonstration-trained critic, enabling rollout-free one-step policy optimization from offline demon-strations. Across diverse VLA configurations in simulation and real-world manipulation, DriftOPD generally outperforms existing one-step distillation baselines while achieving performance compa-rable to multi-step policies. Our results further suggest that limited embodiment-specific data can exacerbate low-noise estimation, while the drifting objective provides stable distribution matching and the critic supplies effective sequence-level guidance. Overall, DriftOPD provides an efficient and sequence-aware approach to one-step VLA distillation without online rollouts or a separate multi-step teacher. Further discussion is provided in Section E. Overview. DriftOPD queries the critic for an action gradient rather than an absolute value, so the critic must vary informatively with the action chunk in a neighborhood of demonstrated behavior. Drift. Drift is the drifting distillation objective of Zhang et al. (2026), applied to action chunks. The feature over which the kernel acts is the flattened action chunk, and one drift problem is instantiated per task in the micro-batch. Task success rate is the primary metric for both simulation and real-world experiments. Since our formulation uses one positive per context, we test directly whether it suffers from that degeneracy. Figure 10 measures seed-to-seed diversity rather than success, because the failure mode in question is a policy that ignores its input noise. We draw 18 tasks at random and 16 observations from each, and at every one of those 288 observations each student generates 32 action chunks that differ only 43 Preprint in the sampled noise z ∼ N (0 , I ). Drift + MSE and MSE only denote a plain sum of the drifting ob-jective and an MSE term, and distillation with the MSE term alone; both follow the training protocol of Table 6. The MSE-only student collapses to an almost deterministic map: its spread is 0.016 , an order of magnitude below Drift ( 0.120 ) and DriftOPD ( 0.117 ), so it discards the initial noise entirely. Success rate alone would not have exposed this, as it degrades far less than the diversity does. Drawing multiple independent noise samples per observation during training did not change the measured diversity, so the collapse is not simply a matter of how many samples each context sees. We instead attribute the gap to three ingredients that our field inherits from Deng et al. (2026) and from the distillation setting itself: the self-masking heuristic, which excludes each generated sample from its own kernel sum; initialization from pretrained weights, which places the student in a region where the teacher’s noise dependence already exists; and the multi-bandwidth kernel, which keeps a repulsive component alive at scales where a single bandwidth would have saturated. ## E LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

Our current instantiation uses a critic trained only from demonstrations, which allows DriftOPD to remain fully rollout-free. While an ideal critic can exactly provide the future-gradient contribution of the sequence-level reverse KLD objective, the practical critic provides an offline approximation whose action-gradient quality is limited by demonstration coverage. This limitation is particularly evident when the one-step teacher already matches its multi-step counterpart, as in several LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus settings (Tables 23 and 24), where the current critic provides little additional im-provement beyond the teacher/student baseline. Importantly, this behavior reflects the limitation of the learned critic rather than an intrinsic ceiling of the sequence-level OPD formulation. A stronger critic with broader coverage could provide more informative guidance toward action sequences that improve beyond the multi-step policy, motivating future work on Q-functions that more accurately capture how the current action chunk influences subsequent task progress. Finally, although we study continuous action-chunk VLAs, the proposed sequence-level OPD for-mulation is not restricted to this setting. For autoregressive VLAs that predict discrete actions or action tokens, step-wise conditional probabilities are directly available, making the local reverse-KL term and its future contribution potentially more direct to instantiate than for continuous flow-based action experts. Extending sequence-level OPD to such next-action prediction models is therefore a natural direction for future work. 45
