## ABSTRACT 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models provide strong behavioral priors for robotic manipulation, yet efficiently adapting them to downstream tasks remains challenging. Recent work addresses this challenge by adapting frozen VLAs through online reinforcement learning (RL), whose sample efficiency depends on the quality of the state representation used by the actor and critic. Existing methods construct such representations either with VLA-independent visual en-coders or through fixed compression of internal VLA representations. Neither de-sign explicitly extracts the task-specific action-relevant VLA features most useful for downstream action refinement and action-value estimation, therefore limiting sample efficiency. To address this limitation, we introduce eRLT, which constructs an effective state representation by routing task-specific action-relevant informa-tion across both tokens and layers of the frozen VLA. Specifically, learned routing tokens dynamically aggregate visual-language features at multiple depths, while a lightweight layer router combines these summaries into a fixed-dimensional RL token. The routing module is initialized using expert demonstrations to cap-ture features predictive of expert actions and then refined using critic feedback from online interactions for action-value estimation. Across seven LIBERO and RoboTwin tasks, eRLT improves mean normalized learning-curve AUC by up to 

23 .7% over representative baselines. Real-robot experiments on USB connec-tor insertion and motherboard ribbon-cable insertion further show AUC improve-ments of 108 .9% and 46 .7% , respectively, over the strongest baseline. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models inherit broad visual and semantic knowledge from internet-scale pretraining and learn generalist control from diverse robot data, enabling generalization across tasks and environments (Kim et al., 2025; Black et al., 2026). However, achieving high reliabil-ity on a specific downstream task often requires further adaptation. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is effective, but heavily relies on collecting additional high-quality task-specific demonstrations, whose coverage of states encountered during deployment is inherently limited. Online reinforce-ment learning (RL) provides a complementary approach by collecting experience from the policy’s own deployment distribution and learning from both successful and failed interactions (Physical In-telligence et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026; Wagenmaker et al., 2025; Xiao et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2026; Gao et al., 2026). In this work, we focus on lightweight online RL methods that keep the pretrained VLA frozen and adapt its behavior through a small actor-critic. The success of lightweight online RL depends critically on the state representation supplied to the actor and critic. A VLA trained for generalist control encodes rich information about visual obser-vations, language instructions, robot states, and actions. 2.1 LIGHTWEIGHT ONLINE REINFORCEMENT LEARNING FOR VLA M ODELS 

Lightweight online RL adapts a pretrained policy through small trainable modules while pre-serving its behavioral prior. Early residual RL learns corrections for conventional controllers (Johannink et al., 2018; Silver et al., 2018); recent VLA methods instead keep the pretrained pol-icy frozen and train a lightweight actor to correct its actions or steer its generative process (Xiao et al., 2025; Wagenmaker et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025b; Xu et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026; Gao et al., 2026; Kowshik et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2026). Some further incorporate human guidance or im-proved initialization (Lu et al., 2026; Tang et al., 2026). eRLT adopts this frozen-VLA actor-critic framework and studies how to construct the state representation supplied to the actor and critic from the VLA’s internal representations. Within this framework, the actor and critic must infer which decisions lead to higher returns from an effective state representation, making the task-specific action-relevant information retained in this representation central to sample-efficient learning. Existing methods construct the state represen-tation either with VLA-independent visual encoders or by compressing a predetermined layer and token set. DSRL and PLD use the former design (Wagenmaker et al., 2025; Xiao et al., 2025); Mi-DAS mean-pools internal VLM tokens, while RLT compresses final-layer tokens using reconstruc-tion (Kowshik et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026). The former constructs state representations separately from those already available in the VLA, while the latter uses only prescribed layers and tokens. Both designs therefore lack a mechanism for selecting the internal VLA information most relevant to task-specific action refinement and action-value estimation, limiting sample efficiency under con-strained online interaction budgets. eRLT instead learns to aggregate task-specific action-relevant information across VLA tokens and layers into an effective RL token. 2.2 INTERNAL VLA R EPRESENTATIONS AND FEATURE AGGREGATION 

Frozen VLA representations encode object relations, robot and action states, task progress, failures, transitions, and future success (Lu et al., 2025; Gu et al., 2025; Bhardwaj et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026; Molinari et al., 2025). Intervention studies further show that motion- and action-related fea-tures can influence robot behavior (H¨ aon et al., 2025; Buurmeijer et al., 2026; Swann et al., 2026; Grant et al., 2026). These signals are distributed: different layers provide complementary represen-tations (Wang et al., 2026; Yao et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2026), while selective token aggregation can improve action generation (Xiao et al., 2026; Ji et al., 2026). These findings support eRLT’s use of frozen internal VLA representations as the source of the RL token supplied to the actor and critic. Feature aggregation methods extract such distributed information either by compressing tokens within a layer or by combining representations across layers. Learned tokens and queries com-press visual or multimodal features within a layer (Ryoo et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023; 2025a), and recent VLA methods use related mechanisms for action generation (Xiao et al., 2026; Ji et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026). Other methods combine hidden representations across depths (Yao et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026). These representations target visual-language understanding or VLA action generation; eRLT adapts both forms of aggregation to construct a fixed-dimensional RL token that retains task-specific action-relevant information for the actor and critic. 

## 3 PRELIMINARIES AND PROBLEM FORMULATION 

3.1 LIGHTWEIGHT ONLINE RL WITH A FROZEN VLA At each policy step t, the robot receives an observation ot = ( It, x t, c ), where It denotes one or more camera views, xt is the robot proprioceptive state, and c is the language instruction. A pretrained VLA provides a strong behavioral prior for generating task-relevant robot actions. Lightweight online RL keeps the VLA frozen as the action prior and generator and improves its behavior by training only lightweight modules around it, such as an actor and critic. The frozen VLA and the trainable actor jointly produce an executable action chunk from the current observation and the 3Figure 2: Layer and token choices affect online RL. Different lightweight RL algorithms may combine the actor and VLA in different ways; their concrete implementations are specified in the experimental setup. Online training alternates between environment interaction and actor-critic updates. After executing an action chunk, the robot receives a reward Rt, the next observation ot+1 , and a termination indi-cator dt. The interaction is stored in a replay buffer and repeatedly reused for off-policy learning (Haarnoja et al., 2019). The critic learns which decisions of the lightweight actor lead to high long-term return, and the actor is updated to favor decisions assigned higher value by the critic. Thus, the lightweight modules improve from both successful and failed trials, while the pretrained VLA remains unchanged throughout online learning. The generic interaction and learning loop is shown in Appendix A. 3.2 RL S TATE REPRESENTATION 

The formulation above leaves the construction of the lightweight controller to the chosen online RL algorithm. The component shared across these algorithms, and the focus of this work, is the state representation supplied to the actor and critic. Our goal is to construct an effective state represen-tation from the frozen VLA’s multi-layer token representations while preserving the task-specific action-relevant information needed for action refinement and long-horizon action-value estimation. We denote the resulting representation by zt, which summarizes information distributed across to-kens and layers. The actor and critic combine zt with proprioception through separate adapters to form their RL states. Within each experimental setting, we keep the frozen VLA, online RL algo-rithm, and action-generation procedure fixed, and vary only how the RL token is constructed and trained. Section 4 introduces the proposed task-specific action-relevant multi-layer token routing method. 

## 4 METHOD 

4.1 MOTIVATING STUDY : L AYER AND TOKEN CHOICES 

We first examine how the VLA layers and tokens used to construct the RL token affect online RL. We use RL Token (RLT) (Xu et al., 2026) as a controlled baseline on two LIBERO tasks (Liu et al., 2023), keeping the frozen VLA, actor-critic architecture, online RL procedure, and interaction budget fixed. We vary only the VLA features supplied to RLT: the source layer with all tokens retained, or the token subset with the final layer retained. We report mean normalized success-rate AUC over three seeds; additional details are provided in Appendix C.5. Figure 2 shows that online RL is sensitive to both choices. The best source layer differs across tasks, improving over the final layer by 2.8 and 2.5 percentage points on Drawer Opening and Bowl-to-Plate, respectively. Selected token subsets also outperform all tokens by 3.9 and 5.8 points, with a different subset performing best on each task. Motivated by this study, eRLT uses learned routing tokens to aggregate multimodal VLA features at m selected VLM layers ℓ1, . . . , ℓ m and a lightweight layer router to combine them into the RL token zt. Token summaries depend on the current input, whereas layer weights are shared within each adaptation task. The routing parameters η comprise the routing-token embeddings ηtok and layer logits ηlay .What the RL token retains is determined by two sources of supervision. Before online interaction, expert action prediction teaches it to preserve differences needed for action refinement. During on-line RL, successful and failed transitions reveal which differences affect task progress and outcomes; the critic objective adapts the routing parameters accordingly for action-value estimation. The first stage provides an action-predictive starting point from existing demonstrations, while the second adapts it to situations encountered by the current policy. The VLA remains frozen throughout both stages. Figure 3 summarizes the routing module and both training stages. 4.3 TASK -S PECIFIC ACTION -R ELEVANT MULTI -L AYER TOKEN ROUTING

## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

We design our experiments to answer three questions: (1) Does eRLT improve the sample efficiency of lightweight online VLA adaptation across different manipulation settings? (2) How do its routing components and two-stage training strategy contribute to the improvement? (3) Does the resulting RL token support efficient adaptation on real-world precision manipulation tasks? 65.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Simulation benchmarks. We evaluate eRLT on LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023) and RoboTwin (Chen et al., 2025), which feature diverse object layouts, language instructions, and manipulation scenar-ios. The four LIBERO tasks are Drawer Opening, Bowl-to-Plate, Cabinet-Bowl-to-Plate, and Two-Moka-Pots, and the three RoboTwin tasks are Adjust Bottle, Move Can–Pot, and Place Container Plate. Together, they cover spatial reasoning, object relocation, and multi-stage manipulation. 

Real-world tasks. We further evaluate eRLT on two precision manipulation tasks: USB connector insertion and motherboard ribbon-cable insertion. Experiments use the left arm of an RB-Y1 robot and two wrist-mounted cameras. Both tasks require precise visual alignment followed by contact-rich insertion. Starting from the official π0.5 base checkpoint, the task-specific VLAs are fine-tuned on 99 USB and 800 ribbon-cable teleoperated demonstrations, respectively. To vary the initial end-effector pose, each reset independently perturbs the seven left-arm joint targets with zero-mean Gaussian noise of standard deviation 0.01 rad per joint. Online RL refines the VLA reference be-havior in adapted episodes. Every method uses 80 trajectories on USB insertion and 90 trajectories on ribbon-cable insertion under the same human-in-the-loop protocol. 

Compared methods. We compare three state representations while keeping the frozen VLA and online RL procedure fixed within each setting. DSRL ∗ uses a VLA-independent visual encoder (Wagenmaker et al., 2025). RLT ∗ compresses prescribed final-layer VLA tokens into an RL token through reconstruction and keeps the RL token fixed during online RL (Xu et al., 2026). eRLT 

instead constructs the proposed task-specific action-relevant RL token through multi-layer token routing. The superscript ∗ denotes use of only the corresponding state representation in our shared online RL framework. We also report the Frozen VLA before adaptation. 

Evaluation protocol and metrics. Our primary measure of online sample efficiency is the nor-malized area under the success-rate learning curve (AUC), computed by trapezoidal integration and normalized by the evaluation horizon. It reflects average success over a fixed interaction budget, re-warding methods that reach high success rates earlier and sustain them. We omit final success rates in simulation because the methods eventually reach similar levels. For the real-world experiments, we report both normalized AUC and success rate in the final 10-episode window; the Frozen VLA reference is evaluated over 10 trials. 

Implementation details. Simulation uses a DSRL-style off-policy actor-critic that steers the frozen VLA in its latent action space (Wagenmaker et al., 2025), whereas the real-world study uses the RLT pipeline (Xu et al., 2026). Within each setting, all variants share the VLA checkpoint, actor and critic architectures, replay data, reward, update schedule, and interaction budget; only the state represen-tation varies. RLT and eRLT reuse the task-specific SFT demonstrations for offline initialization, and eRLT uses one routing token unless otherwise stated. Appendix C provides task visualizations and complete implementation details, including the real-world hardware and collection protocol. 5.2 MAIN SIMULATION RESULTS

Overall, eRLT achieves the strongest aggregate performance across both benchmarks, outperforming RLT ∗ on every task and DSRL ∗ on six of seven. Because all variants share the same VLA and online RL procedure, these gains isolate the effect of the state representation and show that a task-specific action-relevant RL token constructed from internal VLA features improves sample efficiency across environments and robot embodiments. On LIBERO, eRLT achieves the best result on all four tasks: it reaches high success rates earlier on Drawer Opening and Bowl-to-Plate and makes greater progress on the more challenging Cabinet-Bowl-to-Plate task. On RoboTwin, the gains on Adjust Bottle and Place Container Plate demonstrate that the learned RL token remains effective across different object interactions and multi-stage ma-nipulation. DSRL ∗ performs better on Move Can–Pot, although eRLT remains stronger than RLT ∗,indicating that the relative benefit of a state representation can still be task dependent. We conduct ablations on the Bowl-to-Plate task and report normalized learning-curve AUC. Table 2 removes each routing or training component under the same VLA and actor-critic setup: token-wise routing is replaced by uniform token pooling, layer-wise routing by the final VLA layer, offline initialization by a randomly initialized routing module, and online adaptation by a frozen initialized routing module. Table 3 varies the number of routing tokens while keeping the output dimension fixed. Removing any component reduces AUC, with the largest degradation observed without token-wise routing. This suggests that adaptively extracting task-specific action-relevant cues is more effective than uniformly pooling the visual-language prefix. Offline initialization is the next most influential component, indicating that an action-predictive starting point reduces the burden on the critic, while layer-wise routing and online adaptation provide complementary refinement. Increasing the number of routing tokens does not improve performance monotonically. ## 6 CONCLUSION AND LIMITATIONS 

We presented eRLT, which constructs a task-specific RL token for lightweight online RL with a frozen VLA. eRLT uses learned routing tokens to aggregate task-specific action-relevant information across prefix tokens and a layer router to combine the resulting information across VLA depths. The routing module is initialized through expert action prediction and refined by the critic’s Bellman objective. Across LIBERO and RoboTwin, eRLT outperforms the reconstruction-based RLT token on all seven tasks and the independent DSRL encoder on six of seven tasks in normalized learning-curve AUC, indicating improved sample efficiency. Ablations further show that token routing, layer 9routing, offline initialization, and online adaptation make complementary contributions. Results on two real-world insertion tasks demonstrate improved sample efficiency and, on USB connector insertion, a higher final-window success rate under a limited physical-interaction budget. eRLT is limited by the information encoded in the frozen VLA, and its multi-layer extraction and periodic RL token recomputation add computation and memory relative to a fixed final-layer RL token. The real-world evaluation covers only two insertion tasks on one robot under a human-in-the-loop protocol; run-to-run variation and sensitivity to intervention frequency therefore remain insufficiently characterized. Future work should study router transfer across tasks and embodiments, additional backbones and robots, and longer-horizon tasks.

B.1 ROUTING ARCHITECTURE 

Routing path. The routing tokens are used only to construct the RL token and do not affect VLA action generation. We append them after the multimodal prefix and process the resulting sequence with the frozen VLM in a separate forward pass. Each routing token can attend to the original visual-language tokens, while the original tokens cannot attend to the appended routing tokens. We collect the routing-token hidden states at all selected layers from this same forward pass. For K

routing tokens and m selected layers, mean pooling over the K routing-token positions produces m

summaries in RD , and the layer router combines them into one RL token zt ∈ RD . The routing module adds only KD + m effective trainable parameters: KD routing-token parameters and m

scalar layer logits. 

Token layout and tensor shapes. For the π0.5 backbone used in this work, the prefix tensor con-tains three image slots with 256 tokens per slot followed by 200 language-token positions, giving 968 positions in total, with hidden width D = 2048 . The attention mask determines which positions are valid according to the available camera views and language input. Appending K routing tokens produces a sequence with 968 + K positions. During offline initialization, the action-prediction loss updates the routing tokens, layer logits, and temporary action probe. The frozen VLA is traversed by the gradient only to update the appended routing tokens; none of its parameters change. Sample demonstration frames and construct the following H-step expert action chunk, re-peating the final action only when a chunk reaches an episode boundary. Apply the same action normalization used by the frozen VLA, producing an Hd a-dimensional target for action dimension da.2. Run the frozen image and language embedding path, append the routing tokens, and collect their hidden states at the selected layers. Mean-pool the K routing tokens at each layer and combine the resulting summaries with the learned softmax layer weights. 3. Pass the RL token through the temporary action probe. Optimize the routing-token embed-dings, layer logits, and probe jointly with the action-chunk MSE in Equation 3; all VLA parameters remain frozen. 4. Evaluate action MSE periodically, retain the routing parameters with the lowest validation MSE, and discard the probe before online RL. B.3 CRITIC -D RIVEN ONLINE ADAPTATION 

During online learning, the critic loss updates the critic and routing parameters, while the actor uses detached RL tokens. The critic parameters remain unchanged during an actor update, although the derivative of its output with respect to the actor decision is retained for optimizing the actor. The Bellman target is computed using target copies of the actor, critic, and routing module. Because the RL token changes online, replay stores the raw current and next observations rather than precom-puted RL tokens. 

Algorithmic specification. Each learner iteration performs the following steps: 1. Sample transitions from replay and compute the target RL token for the next observation under no gradient. Use the target actor and critic to construct the controller-specific Bell-man target. 2. Update the critic using the RL tokens and update the actor using detached RL tokens. Keep the routing parameters fixed on ordinary learner iterations. 3. Every U learner iterations, recompute the current RL token with gradients enabled and jointly update the critic, routing-token embeddings, and layer logits from Equation 4. 4. Polyak-update the target actor, critic, and routing module toward their online counterparts.

C.3 REAL -W ORLD TASKS AND HARDWARE 

Tasks and hardware. We evaluate USB connector insertion and motherboard ribbon-cable in-sertion using the left arm of an RB-Y1 robot and two wrist-mounted Intel RealSense D405 RGB cameras operating at 30 Hz. Each episode begins with the connector already grasped. Both scenes use fixed tabletop targets: the USB receptacle is mounted upright on a power-strip fixture, while the motherboard is secured flat on the work surface. The deployment stack communicates with the robot through ROS 2 and runs the frozen VLA, routing module, actor, and critic on a workstation with a 24-core Intel Core Ultra 9 285K processor, 64 GB memory, and one NVIDIA RTX PRO 5000 Blackwell GPU. The two wrist views are resized to 224 × 224 before entering the VLA; the head-camera slot is masked for both task-specific checkpoints. A rollout typically lasts approximately 20 s and can extend to 30 s when human assistance is required. At reset, each of the seven nominal left-arm joint targets is independently perturbed by Gaussian noise with zero mean and standard deviation 0.01 rad. This joint-space perturbation induces varia-tion in both the position and orientation of the initial end-effector pose; the torso and uncontrolled right arm are not perturbed. 

Success and reward. Outcomes are judged manually. USB success requires the plug to be in-serted into the USB receptacle, and ribbon-cable success requires the cable to be inserted into the motherboard connector; an attempt that does not achieve the corresponding insertion is a failure. Because the launch configuration imposes no automatic wall-clock limit, the operator declares a timeout when a rollout exceeds approximately 1.5× the usual task-completion time. The operator closes each episode with one of three labels: success, failure, or truncation. The corresponding re-play trajectory receives a sparse terminal reward of one only for success and zero otherwise; success and failure are terminal, while truncation is recorded separately. 17 C.4 REAL -W ORLD IMPLEMENTATION AND TRAINING PROTOCOL 

In the real-world experiments, we adopt the RLT online RL pipeline and vary only the state repre-sentation. Our RLT implementation is built on the open-source RLinf project. All adapted policies use the same task-specific VLA checkpoint, observations, actor and critic architectures, replay data, reward definition, update schedule, and interaction budget. We compare DSRL ∗, RLT, and eRLT; here RLT denotes the condition using the original RL token in the RLT pipeline, while the Frozen VLA before online adaptation is reported as a reference. 

Frozen VLA and action parameterization. The two deployed task-specific π0.5 checkpoints are initialized from the official π0.5 base weights. Their SFT datasets contain 99 complete-task USB demonstrations and 800 complete-task ribbon-cable demonstrations, respectively; we do not other-wise tune the VLA during online RL. The control interface is shared by the two tasks. At each query, the frozen VLA predicts a 30-step chunk and the online policy executes the first 15 steps synchronously at 30 Hz. Both the reference and learned policies use a seven-dimensional left-arm end-effector layout (x, y, z, r x, r y , r z , g ), with Euler angles for orientation. The lightweight actor predicts normalized state-relative pose deltas: values in [−1, 1] correspond to at most 0.01 m of translation and 0.03 rad of rotation per step. Each predicted pose is decoded relative to the current proprioceptive state and converted to an absolute robot command. The gripper channel is not learned and is fixed to the closed command g = 0 ;the deployment adapter additionally applies a 3 mm closing offset to reference gripper commands. Action prefetching and temporal smoothing are disabled, so the next observation is recorded only after the current 15-step chunk boundary. 

State representations and offline initialization. For each task, RLT and eRLT are initialized separately using demonstrations from the corresponding SFT dataset. The USB initializer uses all 99 demonstrations, while the ribbon-cable initializer uses a 195-episode subset. Online RL architecture and objectives. The actor concatenates the first 15 reference actions, the 2048-dimensional RL token, and the 7-dimensional proprioceptive state. A three-layer MLP with widths (256 , 256 , 256) outputs a 15 × 7 normalized action chunk. During collection it uses a fixed Gaussian standard deviation of 0.02 . The critic is a twin-Q ensemble; each Q-function uses three 256-wide hidden layers and receives the RL token, proprioception, and candidate action chunk. The entropy coefficient is zero. For the first 100 learner updates, the actor uses only the behavior-cloning objective with weight 5. The Q-objective weight then increases linearly from 0 to 0.1 over the following 100 updates, while the behavior-cloning weight remains 5. During training, the refer-ence chunk is independently dropped for 30% of samples; rollout inference always receives it. 

Online training and data collection. The replay cache and sampling window retain the most recent 200 trajectories. Learning begins once the replay buffer contains 100 action-chunk transitions, at which point the learner performs an initial block of 150 updates. Each newly collected episode then schedules 16 additional updates, with at most 25 executed in one training call. The critic is updated on every learner step and the actor on every second step. The global batch and micro-batch 18 sizes are both 64. The discount factor is 0.993 , and the Polyak coefficient is 0.005 . The actor and critic use constant learning rates of 3 × 10 −4 with gradient clipping at 10. Each run uses a fixed budget of 80 trajectories for USB or 90 trajectories for ribbon cable, including approximately 15 successful human-in-the-loop warm-up trajectories on average, and takes approx-imately 20–25 minutes. After warm-up, the robot collects autonomous rollouts. Five consecutive failures trigger five additional successful episodes provided by the operator through a 3Dconnex-ion SpaceMouse, after which autonomous collection resumes. The number of assisted trajectories therefore depends on the autonomous policy’s performance. C.5 MOTIVATING -S TUDY PROTOCOL 

We use RL Token (RLT) (Xu et al., 2026) as a controlled interface for studying layer and token choices. RLT compresses a prescribed set of VLA tokens with a reconstruction objective and keeps the resulting RL token fixed during online RL. All variants use the same actor-critic architecture and online RL procedure, with one factor varied at a time: the VLA layer with all tokens retained, or the token subset with the final layer retained. We report mean normalized evaluation-success AUC over three seeds. In Figure 2, gray marks the Final/All baselines, while outlines mark each task’s best mean and parentheses report its gain over the corresponding baseline. For the layer study, the source is varied among hidden-state indices 3, 9, 15, and the final hidden state while retaining all 712 selected prefix positions. For the token study, the final hidden state is fixed and we compare all 712 positions against three 328-token subsets. Each subset always retains all 200 language positions and 128 image positions. The 128 positions are either uniformly spaced or selected by the action-relevance scores computed separately from the Drawer Opening or Bowl-to-Plate demonstrations. These scores use absolute gradient-times-activation of diffusion action MSE, normalized within each layer and camera view and averaged across layers; the score is averaged over diffusion times {0.2, 0.5, 0.8}.Each RLT variant uses the same two-layer reconstruction pretraining described above and remains frozen online.
