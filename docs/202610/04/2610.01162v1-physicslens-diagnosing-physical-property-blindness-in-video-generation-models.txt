## ABSTRACT 

Reliable video world models could provide scalable predictive environments for robot learning, planning, and evaluation. However, generated robot videos can violate physical principles and complete tasks through physically implausible be-havior, limiting their reliability for robot learning and planning. Current video-generation benchmarks exclude physics that are inherently hidden by visuals (e.g., weight, viscosity, friction). Due to this, video models are evaluated on the fidelity of physics, not the underlying accuracy of physics. We introduce PhysicsLENS, a dataset and benchmark for evaluating plausibility of physical properties grounded in robotics. PhysicsLENS uses matched scenario pairs that hold the same condi-tioning frame and task, while varying underlying physics in the scene description. Scenarios are curated from public robot video sources and annotated across seven physical domains: collision, gravity, momentum, friction, deformation, fluid, and causality. We evaluate across four video generation models, producing over 400 human-annotated labels. Results show that plausible-looking videos often ignore the stated property (34 of 47), and that stating the property lowers plausibility only slightly and not significantly. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Video generation models could provide scalable predictive environments for robot learning (Yang et al., 2024; NVIDIA, 2025a), planning (Du et al., 2023), and evaluation (Quevedo et al., 2025). By generating diverse interactions without repeatedly collecting real-world data, these models could ex-pose robots to rare events, unfamiliar objects, and changing physical conditions (Jang et al., 2025). However, increasing the volume of generated data is useful only if the videos reliably represent the physical consequences of actions. A video may appear realistic and depict successful task comple-tion while violating friction, momentum, object permanence, or other physical constraints. Such violations undermine the usefulness of generated videos as predictive environments, yet may go un-detected by benchmarks focused on visual realism and task success. We therefore require a bench-mark that evaluates whether generated interactions respect the physical properties and constraints that govern them. Existing video-generation benchmarks capture this reliability only partially. Most benchmarks focus on visual quality, temporal consistency, or motion plausibility. These criteria can identify obvious artifacts, but cannot determine whether a model correctly grounds the physical properties specified in its conditioning input. Properties such as weight, friction, viscosity, and deformability may not be observable from a single image. Consequently, visually identical scenes can require different outcomes: a robot should interact differently with a heavy object than with a light one, or with a slippery surface than with a rough one. A model can therefore generate a plausible video while producing behavior that is physically incorrect for the stated condition. We introduce PhysicsLENS, a benchmark for evaluating both physical plausibility and physical-property grounding in robot video generation. ## 2 RELATED WORK 

Evaluating physics in generated video. Video world models are increasingly used as simula-tors and data sources for robot learning (Yang et al., 2024; NVIDIA, 2025a; Jang et al., 2025), yet videos that look realistic can still break physics (Kang et al., 2025; Motamed et al., 2026). Dis-tributional metrics and general benchmark suites mainly measure perceptual quality (Unterthiner et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2024), although VBench-2.0 adds physics dimensions (Zheng et al., 2025). Physics benchmarks score commonsense physics in generated videos (Bansal et al., 2025; 2026; Meng et al., 2025; Gu et al., 2026), compare generations with real experiments or recorded outcomes (Zhang et al., 2025; Motamed et al., 2026), or turn measured motion into verifiable re-wards (Le et al., 2025). Physion-Eval and PQSG also localize failures, using expert reasoning traces and VLM-generated question graphs, respectively (Zhang et al., 2026; Pothiraj et al., 2026). For robotics, WorldSimBench, EWMBench, RBench, and RoboWM-Bench evaluate generated manip-ulation videos on visual quality, motion, task adherence, or whether the motion can be executed as actions (Qin et al., 2025; Yue et al., 2025; Deng et al., 2026; Jiang et al., 2026). Hidden physical properties. Inferring properties that appearance does not reveal is a classic test of physical understanding: Physion++ and ComPhy ask models to infer mass, friction, elasticity, or charge from observed interactions (Tung et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2022). PhysicsLENS reverses this flow of information: the property is stated in text, and the generator must turn it into motion. Controllable generators receive physical quantities as dedicated inputs, such as forces or mass and friction coefficients (Liu et al., 2024; Gillman et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026). T2V-CompBench tests whether prompt attributes bind to the right objects, but only for visible attributes such as color, shape, and motion direction (Sun et al., 2025). We instead test whether general-purpose image-to-video models follow a property given only in plain language, with the frame and task held fixed. 

VLM judges of physical plausibility. Automatic physics evaluation often relies on vision-language models (VLMs), either fine-tuned or prompted (Bansal et al., 2025; Meng et al., 2025; NVIDIA, 2025b), but their reliability is limited. VLMs struggle with physical-world understanding (Chow et al., 2025), most models score near chance on intuitive-physics tests (Bordes et al., 2025), and plausible verbal explanations can hide wrong estimates of velocity and acceleration (Puyin et al., 2026). Multimodal judges also show biases and inconsistent judgments (Chen et al., 2024), and video VLMs have limited fine-grained motion and temporal grounding (Hong et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). Measured evidence helps: MASS adds depth, object grounding, and motion tracking to VLM reasoning (Wu et al., 2026), and GeoPhys finds that VLM judges detect physics violations near chance while a training-free score on frozen image-encoder features does better (Intern` o et al., 2026). PhysicsLENS follows this direction by combining tracked measurements with targeted VLM questions, and we test it directly against human annotations. 

## 3 PHYSICS LENS B ENCHMARK 

3.1 OBSERVABLE AND UNOBSERVABLE CONDITIONS 

Image-conditioned video generation relies on two sources of information: an initial frame and a text prompt describing the scene and requested task. In ordinary robot videos, visual appearance is often correlated with physical properties; for example, size may correlate with mass, and visible wetness may suggest low surface friction. A model may therefore generate plausible motion from visual priors without grounding the physical properties specified in text. PhysicsLENS evaluates whether models follow text-specified properties when those properties cannot be inferred from the initial frame. We define two conditions. In the observable condition, the prompt is consistent with the physical properties suggested by the frame. In the unobservable condition, the same frame is 3Preprint. 

Figure 2: Example matched prompt pairs. Each pair shares the initial frame and task. The observable description (blue) states what the frame shows; the unobservable description (orange) replaces it with a latent property that departs from what the frame suggests. paired with a prompt that specifies a latent property that departs from those visual expectations. For example, a prompt may state that liquid inside an ordinary teapot is “thick and viscous, similar to molasses” (Figure 2). A model that relies only on appearance may instead generate water-like flow. We refer to this failure to condition on a text-specified latent property as physical property blindness .

Matched-pair construction. Each unobservable scenario is derived from an observable counter-part with the same initial frame and task instruction. Only the scene description changes: the ob-servable description is consistent with the frame, whereas the unobservable description introduces a latent property that conflicts with its visual appearance. This construction holds the visual scene, task, and frame quality fixed, making the textual property the sole intervention. Because we generate only one video per prompt and model, individual pairwise differences may reflect generation noise; we therefore analyze matched pairs in aggregate. The unobservable scenarios specify four task-relevant properties: viscosity for pouring (10 scenarios), surface condition for wiping (8), elasticity for deformable objects (7), and mass for pushing (5). Each property is expressed in plain language within the scene description provided to the video generator. The generator receives neither a refer-ence continuation nor the expected outcome. The design supports two complementary evaluations. Property grounding assesses whether the gen-erated video visibly reflects the stated latent property, using the human adherence score (Section 3.3). 

Matched-condition comparison tests whether specifying that property affects physical plausibility while holding the initial frame and task fixed—that is, whether failure to express the property co-incides with visibly less plausible physics. Together, these evaluations distinguish general visual plausibility from adherence to the specified physical condition. 3.2 SCENARIOS AND GENERATED VIDEOS 

PhysicsLENS contains 110 prompts: 80 observable scenarios and 30 matched unobservable variants. The 80 starting frames come from 11 public robot datasets that cover humanoids, dual-arm and single-arm manipulators, and mobile manipulators (Table 6). Each frame shows the scene before the robot interacts with solid objects or liquids. Appendices A and B describe frame preparation, prompt generation, and the annotation schema. We used GPT-4o to draft structured scene and action descriptions from each frame and its logged task. An author subsequently reviewed the descriptions. We generate one video per prompt using each of four models: Wan 2.2 (Team Wan et al., 2025), Cosmos 3 Nano (Agarwal et al., 2026), HunyuanVideo 1.5 (Wu et al., 2025a), and MAGI 4.5B Dis-till (Teng et al., 2025). Together, they span diverse generative paradigms, including diffusion video models, autoregressive video generation, and world/action foundation modeling. One unobservable HunyuanVideo 1.5 prompt did not produce a usable video. The realized benchmark hence con-tains 439 videos: 320 observable and 119 unobservable. HunyuanVideo 1.5 contributes 109 videos, including 29 unobservable videos, while each other model contributes 110. Since the benchmark contains one generation per prompt–model combination, the reported results characterize these gen-erated clips and do not estimate variability across generation seeds. 4Preprint. 

Figure 3: Evidence flow through the PhysicsLENS diagnostic pipeline. 3.3 HUMAN ANNOTATION 

Annotators score each video on up to three scales, which we report separately because they can disagree. Physical plausibility P ∈ { 1, 2, 3, 4} judges only the visible physics: contact, motion, deformation, and object persistence. Task completion A ∈ { 0, 1} records whether the task is done by the end, even if the physics looks wrong. Hidden-property adherence H ∈ { 1, 2, 3, 4}, rated only for unobservable videos, records whether the outcome shows the stated property. We treat P ≥ 3 as mostly or fully plausible and H ≥ 3 as mostly or fully following the property. When P < 4, annotators must describe the physics violation in the video and select one or more of eight violation labels. The labels are the seven physical domains plus contact, which annotators could select separately from collision; we merge contact into collision in all analyses. Appendix B gives the full rubric, the label definitions, and how videos were assigned to annotators. 3.4 AUTOMATED DIAGNOSTIC PIPELINE 

PhysicsLENS also includes an automated evaluator (Figure 3). Because VLM judges are unreliable on their own (Section 2), the evaluator pairs motion measurements, which record how objects and the camera move, with targeted VLM questions, which identify what went wrong. Its input is the generated video and the task. For unobservable scenarios, the hidden-property question also receives the stated property and the scenario’s expected outcome. Human labels are used only to fit how the evidence is combined, on training folds that exclude the scenario being judged. The evaluator has four stages. Screening measures motion over the first 5 seconds of the video: frame differences, optical flow, camera motion, flow-direction entropy, and how DINOv2 frame em-beddings change. It also asks a VLM a physics-error question that lists specific errors instead of asking about overall realism. Localization tracks points with camera motion removed and measures their kinematics: speed, acceleration, the time of peak acceleration, and points that disappear inside the frame rather than at its border, the sign of an object vanishing mid-video. VLM verification shows each VLM eight frames and the task and asks three questions: (i) whether the task was completed; (ii) which of eight failure descriptions (one per physical domain, plus an object appearing or vanish-ing) best describes the main physics problem, if any; and (iii) for unobservable scenarios, whether the outcome shows the stated property. Reporting merges the evidence into a per-video JSON report with each finding’s time window, object, violation type, confidence, and a short explanation. We do not evaluate time localization because our human labels are not time-dependent. ## 4 EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS 

This section presents results on the PhysicsLENS benchmark and evaluates its diagnostic pipeline through four research questions. RQ1–RQ3 are answered with human annotations, and RQ4 com-pares the pipeline against them. RQ1: Does high physical plausibility imply that a model followed a grounded specification (Sec. 4.1)? RQ2: Does stating a latent property in text change plausibility (Sec. 4.2)? RQ3: What do individual failure cases reveal about the distinction between grounding and plausibility (Sec. 4.3)? RQ4: Can the PhysicsLENS pipeline approximate human judgments (Sec. 4.4)? 4.1 RQ1: D OES HIGH PLAUSIBILITY IMPLY PROPERTY GROUNDING ?A video may look physically plausible without reflecting the physical property specified in its prompt. To test this, we collected annotations rating each video’s plausibility ( P ) and its adher-ence to the stated property ( H) on 1–4 scales (Sec. 3.3). We consider a video plausible when P ≥ 3

and property-adherent when H ≥ 3.Across the total of 119 videos with unobservable properties, human annotators noted 47 as plausible. Yet 34 of those 47 (72%) do not adhere to the stated property ( H ≤ 2). This is the physical property blindness described in Section 3.1. The pattern appears for all four generators (Table 2). MAGI has the highest mean plausibility but the lowest mean adherence (Fig. 4); it accounts for 21 of the 34 plausible but ungrounded videos. The result persists without MAGI: 13 of the remaining 26 plausible videos (50%) ignore the property. Conversely, of the 28 videos that follow the property ( H ≥ 3), 15 are rated implausible ( P ≤ 2). These videos reflect the stated property but violate other aspects of physics. Thus, plausibility and property grounding must be evaluated separately: a video can satisfy either criterion without satisfying the other. 

4.2 RQ2: D OES STATING A L ATENT PROPERTY IN TEXT CHANGE PLAUSIBILITY ?We next compare how the same physical property is presented. Each unobservable scenario has an observable counterpart with the same frame, task, and generator. Only the description differs: it either matches the frame or specifies a different latent property (e.g., molasses-like tea in an ordinary teapot). This comparison tests whether the presentation of the property changes the plausibility of the generated video. Mean plausibility is lower in the unobservable condition for all four generators, by 0.13 to 0.38 points on the 1–4 scale (Table 3). Across individual pairs, plausibility decreases in 40 cases and increases in 26. Pooling the 29 scenarios generated by all four models gives a mean decrease of 0.28 points (Fig. 5; p = 0 .085 ). Stating a property that the frame does not show lowers plausibility for all four generators, but the drop is small and not statistically significant.

Combining VLM judgments with video signals improves agreement. The full evaluator com-bines answers from an ensemble of ten VLMs with Stage-1/2 signals. It reaches an AUC of 0.72 for overall plausibility, 0.81 for task completion, 0.73 for violation detection, and 0.71 for hidden-property adherence (Table 4). It achieves the highest or tied-highest score on five of the seven measures. Adding signals to an individual VLM raises mean overall plausibility AUC from 0.53 to 0.61, violation detection from 0.58 to 0.72, task completion from 0.71 to 0.76, and property ad-herence from 0.57 to 0.70. Signals alone reach 0.70 for violation detection; one frequently selected signal tracks objects vanishing during a video. Generator identity is a strong baseline because the four generators differ substantially in quality. It slightly exceeds the full evaluator on unobservable-video plausibility (0.72 versus 0.71), but assigns the same score to every video from a given generator. Its within-generator AUC is therefore 0.5 by construction, whereas the automated evaluator reaches 0.67 for unobservable plausibility and 0.76 for task completion. Generator identity also cannot score videos from a new generator without first obtaining human ratings for that generator.

To focus their judgments on physical behavior, we instead ask about specific errors, such as objects passing through one an-other, vanishing, floating, changing shape impossibly, or moving without contact. The question also instructs the VLMs to disregard task success and the amount of motion. ## 5 CONCLUSION 

We introduced PhysicsLENS, a benchmark for testing whether generated robot videos actually re-flect the physical properties they are told to have. By keeping the initial frame and action the same and only changing how the property is described, matched scenarios let us look at property ground-ing and physical plausibility side by side. Across 439 clips from four generators, human evaluation found a clear gap between the two: 34 of 47 clips rated plausible under unobservable conditions did not follow the stated property, and 15 of 28 clips that did follow it were still physically implausible. In other words, a video can look right without actually respecting the physics it was asked to show, or get the property right while breaking something else entirely. Pairing targeted VLM questions with measured video signals meaningfully improves agreement with these human judgments, reaching an AUC of 0.72 for plausibility and 0.81 for task completion, though judging whether a specific hidden property was followed, and figuring out which physics family a violation belongs to, are both still hard problems. Taken together, these results make the case for evaluating plausibility and property grounding as two separate questions, not one. We kept this study deliberately focused on 80 initial frames and four video-generation models so we could isolate property grounding under tightly matched conditions. From here, a natural next step is testing more models, more hidden-property types, and multiple generations per prompt, which would help separate how much of the variation comes from the generation process itself versus the property being tested. 9Preprint.

Annotation procedure. Scenarios were curated from public robotics video sources rather than authored domain-first, and each scenario was subsequently annotated with its governing physical domain by manual review of its action and outcome. This annotation was performed independently of the taxonomy’s construction to avoid circularity: the seven domains were fixed in advance from the tool’s seven specialists (Section 3.4), and each scenario was assigned to the domain its physics most directly tests, rather than domains being defined post hoc to fit whatever scenarios happened to be available. 

Interaction with observability. The observable/unobservable axis introduced in §3.1 is orthog-onal to domain: a scenario’s domain label is fixed by the physical law under test, while its ob-servability condition determines whether the property governing that law is visually apparent or text-specified. Both matched members of an observable/unobservable pair therefore carry the same domain label. 

## B HUMAN ANNOTATION 

B.1 ANNOTATION INSTRUMENT 

The annotation interface presents one generated clip at a time together with the fields summarized in Table 8. Physical plausibility and action completion are collected for every clip. Hidden-property adherence is collected only for unobservable scenarios, for which the form includes a property-specific question. When a clip receives a plausibility score below 4, annotators must also describe the issue and select at least one violation category. Figure 9 shows the interface presented to annotators. 

Interpretation of the scores. The physical-plausibility score P ∈ { 1, 2, 3, 4} assesses only the visible consistency of contact, motion, deformation, and object persistence. It is a human judgment rather than a measurement against a physical simulator. Task incompletion alone does not imply physical implausibility. The action-completion score A ∈ { 0, 1} assesses the final task outcome rather than the physical correctness of the process. A clip may therefore receive A = 1 while exhibiting severe physical violations, or A = 0 despite otherwise plausible motion. The hidden-property score H ∈ { 1, 2, 3, 4} measures whether the generated behavior demonstrates the property stated in the prompt. It is not collected for observable scenarios, and missing values in those scenarios are not interpreted as zero or as failures. Importantly, a low adherence score does not distinguish between two cases: the video may visibly contradict the stated property, or it may fail to perform an interaction that would reveal the property. Because the instrument does not provide a separate “insufficient evidence” response, we retain this ambiguity when interpreting H ≤ 2.

Aggregation. We report rating distributions, descriptive means of the ordinal scores, and action-completion proportions. For thresholded summaries, P ≥ 3 denotes mostly plausible or physically realistic behavior, while H ≥ 3 denotes that the stated property is mostly or fully followed. These eight human labels are distinct from the seven physical domains used to organize the bench-mark and the tool’s specialists (Section 3.4). We preserve the human labels as recorded and merge Contact into Collision in all analyses. A blank category field indicates only that no label was recorded; it is not independent confirmation that the clip is physically correct. Some violations may reasonably receive more than one label. For example, an object passing through a supporting surface may involve both Contact and Collision, while unsupported floating may involve both Contact and Gravity. The multi-select interface preserves these overlaps rather than forcing each failure into a single category. B.3 ANNOTATOR ALLOCATION AND RELIABILITY LIMITS 

Annotations were produced by members of the research team, mainly graduate students in computer science, rather than by externally recruited participants. Clips were assigned to annotators at ran-dom. All observable clips were distributed in batches of 16–17 clips per annotator. Unobservable clips were distributed in batches of 6. Some observable Cosmos records contain a combined annotator label. Because these records con-tain only one final set of scores rather than separate ratings attributable to each individual, we treat 18 Preprint. the combined label as a single annotation source. It does not constitute overlapping independent annotation and cannot be used to estimate agreement between the named annotators. Each clip re-ceived one final annotation rather than independent ratings from multiple annotators. Consequently, the dataset does not support an estimate of inter-annotator reliability. 

## C PHYSICS LENS P IPELINE DETAILS 

This appendix describes the pipeline as it was run for Section 4.4. Where the interactive tool of Section 3.4 does more, we say so. 

Screening. Stage 1 measures motion over the first 5 s of each video, sampled at 8 frames per second in grayscale with the longer side resized to 320 pixels. It computes the mean absolute difference between consecutive frames; dense optical flow (Farneb¨ ack, 2003); camera motion, taken as the median flow vector of each frame pair; object motion, the flow left after subtracting camera motion; and the entropy of object-motion directions over the fastest-moving 10% of pixels. It also embeds the eight VLM frames (below) with DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024) and measures how the embedding moves: the step size between frames, how that step size changes, whether successive steps keep their direction, and net displacement relative to path length. Each measurement is reduced to one number per video (a mean, 95th percentile, maximum, or ratio), giving 22 screening signals. No signal is thresholded into a flag. Signals become scores only when evidence is combined, by ranking videos against each other (below). Stage 1 also asks the VLM the physics-error question (Table 10). It asks the model to count specific physics errors (objects passing through each other, appearing or vanishing, floating, changing shape impossibly, or moving untouched) and to ignore task success and the amount of motion. 

Localization. Stage 2 follows up to 120 corner points from the first frame through the same frames with Lucas–Kanade tracking (Lucas & Kanade, 1981). It removes camera motion by subtracting each frame’s median point velocity, then computes 16 signals from the remaining motion: the num-ber of points, the fraction still tracked at the end, and the fraction lost away from the frame border, which is how an object vanishing mid-video shows up; the mean point speed and the 95th percentiles of speed, acceleration and jerk; seven statistics aimed at single families: the vertical acceleration of downward-moving points and its magnitude (gravity), speed after the sharpest slowdown relative to speed before it (momentum), the rate of direction reversals, the growth and fluctuation of the point cloud’s spread (deformation), and centroid travel relative to rotation (friction); and the time and rel-ative strength of the frame with the largest mean point acceleration. In the interactive tool, Stage 2 goes further. A VLM names the objects, SAM 3 (Carion et al., 2026) segments and tracks them, Stage-1 flags are grouped into time windows, and each constraint family is ranked with a confidence and a time window. That confidence is the larger of a heuristic prior from the Stage-2 evidence and a VLM triage score. Section 4.4 uses only the 16 point-track signals. 

VLM verification. Stage 3 asks each of the ten VLMs three more questions (Table 10): task completion, violation type and, on unobservable videos only, adherence to the stated property. All four questions, including the Stage-1 physics-error question, show eight frames spaced evenly from the first to the last frame, with the longer side at most 512 pixels (Llama-4-Scout receives four frames). Every question states the task. Only the hidden-property question states the property, and it also gives the scenario’s expected outcome. The violation question lists eight failure descriptions and a “no physics problem” option. The eight descriptions are one for each family in Table 10 plus object permanence (an object appearing or vanishing). The options are shuffled per video with a fixed seed, so a preference for the first-listed option does not favor any family. These options are descriptions, not the human labels. There is no Contact option, so Contact is merged into Collision when scoring, and permanence has no human label. Each answer is read from the probabilities of the VLM’s first output token. We sum the probability of each allowed answer over its surface forms (e.g. “3” and “ 3”), renormalize over the allowed answers, and take the expected rating for the 1–4 questions and P (yes) for completion. Violation detection uses 1 − P (none) , and family attribution uses P (family ). A missing answer is replaced by that VLM’s mean score. Combining evidence. For each question, each VLM’s scores are converted to ranks over the 439 videos, scaled to [0 , 1] , and averaged over the ten VLMs, so that no model’s calibration dominates. The 38 Stage-1–2 signals are reduced to one signal score per question. On the training folds, we keep the five signals with the largest absolute Spearman correlation with the human label (the 1–4 rating for plausibility and adherence, the binary label otherwise), flip the sign of those that correlate negatively, and average their ranks. The final score is w r VLM + (1 − w) rsig , where r denotes a rank and w ∈ { 0, 0.1, . . . , 1} maximizes AUC on the training folds. The training-fold signal scores used to choose w are themselves computed out of fold, and test videos are ranked against the training videos. Signal selection and w are refit for every question and fold, using five-fold cross-validation that keeps every version of a scenario (both observability conditions and all four generators) in the same fold. Family attribution is fitted on violating videos only, and adherence on unobservable videos only. 

Reporting and routing. Stage 4 writes a JSON report for each video from the shared evidence store. The report holds a chronological timeline of findings, each with its time window, object, violation type, confidence and the specialist’s explanation, together with the Stage-2 family ranking and each tool’s severity. An optional text LLM summarizes the report in prose. Stage 4 adds no score of its own. In the interactive tool, routing happens between Stages 2 and 3: only the specialists 20 Preprint. that Stage 2 ranks highest (three by default) run on a video. Section 4.4 applies no routing, so every signal and every question is computed for all 439 videos. 

VideoPhy-2 check. To test whether the physics-error question helps outside robot videos, we com-pare it with the standard question on VideoPhy-2 (Bansal et al., 2026). We use 1,200 videos from its test split, sampled evenly across the seven generators and the five physical-commonsense rat-ings. We count a video as plausible when its human physical-commonsense score is at least 4 and as implausible when it is at most 2, and drop the 274 videos rated 3, which leaves 926 videos (541 plausible, 385 implausible). We ask Qwen3-VL-32B, Qwen3-VL-8B and Qwen2.5-VL-7B both questions with the same frame sampling and answer scoring as above, and compare the two AUCs with a paired DeLong test. Qwen3-VL-32B improves from 0.645 to 0.720 ( p < 10 −6), Qwen3-VL-8B from 0.666 to 0.708 ( p = 0 .006 ), and Qwen2.5-VL-7B from 0.581 to 0.655 ( p < 0.001 ).
