Abstract — Visuomotor policies trained via imitation learning often inherit the unnecessarily slow timing of teleoperated demonstrations. Yet uniform speedup is unreliable because different phases of a manipulation task tolerate acceleration differently. In this work, we introduce AdaTempo, a self-supervised method that accelerates visuomotor policies by exploiting shared relative-tempo structure in demonstrations. AdaTempo establishes phase correspondence, aggregates the aligned relative tempo into a consensus, and maps it to a continuous speedup profile used to resample demonstrations into accelerated training trajectories. Training standard policies such as ACT or Diffusion Policy on these resampled trajectories directly embeds the desired tempo in the learned behavior, without runtime tempo selection or online retiming. Extensive evaluations show that AdaTempo achieves up to a 3.57 ×

speedup and yields a stronger success–speed trade-off than the original policies and representative acceleration baselines. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Visuomotor imitation learning has become a practical paradigm for acquiring complex manipulation skills from teleoperated demonstrations [1]–[3]. Recent large-scale poli-cies have further improved generalization across tasks and robot embodiments [4]–[6]. Yet standard imitation objectives optimize what actions to reproduce, not how quickly they should be executed. Since teleoperated demonstrations are often slow and cautious, the resulting policies tend to inherit this conservative execution timing [7]–[10]. Removing this inefficiency is not as simple as down-sampling the demonstrated trajectories by a constant rate. Different phases of a manipulation task tolerate acceleration

differently. Uniform acceleration can skip important inter-mediate states, alter the closed-loop state distribution, or request motions that the controller cannot track over the shortened time horizon [8], [10], [11]. Effective speedup therefore requires determining both how much to accelerate a task and how to distribute that acceleration over its progress. Recent work has made substantial progress in acceler-ating learned manipulation policies beyond demonstration speed [7]–[11]. However, existing methods typically de-termine how acceleration should vary using policy-derived proxies, interaction-based optimization, or additional runtime mechanisms, requiring auxiliary machinery during train-ing or deployment. This motivates a central question: can demonstrations themselves provide a direct and fine-grained supervisory signal for phase-dependent speedup? As illustrated in Fig. 1, our key observation is that demon-strations may differ in absolute speed yet tend to exhibit similar relative-tempo patterns over task progress, consistent with prior observations of approximate relative-timing invari-ance in skilled movement [12]. Rather than absolute speed, we focus on relative tempo: within each demonstration, which phases are executed relatively faster or slower? When corresponding phases exhibit similar relative tempo across demonstrations, this consistency suggests phase-specific task characteristics. Phases consistently executed more cautiously, such as grasping or insertion, may tolerate less temporal compression, while relatively fast phases such as free-space transport offer greater acceleration potential. Based on this insight, we introduce AdaTempo , a self-supervised method for learning cross-demonstration relative tempo to accelerate visuomotor policies. AdaTempo aligns arXiv:2610.02706v1 [cs.RO] 2 Oct 2026 demonstrations with different durations, constructs episode-relative tempo profiles at corresponding task phases, and aggregates them into a cross-demonstration consensus while downweighting phases with high disagreement. It maps this consensus to a continuous speedup profile used for smooth virtual-time resampling of the original trajectories. A stan-dard visuomotor policy, such as ACT or Diffusion Policy (DP), is then trained on the accelerated trajectories. The desired tempo is thus embedded directly in the learned be-havior, requiring no runtime tempo-selection module, online retiming, or changes to the control interface at deployment. Extensive simulation and real-world evaluations show that AdaTempo achieves up to a 3.57 × speedup over demonstra-tions and yields a stronger success–speed trade-off than the original policies and representative acceleration baselines. Our contributions are:  

> •

We identify shared relative tempo across demonstrations as a self-supervised cue for phase-dependent speedup, revealing recurring phase-wise timing structure beyond demonstrator-specific absolute speed.  

> •

We develop AdaTempo, which phase-aligns demonstra-tions, converts their relative-tempo consensus into a continuous speedup profile for offline virtual-time re-sampling, without auxiliary models or additional mech-anisms at deployment.  

> •

A. Visuomotor Imitation Learning 

Modern visuomotor policies learn temporally coherent manipulation behaviors through action chunking, generative sequence modeling, and increasingly large-scale multi-task training [1]–[6], [13]–[18]. These methods typically train on the temporal sampling provided by demonstrations and can therefore reproduce not only demonstrated motions but also their execution tempo [7]–[10]. Orthogonal work reduces policy inference latency through distillation, efficient genera-tive modeling, or asynchronous action generation [19]–[22]. AdaTempo instead targets physical task execution time by temporally resampling demonstrations before policy training. 

B. Acceleration of Imitation Learning Execution 

Existing approaches accelerate manipulation policies ei-ther by modifying policy execution at runtime or by altering demonstrations and training targets before deployment. 

a) Runtime policy-execution acceleration: Runtime methods advance within action chunks, select speed mul-tipliers, or retime commands based on motion complexity, tracking error, latency, or physical reachability [8]–[10], [23], [24]. Related approaches expose speed explicitly through variable-frequency conditioning or continuous action param-eterizations that can be adjusted at deployment [25]–[27]. While effective for adapting frozen policies or enabling on-demand speed control, these approaches require additional mechanisms to select execution speed at runtime. Some, such as SpeedTuning, further learn speed selection through reinforcement learning and environment interaction [9]. 

b) Demonstration- and training-side acceleration: An-other family of methods accelerates policy execution by mod-ifying the temporal structure of demonstrations or training targets before deployment. DemoSpeedup estimates condi-tional action entropy with a proxy generative policy and assigns different downsampling factors to the resulting trajec-tory segments [7]. Semantic approaches instead use vision– language reasoning, gripper–object relations, or propagated stage labels to identify regions that can be accelerated, while motion-frequency cues have been used to remove temporally redundant frames [28]–[30]. AutoSpeed opti-mizes over smoothly speed-transformed action targets using a prediction-error and horizon criterion, whereas SpeedAug combines variable-tempo augmentation with reinforcement-learning refinement [11], [31]. However, these methods typ-ically rely on auxiliary signals or objectives to determine where and how much to accelerate. In contrast, AdaTempo derives a continuous speedup signal directly from relative-tempo structure shared across demonstrations and incorpo-rates it through temporally resampled training data. 

C. Temporal Correspondence and Relative Tempo 

Temporal alignment enables comparison between demon-strations that follow similar task progress with different dura-tions. Dynamic time warping (DTW) constructs a monotone correspondence between sequences [32] and has been used to align demonstrations before extracting or transferring skill structure [33]. In acceleration work, ESPADA uses DTW to propagate semantic stage labels from an annotated trajectory to other demonstrations [28]. Complementary evidence from motor-control studies indicates that skilled movements can retain relative timing and normalized velocity structure under changes in execution speed [12], [34]–[36]. This observation motivates comparing demonstrations in a normalized tempo-ral coordinate rather than by their absolute execution speeds. AdaTempo uses DTW not to propagate predefined labels, but to establish correspondences across demonstrations, enabling relative tempo to be compared at matched task progress. We evaluate AdaTempo along four questions: (1) can it accelerate visuomotor policies across different policy backbones and simulated and real-world settings; (2) how does its success–speed trade-off compare with uniform and adaptive acceleration baselines; (3) do demonstrations exhibit consistent relative-tempo structure within tasks, and is this structure reproducible across demonstrators; and (4) which components are important for reliable acceleration?

B. Simulation Results 

Table II reports the simulation results across the ACT and DP task suites. Averaged over all five tasks, AdaTempo achieves the highest success rate of 75 .5% with a mean SOD of 2.83 ×. DemoSpeedup attains a comparable mean SOD of 

2.86 ×, but a lower success rate of 68 .1% . This suggests that AdaTempo improves the success–speed trade-off rather than maximizing execution speed alone. The advantage is consistent across both policy backbones. On the ACT tasks, AdaTempo achieves the highest success on both Transfer Cube and Insertion while maintaining substantial acceleration. On the DP tasks, it preserves perfect success on Lift at 3.28 × SOD, achieves the highest SOD on Can, and the highest success on Square. Although compet-ing methods obtain higher speed on individual tasks, they often do so with a larger degradation in success. Overall, AdaTempo provides a more balanced acceleration profile across tasks and policy architectures. 

C. Real-World Results 

Table III summarizes the real-world evaluation. Across all six tasks, AdaTempo achieves an aggregate success rate of 77 .8% , substantially higher than ACT ( 58 .3% ) and De-moSpeedup ( 56 .7% ). At the same time, AdaTempo reaches a mean task-wise completion-time speedup of 2.02 × over ACT, comparable to the 1.95 × achieved by DemoSpeedup. This demonstrates a substantially improved success–speed trade-off rather than simply more aggressive execution. AdaTempo achieves the best or tied-best success on four of the six tasks and the shortest completion time on four. The advantage is particularly pronounced on Conveyor Fast, where timely execution is critical, while AdaTempo main-tains high reliability at the fastest completion time. Although DemoSpeedup performs better on some individual metrics, the overall results show that AdaTempo more consistently preserves task success under accelerated execution.

Table IV summarizes the results. Cross-demonstration consensus improves robustness by suppressing demonstration-specific timing variations: using a single demonstration’s tempo profile reduces success, particularly on Insertion. Confidence weighting further improves reliability by moderating acceleration in phases where demonstrations disagree; removing it yields higher SOD but lower success on both tasks. Temporal regularization provides an additional stabilizing effect, with its removal producing a more aggressive success–speed trade-off, especially on Insertion. RBD-style augmentation provides no consistent benefit. Unlike integer-rate downsampling, where offset replicas re-cover source frames omitted by a fixed stride, continuous nonuniform resampling already samples at fractional source times. Additional offset variants therefore introduce more interpolated state–action targets rather than simply recover-ing discarded demonstrated states, which may explain why RBD-style augmentation does not improve performance in our experimental setting. V. C ONCLUSION AND LIMITATIONS 

We presented AdaTempo, an offline method that turns relative-tempo structure shared across demonstrations into accelerated training trajectories. Standard visuomotor poli-cies can then be trained without auxiliary models and de-ployed without additional runtime mechanisms. Across five simulated and six real-world tasks, AdaTempo reaches up to 3.57 × SOD; it achieves the highest average simulation success at a mean 2.83 × SOD and, in the real world, raises aggregate success from 58 .3% to 77 .8% over ACT while completing tasks 2.02 × faster on average. These results support cross-demonstration relative tempo as a useful self-supervised cue for phase-dependent speedup. Relative tempo is an empirical cue rather than a direct es-timate of the maximum feasible speed. Shared teleoperation habits may influence it, and a single medoid and consensus may be inadequate when demonstrations contain unrelated pauses, ambiguous correspondences, or qualitatively differ-ent strategies. Strategy-aware clustering is a natural extension for such data. The acceleration bounds (cmin , c max ) are also task-specific and are currently selected with held-out validation rollouts. In addition, resampling cannot remove physical limits: ag-gressive targets may expose tracking error, actuator limits, latency, or unmodeled contact dynamics, while high-gain control provides no formal safety guarantee. Finally, the learned tempo is fixed at deployment and cannot adapt online to changing conditions. Automatic bound selection and lightweight feasibility-aware adaptation are important directions for future work. ACKNOWLEDGMENT
