Abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have achieved remarkable advances
in robotic manipulation, yet their zero-shot generalization under out-
of-distribution (OOD) conditions remains limited.
These models often
entangle task-relevant invariant structure with environment-specific non-
invariant factors, causing policies to rely on spurious appearance cues dur-
ing action prediction. In this work, we propose MixVLA, a model-agnostic
training framework that improves the generalization of VLA models with-
out requiring additional OOD data or architectural modifications. The key
component of MixVLA is Adaptive Mixing of Non-Invariant Infor-
mation (AMI). AMI stochastically mixes non-invariant representations
to regularize distribution-specific variability while preserving complemen-
tary predictive cues. The mixed non-invariant features are then fused with
invariant representations for final action prediction, resulting in improved
robustness without sacrificing policy expressiveness. Extensive experiments
across challenging manipulation settings, including LIBERO, LIBERO-
Plus, the RoboTwin perturbation suite, and real-world tasks, demonstrate
that MixVLA improves overall zero-shot robustness while retaining strong
in-domain performance.

Introduction
Vision-Language Models (VLMs) (Achiam et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2023) have advanced
rapidly in recent years and are increasingly applied to robotic manipulation. This trend has
led to the emergence of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models (Kim et al., 2024; Brohan
et al., 2022) that map multimodal observations and language instructions into low-level
control actions. Benefiting from rich cross-modal representations learned by VLMs, these
models achieve competitive task success rates across both simulated (Liu et al., 2023; Chen
et al., 2025) and real-world (Walke et al., 2023) manipulation benchmarks.
Despite strong performance on in-distribution benchmarks, VLA models still struggle to
achieve robust zero-shot generalization under out-of-distribution (OOD) shifts (Li et al.,
2025a; Zhou et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026). Recent studies (Fei et al., 2025b) show that
even mild variations in lighting, appearance, or camera viewpoint can cause substantial
performance degradation. Some prior works (Zitkovich et al., 2023; Team, 2025) attempt
to improve robustness through large-scale and diverse data collection, but they incur sub-
stantial labor and financial costs. Other approaches (Abouzeid et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025)
enhance specific aspects of generalization by introducing model-specific modifications, which
often limit their general applicability. In this work, we instead focus on improving zero-shot
generalization through a model-agnostic training strategy that does not rely on additional

To better understand this intuition, we analyze the problem from a representation learning
perspective. Policies trained with empirical risk minimization (ERM) (Vapnik, 1999) tend
to capture both task-relevant invariant correlations and environment-specific non-invariant
signals. While invariant features provide stable cues for action prediction, non-invariant
factors such as lighting, texture, and viewpoint often contain both nuisance variations and
complementary predictive information. Under ERM training, directly relying on raw non-
invariant features can introduce spurious dependencies under distribution shifts. Conversely,
enforcing strict invariance may be overly conservative, as it can discard informative signals
that remain useful for action prediction. This observation motivates explicitly regularizing
the variability of non-invariant signals rather than relying on invariant features alone.
Building on the above insights, we propose MixVLA, a model-agnostic training framework
that improves zero-shot generalization of Vision-Language-Action models, without modi-
fying model architectures or requiring additional data. The core of MixVLA is Adaptive
Mixing of Non-Invariant Information (AMI). Specifically, we first disentangle invariant
and non-invariant representations using an information-theoretic objective. We then per-
form stochastic mixing within the non-invariant feature space to regularize environment-
specific variability while preserving complementary predictive cues. The resulting mixed
non-invariant representation is subsequently fused with invariant features for final action
prediction, enabling a more favorable balance between robustness and policy expressive-
ness. We evaluate MixVLA across a range of challenging manipulation settings, including
the LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus benchmarks, the RoboTwin perturbation suite, and real-
world robotic manipulation tasks. The results show that MixVLA substantially improves
the robustness of VLA policies under diverse observation shifts without requiring additional
data collection.
Our key contributions are three-fold:
• A model-agnostic framework for generalized VLA models.
We present
MixVLA, a model-agnostic training framework that improves the zero-shot general-
ization of Vision-Language-Action models under out-of-distribution (OOD) data without
additional data collection or architectural redesign.

Related Work
2.1
Vision-Language-Action Model
Recent Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models build upon large-scale Vision-Language Mod-
els (VLMs) (Bai et al., 2023; Team et al., 2025; Achiam et al., 2023; Comanici et al., 2025;
Zhang et al., 2026b;c) and are fine-tuned on diverse robot trajectory datasets to learn
language-conditioned visuomotor policies (Brohan et al., 2022; Zitkovich et al., 2023; Walke
et al., 2023; Ebert et al., 2021; O’Neill et al., 2024). RT-1 and RT-2 (Brohan et al., 2022;
Zitkovich et al., 2023) demonstrate that scaling cross-modal pretraining together with large
robot datasets can substantially improve manipulation performance and semantic ground-
ing. Following this paradigm, OpenVLA (Kim et al., 2024) discretizes robot actions into
rarely used vocabulary tokens and performs autoregressive action generation on top of the
Prismatic-7B backbone (Karamcheti et al., 2024). The π0 family (Black et al., 2024; Intelli-
gence et al., 2025) further introduces a dual-system design that augments a VLM backbone
with a dedicated action expert to jointly model high-level reasoning and low-level control.
Beyond architectural scaling, recent efforts explore richer spatial representations (Zhen et al.,
2024; Bhat et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025a; 2026a), intermediate supervision such as sub-
task decomposition and trajectory prediction (Zheng et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025; Zhang
et al., 2025c; Zhong et al., 2025; Cen et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b), and
post-training optimization via planning or reinforcement learning (Guo et al., 2025; Zhang
et al., 2025d; Lu et al., 2025; Fei et al., 2025a; Huang et al., 2025) to enhance VLA capa-
bility. Despite these advances in scaling and architectural design, improving robustness to
observation variations in unseen environments remains an open challenge for VLA policies.
This motivates our focus on enhancing the intrinsic generalization ability of VLA models
through a model-agnostic training strategy.
2.2
Generalization in Robotic Manipulation
Recent work has explored multiple directions to improve generalization in robotic manipu-
lation. One common line of work (Brohan et al., 2022; Driess et al., 2023; Zitkovich et al.,
2023; Zhaxizhuoma et al., 2025; Team, 2025) seeks to enhance robustness through large-
scale and diverse data collection. By expanding the coverage of training distributions, these
approaches aim to improve policy generalization across varied environments. However, such
strategies often incur substantial human and financial costs, and the practical boundary
of data-driven generalization for embodied models remains insufficiently understood (Team,
2025). Complementary to data scaling, another line of research improves robustness by intro-
ducing additional architectural modules or auxiliary components. For example, GeoAware-
VLA (Abouzeid et al., 2025) incorporates VGGT (Wang et al., 2025) to improve robustness
to camera viewpoint changes. AffordDP (Wu et al., 2025) enhances cross-scene manipula-
tion through affordance retrieval and transfer, while 3DS-VLA (Li et al., 2025b) improves
robustness to appearance and viewpoint variations by introducing a 3D point cloud per-
ception module. While these designs can improve robustness under specific perturbations,
they often introduce additional system complexity and may not generalize well to diverse
out-of-distribution scenarios, where realistic shifts are typically multifaceted rather than iso-
lated. A separate line of work (Yang et al., 2024a;b; Seo et al., 2024; Ryu et al., 2023) seeks
to improve policy generalization by learning robust visual representations or incorporating
structured equivariant priors.
These approaches typically integrate mechanisms such as
associative memory or geometric equivariance to maintain policy consistency under object-
level transformations, including scaling, rotation, and translation. However, such methods

level without modifying the backbone or the action head.

3.3
Adaptive Mixing of Non-Invariant Information
Purely invariant representations can be overly conservative, while exploiting non-invariant
features may introduce spurious dependencies.
We propose Adaptive Mixing of Non-
Invariant Information (AMI) to improve robustness to shifts in the environment-conditioned
non-invariant feature distribution P(NIe | E), where E denotes the environment. AMI
couples non-invariant features across samples through controlled, structure-aware mixing,
thereby smoothing environment-conditioned variations in the latent space.

For each sample i in a mini-batch, we randomly sample a partner index

Our experiments assess whether regularizing, rather than discarding, non-invariant informa-
tion improves zero-shot robustness to distribution shifts while retaining strong in-domain
performance. Beyond overall performance, we investigate the role of non-invariant informa-
tion in generalization, the applicability of MixVLA across policy architectures and real-world
settings, and the potential to reduce training requirements while preserving robustness gains

Robust Generalization.

Q2. Representation Analysis. What does regularized non-invariant information con-

Q3. General Applicability. Do the robustness gains extend across policy architectures
and to real-world manipulation?
Q4. Training Cost. Can MixVLA retain its robustness benefits using a single Invariant
Net training run, without training a separate standard policy?

Robustness to Unseen Distribution Shifts
Robustness from LIBERO to LIBERO-Plus. We build MixVLA on OpenVLA-OFT
and train it on LIBERO, evaluating on LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus. As shown in Table 1,
MixVLA achieves the highest overall success rate of 76.2% among the compared methods,
surpassing OpenVLA-OFT by 6.6%. However, its lower performance under camera per-
turbations suggests that non-invariant feature mixing alone may be insufficient to address
viewpoint-induced geometric shifts. On LIBERO, MixVLA retains strong in-domain perfor-
mance, achieving an average success rate of 96.7% compared with 97.1% for OpenVLA-OFT
(Table 2), with a decrease of only 0.4%.
Clean-to-randomized generalization on RoboTwin. We build MixVLA on π0.5 and
train it exclusively on clean demonstrations from RoboTwin 2.0.
As shown in Table 3,
MixVLA improves C2R success from 46.0% to 62.4% (+16.4%) over π0.5, while also increas-

Taken together, these results support a positive answer to Q1: MixVLA improves robustness
to a range of unseen distribution shifts while retaining strong in-domain performance.

Conclusion
In this work, we presented MixVLA, a model-agnostic framework that improves VLA
generalization by regularizing rather than discarding non-invariant information. MixVLA
learns invariant representations through an information bottleneck objective and derives a
complementary branch through weight arithmetic. AMI regularizes non-invariant features
9


through stochastic mixing before fusing them with invariant representations for action pre-
diction.
Experiments demonstrate improved overall zero-shot robustness while retaining
strong in-domain performance.
Our findings suggest that non-invariant information can
provide useful predictive cues when appropriately regularized, offering a practical direction
for generalizable embodied policies without additional OOD training data.

training seed of 42. The OpenVLA experiments use eight NVIDIA A100 GPUs with 80

accumulation.
Optimization and schedules.
Normal Net and Invariant Net are independently ini-
tialized from the pretrained backbone. AMI initializes its main branch from the selected
Invariant Net checkpoint and uses a newly initialized fusion module and optimizer. Open-
VLA uses AdamW with (β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.999), ϵ = 10−8, and weight decay 10−2. Its Normal
Net and Invariant Net schedules have no warmup and reduce the learning rate by a factor
of ten at step 100k. The AMI learning rate remains constant during its short training stage.
For π0.5, AdamW uses (β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.95), ϵ = 10−8, weight decay 10−10, and gradient-
norm clipping at 1.0. Linear warmup is followed by cosine decay to one tenth of the peak
learning rate at the end of each run.
Information bottleneck.
The OpenVLA implementation uses the Donsker–Varadhan
MINE estimator, with an exponential-moving-average coefficient of 0.01 and three ReLU
hidden layers of width 400. The critic receives pooled visual-prefix hidden states and flat-
tened action-token hidden states. The policy IB coefficient is 10−3. The critic is optimized
separately with Adam at a constant learning rate of 5 × 10−6, with its loss multiplied by
0.1.
The π0.5 implementation uses a Jensen–Shannon critic with two SiLU hidden layers of width
512. Its inputs are the masked mean of context tokens and the mean of action-expert tokens.
Marginal pairs are formed within each GPU’s minibatch. The critic maximizes the bound,
while gradient reversal encourages the policy to minimize it. The selected configuration uses
an IB coefficient of 0.01 and a constant critic learning rate of 10−4. The critic uses AdamW
with the same coefficients and clipping threshold as the policy, with one critic update per
policy update.

Effect of mixing strategies and probability.
We compare hard and soft mixing on a
LIBERO-Plus subset containing 2,318 evaluation episodes, varying the mixing probability
from 0.1 to 0.9. As shown in Figure 6, both strategies exhibit a non-monotonic response
to the mixing probability, with pronounced performance drops at intermediate values of 0.4
for soft mixing and 0.5 for hard mixing. Soft mixing exhibits smaller fluctuations in the
higher-probability range of 0.6–0.9, whereas hard mixing shows a more substantial decline.
Both strategies achieve their highest observed success rates at a mixing probability of 0.1.
These results indicate that increasing the mixing probability does not necessarily improve
robustness. Instead, the effectiveness of non-invariant feature regularization depends on the
interaction between the mixing strategy and its probability, highlighting the importance of
considering these choices jointly.

Limitations under Large Geometric Shifts.
Limitations arise under perturbations
that induce large geometric changes in observations, such as significant camera viewpoint
shifts. As shown in our experiments, the improvement under the Camera perturbation is less
pronounced compared with other appearance-related perturbations. This may be because
viewpoint changes introduce discontinuous variations in the observation space, where latent-

space vicinal smoothing becomes less effective. Addressing such geometric shifts may require
incorporating explicit spatial reasoning or multi-view representations.
Future Research Directions.
Several directions may further extend the proposed frame-
work. First, more advanced mixing strategies could be explored to better capture the struc-
ture of non-invariant representations.
For instance, structure-aware or attention-guided
mixing may provide finer control over how non-invariant information is regularized.
Second, integrating AMI with test-time adaptation or online representation updates may
further improve robustness in highly dynamic environments.
Third, Self-MixVLA motivates further exploration of the model’s own optimization tra-
jectory for improving generalization. Future work could investigate how to identify and
reuse parameter-update directions across training checkpoints to construct more effective
complementary representations. Adaptive checkpoint selection and trajectory-based weight
extrapolation may help narrow the robustness gap to full MixVLA while avoiding a separate
Normal Net training run.
