ABSTRACT 

Adapting vision-language-action (VLA) models to deployment-time distribution shifts is important for reliable robotic operation, but conventional first-order adap-tation can exceed the memory budget of inference-oriented deployment plat-forms. Zeroth-order (ZO) optimization offers a forward-only alternative with inference-level memory, but accurate gradient estimation requires many pertur-bation queries, making naive ZO prohibitively slow for large VLA models. We present VLA-ZO, a framework for fast ZO adaptation that exploits the structure of VLA computation. By confining adaptation to the action side, VLA-ZO keeps the expensive vision-language prefix frozen and reuses its conditioning states across perturbation queries and optimizer steps, while schedule-aware prefetching hides state-transfer overhead. On LIBERO camera-viewpoint shifts, VLA-ZO reduces end-to-end adaptation time by 25.59 × at q = 16 and 32.54 × at q = 64 relative to baseline ZO, while improving average task success from 48.27% without adap-tation to 58.17% and 63.58%, respectively. These results show that making ZO faster can make larger query budgets practical, providing a promising path toward resource-efficient VLA adaptation on deployment platforms. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models are emerging as a general interface for robotic control (Kim et al., 2024; Black et al., 2025b;a), but deploying a fixed policy is often insufficient in real-world environments. Changes in camera viewpoint, appearance, and operating conditions shift the obser-vation distribution away from that seen during training, causing VLA policies to degrade sharply under such shifts. Specifically, the success rates of state-of-the-art VLAs collapse from 95% to be-low 30% under modest perturbations to camera pose and robot initial state (Fei et al., 2025). Recent work has explored one-shot adaptation (Li et al., 2026; Kang et al., 2026), in which a VLA policy is adapted using a single demonstration from a target environment. This setting enables data-efficient post-deployment adaptation to address these distribution shifts. This adaptation, however, must run on the deployment platform itself, under a compute and memory budget provisioned for inference rather than training. Conventional first-order adaptation fits this setting poorly (Figure 1a, left): backpropagation requires retaining intermediate activations that can otherwise be discarded during inference, substantially increasing memory requirements (Cai et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2023). Even with gradient checkpointing enabled, this overhead grows with batch size: for π0.5, first-order adaptation uses 20.27 GiB of peak memory at batch size 16 and 54.69 GiB at batch size 64, compared with the corresponding inference-level footprints of 12.74 GiB and 27.71 GiB for forward-only ZO. The relative gap therefore increases from 1.59 × to 1.97 ×. Moreover, gradient checkpointing trades memory for additional recomputation and does not remove the need for a backward-capable execution path. These requirements make first-order adaptation difficult to accommodate in inference-oriented deployment stacks as VLA models scale. This motivates zeroth-order (ZO) optimization as a natural alternative (Figure 1a, right), as it en-ables adaptation using only forward evaluations without backpropagation. By estimating parameter updates solely from forward evaluations, ZO avoids backpropagation and substantially reduces the mem-ory requirements for adaptation, making it attractive for memory-constrained devices as well as deployment environments where backward execution is unavailable or undesirable. Despite these advantages, the potential of ZO optimization for adapting VLA policies remains largely unexplored. This trade-off is governed by the query budget q, the number of random per-turbation directions evaluated per optimization step, each as a ± pair of forward passes. A larger 

q improves the gradient estimate, allowing adaptation quality to approach first-order performance while peak memory remains at the inference level. The same knob, however, also determines the computational cost: every additional direction requires two more forward passes through the full model, causing adaptation time to grow with q and quickly exceed that of first-order adaptation. Be-cause modern VLAs contain expensive vision and language components, naively applying ZO can therefore require hours of adaptation. Thus, the main obstacle to practical ZO-based VLA adaptation is not whether it works, but whether it can run fast enough. 

2 RELATED WORK 

VLA adaptation. Recent work has increasingly studied data-efficient adaptation of pretrained VLA policies from limited target-domain supervision (Li et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026; Nguyen et al., 2026). At the extreme of this low-data regime, one-shot methods adapt a VLA policy using only a single target-domain demonstration. FTM and FLA enable such adaptation to novel camera viewpoints (Li et al., 2026), while DART addresses visual and embodiment shifts and considers domain-level adaptation across tasks (Kang et al., 2026). Our focus is complementary: rather than developing a new adaptation method, we study how to reduce the computational cost of zeroth-order VLA adaptation under deployment-time compute and memory constraints. 

Zeroth-order optimization. Zeroth-order (ZO) optimization estimates gradients from perturbed forward evaluations without backpropagation. MeZO (Malladi et al., 2023) enables LLM fine-tuning with approximately the memory footprint of inference and reports performance comparable to back-propagation on several language tasks. Subsequent benchmarking examines how task alignment and optimizer design affect ZO fine-tuning (Zhang et al., 2024), while SubZero explores low-rank per-2turbations in random subspaces (Yu et al., 2025). For execution efficiency, MobiZO parallelizes per-turbation evaluations through Multi-Perturbed LoRA within existing inference engines (Gao et al., 2025). In general deep-network training, DeepZero combines sparse coordinate-wise gradient esti-mation with reuse of features preceding the perturbed layer and forward parallelization (Chen et al., 2024).

We study post-deployment adaptation of a pretrained VLA policy from a single target-domain demonstration, a common one-shot VLA adaptation setting used in recent studies (Li et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2025; Kang et al., 2026). Following the domain-level setting of Kang et al. (2026), the demonstration is collected from one task in the target domain, and the adapted policy is evaluated on both the demonstrated task and unseen tasks in the same domain. For adaptation, we use one target-domain demonstration trajectory per scene, D = {(oi, ℓ, a i)}Ni=1 , where N is the number of observation–action pairs, oi denotes the i-th observation (camera images and proprioceptive state), 

ℓ is the language instruction shared across the trajectory, and ai is the corresponding action chunk. To characterize the computation overhead during ZO adaptation, we separate the VLA policy into a vision-language prefix fϕ with parameters ϕ and an action expert gψ with parameters ψ. Let πθ ,with θ = ( ϕ, ψ ), denote the VLA policy. For each sample (oi, ℓ, a i) ∈ D , the frozen prefix first forms a complete, sample-specific conditioning state hi; the action expert then uses hi to define the policy over action chunks:

Prior VLA adaptation methods often adapt vision-side parameters, including vision encoders, visual represen-tations, and visual projection layers (Kim et al., 2024; Grover et al., 2026; Abouzeid et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026). Among these, Feature Linear Adaptation (FLA) (Li et al., 2026) adapts the vision encoder with low-rank updates while keeping the downstream language and action com-ponents frozen. We instead ask whether ZO adaptation can be confined to the action expert while keeping the ex-pensive vision-language prefix frozen. Figure 2a compares two LoRA placements for π0.5. The 

vision-side placement targets the feed-forward projec-tions of every block of the vision encoder inside fϕ,following the placement used by Li et al. (2026). The

Both configurations keep the pretrained model weights frozen and optimize only their LoRA parameters with ZO using the objective in Eq. (2). Under this matched setup, Figure 2b shows that action-side ZO increases average success across the three camera-viewpoint shifts from 48 .3% to 58 .2% , while matching vision-side ZO on average ( 56 .6% ). Given that it uses approximately 3.8× fewer trainable parameters, this result motivates confining adaptation to the action ex-pert while keeping the vision-language prefix frozen. This makes action-side adaptation a more efficient choice in our method, achieving comparable performance with substantially shorter adaptation time, in addition to re-quiring fewer trainable parameters. This efficiency stems from the prefix output being independent of the perturbed parameters. The key observation is that once adaptation is confined to the action expert, the expensive vision-language prefix becomes independent of the ZO perturbation. In a naive ZO step, each of the 2q perturbed evaluations recomputes the same frozen-prefix state for a sample, resulting in 2qBT prefix executions over T updates despite only N distinct conditioning states. VLA-ZO addresses this computational bottleneck in ZO adaptation through three optimizations, illustrated in Figure 3. First, within an update step, it computes the prefix once per sample and reuses the resulting state across all 2q probes (Section 4.1). Second, the same prefix states are retained across update steps, so that each distinct observation requires only one prefix evaluation over the full adaptation run (Section 4.2). Finally, VLA-ZO overlaps transfer of the next cached batch with the current probe evaluations using schedule-aware prefetching (Section 4.3).

The three mechanisms below progressively reduce the remaining prefix-related overhead: first by eliminating repeated prefix execution, then by reusing cached prefix states across update steps, and finally by hiding cache-transfer latency behind action-expert computation. 4.1 WITHIN -S TEP STATE REUSE 

Within a ZO update, all 2q probes for a given sample share the same input (oi, ℓ ). Because adaptation is confined to the action expert, perturbing the adaptation parameters changes only the action expert, while the frozen prefix produces the same conditioning state hi for every probe. VLA-ZO computes 5hi = fϕ(oi, ℓ ) once per sample and reuses it across all 2q perturbed action-expert evaluations. For a minibatch of size B, this reduces the number of prefix evaluations per update from 2qB to B,while leaving the required 2qB action-expert evaluations unchanged. Accordingly, the per-update cost decreases from Eq. (4a) to Eq. (4b). The mathematical equivalence of reuse and recomputation rests on three conditions, all of which are met by our cached action-side implementations. First, all perturbed parameters must lie after the reuse boundary: we adapt the action expert in π0.5 and the action head in OpenVLA-OFT. Second, 

hi must contain the complete information passed from the prefix to the action expert: π0.5 caches the prefix attention state and padding mask, while OpenVLA-OFT caches the action-token hidden features consumed by its action head. Third, the frozen prefix must be deterministic for a fixed (oi, ℓ )

under the execution configuration used for caching. Thus, under exact arithmetic, reuse leaves the objective and finite-difference estimator unchanged.

Within-step reuse still recomputes the prefix whenever a sample appears in a new optimizer step. In one-shot adaptation, however, every minibatch is drawn from the same demonstration of N obser-vations, and the prefix remains frozen for the entire run. Therefore, once hi has been computed for a sample, the same state can be reused every time that sample is revisited. VLA-ZO stores each con-ditioning state after its first computation and looks it up on subsequent visits. The cache is populated lazily, so a miss runs the prefix once and stores the result, while a hit skips prefix execution entirely. Over T updates, within-step reuse performs BT prefix evaluations, whereas ideal across-step reuse requires at most one prefix evaluation for each of the N demonstration observations. Across-step reuse replaces repeated prefix execution with cached-state loading, but keeping all N

conditioning states resident on the accelerator would make device memory grow with the demonstra-tion size. VLA-ZO therefore stores the persistent cache on disk and stages only the states required by each minibatch. This bounds accelerator-side cache storage, but places disk loading, batch as-sembly, and host-to-device transfer on the critical path if performed synchronously. VLA-ZO hides 6this latency by overlapping preparation of the next cached batch with the 2q probe evaluations of the current update, as illustrated in Figure 3. 

Schedule-aware lookahead. Let Bt denote the minibatch used at update t and let κt denote the corresponding sampler position. For a fixed sampler seed s, the minibatch schedule is determined by the sampling rule, Bt = S(s, κ t). Thus, while update t is running, VLA-ZO can reconstruct 

Bt+1 from (s, κ t+1 ) using a separate local generator. This lookahead follows the same permutation and batch-formation rules as the live sampler, but does not advance its state or consume randomness used for the perturbations or objective. For cached samples in Bt+1 , a background worker reads the conditioning states, assembles the next batch, and transfers it to the accelerator while the current batch evaluates its 2q action-expert probes. The worker never invokes the policy or materializes cache misses; an incompletely cached future batch remains on the ordinary demand path when that update begins. We prefetch only one update ahead, so device staging contains at most the current and next batches of conditioning states. Implementation details are given in Appendix B. 

Effect of latency hiding. For a fully cached minibatch, loading its conditioning states costs Bc ld ,while evaluating the 2q action-expert probes costs 2qBc suf . Without prefetching, these costs are serialized, yielding Cacross = B(cld + 2 qc suf ). With one-update lookahead, loading for the next up-date overlaps with probe computation for the current update. 5 EXPERIMENTS 

5.1 SETUP 

Benchmark and Evaluation We evaluate π0.5 (Black et al., 2025a) and OpenVLA-OFT (Kim et al., 2025) on all 40 tasks in the LIBERO Spatial, Object, Goal, and LIBERO-10 suites (Liu et al., 2023). Following DART’s scene-wise one-shot protocol (Kang et al., 2026), adaptation uses five expert demonstrations, one per scene, rendered under the target visual condition. We test three camera shifts ( Small , Medium , and Large ) and compound shifts combining Medium with image blur or altered lighting. Each condition is evaluated over 40 initial states per task (1,600 episodes), using indices 10–49 and including tasks without adaptation demonstrations. Initial-state indices 0–9 are excluded because they were used for learning-rate selection. 

Baselines and Ablations We compare VLA-ZO with the unadapted policy, FLA (Li et al., 2026), and DART (Kang et al., 2026). Our FLA and DART baselines, denoted by †, use zeroth-order gradient estimates computed using only forward passes and update the parameters with SGD. At each query budget, the execution ablation fixes the adaptation settings and progressively enables within-step reuse, across-step reuse, and schedule-aware prefetch. We presented VLA-ZO, a fast forward-only framework for one-shot VLA adaptation that elimi-nates redundant computation across zeroth-order queries. This appendix specifies the cache boundary, minibatch schedule, and transfer protocol behind Sec-tion 4.3. B.1 CACHE CONTENTS 

The reuse boundary of π0.5. For π0.5, a cached conditioning state contains the attention keys and values from every frozen vision–language layer, together with the prefix padding mask. For a single observation, each layer’s key and value tensors have shape [1 , H KV , L, d h], and the Boolean mask has shape [1 , L ]. Here, L is the padded prefix length, HKV the number of key/value heads, and dh the head dimension. This state contains the information required by the action expert from the frozen prefix. Action targets, sampled action noise, flow time, and trainable action projections remain outside the cache. 

The reuse boundary of OpenVLA-OFT. For OpenVLA-OFT, a cached conditioning state con-tains the final-layer hidden states of the frozen language model at the action-token positions. For a single observation, this tensor has shape [1 , KD a, d ], and the L1 head reshapes it to [1 , K, D ad]

before its first projection. Here, K = 8 is the action-chunk length, Da = 7 the action dimension, and d = 4096 the language-model width. Both camera images, the proprioceptive state, and the prompt are processed before this boundary by the frozen vision backbone, projectors, and language model, and the action head reads no other input; the state therefore contains all information the head requires from the frozen VLA. 

Cold and mixed minibatches. Cache construction is lazy and takes place in the training thread. If every sample in a microbatch is cached, its states are loaded in sampler order and prefix computa-tion is skipped. If any record is absent, the implementation executes the frozen prefix at the original full microbatch shape. It writes only new records and checks that previously stored rows match the recomputed values exactly. Keeping the original shape avoids changing matrix-multiplication and attention kernels by compacting only the missing samples. Consequently, a mixed microbatch can recompute cached observations during cache population: there is at most one stored record per observation and namespace, but there can be more than one physical prefix evaluation of that obser-vation. The N c pre population term in Section 4.2 describes ideal per-observation materialization; this conservative fallback can add cold-cache computation. Missing files fall back to demand-time computation, whereas corrupt records, incompatible layouts, changed inputs, or detected numerical mismatches raise an error. B.2 RECONSTRUCTING THE NEXT MINIBATCH 

Let b be the microbatch size and a the number of accumulated microbatches, so that the effective batch size is B = ab . For update t, the training thread first obtains all a microbatches and their cached or freshly computed prefix states. It then schedules the a microbatches of update t + 1 before evaluating the current update’s 2q probes. The queue is bounded to one future optimizer update. Only fully cached future microbatches are eligible; incomplete ones remain on the ordinary demand path. A future that is not ready when requested delays its own batch and never changes the sample order. The implementation requires standard ordered batching and the configured full microbatch shape. The consumed cursor, optimizer state, and training random-number state are checkpointed; speculative futures are rebuilt after resume, and persistent files can either be reused or repopulated lazily. B.3 HOST STAGING AND CUDA S TREAMS 

The cache persists on disk, while a single background worker reads records and assembles a com-plete microbatch in reusable pinned host buffers. On a dedicated CUDA stream, the worker allo-cates device tensors with matching shapes, dtypes, and strides, enqueues nonblocking host-to-device copies, and records a completion event. Before consuming a staged batch, the compute stream waits on that event and records its use of the device tensors through record stream . This establishes the copy-to-compute dependency without synchronizing the entire device. The host buffers remain 16 alive and unchanged until their copy event completes; only then can they return to the staging pool. The worker performs file reads, tensor assembly, and transfers only. All model calls, cache-miss computation, and objective randomness remain in the training thread. B.4 WHAT REUSE COSTS AND WHAT IT PRESERVES 

What reuse costs. The price of all three mechanisms is memory, and it is bounded by the demon-stration rather than by the workload. The accelerator staging budget covers two batches of condition-ing states: the B the current update uses, and the B the prefetcher is staging for the next one. What reuse preserves. VLA-ZO changes only the execution of ZO adaptation: it does not alter the objective, perturbation directions, trainable parameters, or update rule. Action noise and flow time remain sam-pled for each direction and are replayed within its ± pair. Bitwise-identical training trajectories are not implied: the cached path separates prefix prefill from action denoising and uses a different GPU execution path, while deterministic CUDA algorithms are not enforced.
