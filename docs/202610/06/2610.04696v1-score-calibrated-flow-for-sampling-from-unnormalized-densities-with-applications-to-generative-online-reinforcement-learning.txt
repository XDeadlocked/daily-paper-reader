## ABSTRACT 

Diffusion and flow models provide expressive policy classes for online reinforce-ment learning (RL), enabling multimodal behaviors and improved performance. However, training these policies remains challenging: the critic specifies the de-sired policy as an unnormalized Boltzmann density but does not provide direct samples from it. Many existing methods rely on importance sampling to con-struct training signals, which can suffer from high variance, increasing computa-tional cost and destabilizing training. In this paper, we propose Score-Calibrated Flow (SCF), a simple and efficient algorithm for training generative models to sample from unnormalized densities without importance sampling or backprop-agation through the sampling trajectory. Our key idea is to learn the desired flow by enforcing self-consistency, bypassing the challenging estimation of the target posterior mean. By jointly exploiting the prescribed target score and the structure of flow matching, we establish these self-consistency requirements as 

score-calibrated optimality conditions , first for the terminal density and then for the trainable velocity field. We prove that their unique solutions are, respectively, the target density and the ideal flow model that conditional flow matching (CFM) would recover if target samples were available. We formulate the velocity con-dition as a fixed-point equation and exploit its conditional-expectation structure to construct a stop-gradient objective for enforcing it. The resulting training pro-cedure retains the scalable sample–interpolate–regress structure of CFM despite the absence of target samples, using endpoints generated by the current flow. For online RL, the critic gradient supplies the target score at the generated actions, yielding a direct approach to actor training. Experiments on RL benchmarks demonstrate that SCF matches or improves upon state-of-the-art generative-policy baselines, while substantially reducing training time. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Diffusion and flow models learn expressive distributions by transforming initial noise through stochastic or deterministic dynamics (Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015; Ho et al., 2020; Lipman et al., 2023). They are appealing as policy classes since several distinct actions may be valuable in the same state, whereas a restrictive distribution may fail to represent these alternatives. Greater policy expressiveness can also translate into improved performance. These advantages have motivated the use of generative policies in imitation learning (Chi et al., 2025) and offline reinforcement learning (RL) (Wang et al., 2023). In these settings, expert demonstrations or offline datasets provide the samples needed in the standard training recipe for generative models. In online RL, however, direct samples from the target density that policy improvement asks the actor to learn are unavailable. The difficulty is particularly clear in maximum-entropy RL (Haarnoja et al., 2018). At a given state 

s, soft policy improvement targets the Boltzmann policy πQ(a|s) ∝ exp( τ Q (s, a )) , where Q is the critic and τ > 0 is the inverse temperature. Soft actor-critic (SAC) approximates this target within a Gaussian policy family using a mode-seeking objective. A diffusion or flow policy offers a richer representation, but standard training methods such as conditional flow matching (CFM) require

More broadly, the challenge is learning to sample from unnormalized densities , rather than learning from target samples as in standard generative training. Existing methods for generative online RL can be grouped into four categories (see Appendix A for a detailed discussion). Direct backpropagation (Wang et al., 2024; Lyu et al., 2025; Celik et al., 2025) optimizes critic-based objectives by differentiating through the entire sampling trajectory, which in-curs substantial computational cost and destabilizes training. Value-guided sampling (Psenka et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2026) uses critic information to guide sampling and exploration through stochastic or deterministic dynamics. Weighted regression (Ding et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2025) trains the policy on candidate actions using value-dependent weights, adding sampling overhead and making the training target depend on the proposal distribution and weighting rule. These approaches rely on surrogate objectives or sampling approximations that can introduce bias relative to exact soft policy improvement. In contrast, Boltzmann targeting (Dong et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2025; Jain et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026) derives training signals directly from the Boltzmann density, recovering the desired policy at the ideal population optimum. In practice, however, the required posterior means are typically estimated through multi-particle importance sampling. This incurs computational over-head at each update and can produce high-variance estimates that destabilize training. In this paper, we propose Score-Calibrated Flow (SCF), a simple and efficient algorithm for learning to sample from unnormalized densities. Our key idea is to learn the desired flow by enforcing self-consistency, bypassing the challenging estimation of the target posterior mean. By jointly exploiting the prescribed target score and the structure of flow matching, we establish these self-consistency requirements as score-calibrated optimality conditions . We first derive conditions that characterize the target density, then develop corresponding conditions for the trainable velocity field. The velocity condition takes the form of a fixed-point equation whose conditional-expectation struc-ture enables training with self-generated samples. Specifically, SCF generates an endpoint with the current flow, interpolates it with independent source noise, and constructs a regression target whose conditional mean equals the right-hand side of the fixed-point equation. This retains the scalable sample–interpolate–regress structure of CFM despite the absence of target samples, without impor-tance sampling or backpropagation through the sampling trajectory. Our contributions are summarized as follows. 1. Score-calibrated optimality conditions. We establish necessary and sufficient self-consistency conditions for both the terminal density and the trainable velocity field. Their unique solutions are, respectively, the target density and the ideal flow model that CFM would recover if target samples were available. 2. Scalable training from self-generated samples. We construct a sample-wise regression target whose conditional mean equals the score-calibration operator applied to the current velocity. We use this target in a stop-gradient objective to enforce the velocity condi-tion. Each target requires only one self-generated endpoint and one target-score evaluation, avoiding importance sampling and backpropagation through the sampling trajectory. 3. Principled design and strong RL performance. We derive coefficient schedules that cancel source-end singular factors and handle bounded actions through an exact change of variables that preserves the intended target density. Experiments on online RL bench-marks demonstrate that SCF matches or improves upon state-of-the-art generative-policy baselines, with substantial savings in training time.

## 3 SCORE -C ALIBRATED FLOW 

In this section, we develop Score-Calibrated Flow (SCF) for learning generative models to sample from unnormalized densities. The key paradigm shift enabling SCF is to reinterpret the flow match-ing framework as a one-to-one correspondence between terminal densities and their associated ideal flow models, rather than solely as a procedure for learning from target samples. Each terminal den-sity determines the population minimizer of the CFM loss, which is the velocity field that CFM would learn if samples from that density were available. Conversely, pushing the source density through the resulting flow induces the terminal density. We exploit the two directions of this correspondence to develop our optimality conditions. First, we view the flow model as a function of its terminal density and derive a score-calibrated condition that characterizes the target density through the flow it induces. We then let the trainable velocity field determine its own terminal density through the pushforward map, yielding a self-consistency condition directly on the velocity. This condition takes the form of a fixed-point equation whose conditional-expectation structure enables scalable regression using self-generated samples. Finally, we develop principled design choices to address practical challenges, including numerical singular-ities and bounded domains, and demonstrate how to apply SCF to generative online RL. 3.1 SCORE -C ALIBRATED OPTIMALITY CONDITION FOR TERMINAL DENSITY

## A RELATED WORK 

A.1 GENERATIVE ONLINE REINFORCEMENT LEARNING 

We organize existing methods by how they construct supervision signals for actor training. 

Direct backpropagation. DACER (Wang et al., 2024), FlowRL (Lyu et al., 2025), and DIME (Ce-lik et al., 2025) differentiate critic-based objectives through the sampling trajectory, incorporating additional approximations and regularization to make policy optimization more stable. Specifically, DACER estimates policy entropy by fitting Gaussian mixture models to sampled actions, FlowRL uses a flow-matching surrogate for Wasserstein regularization toward high-value replay-buffer be-haviors, and DIME optimizes a tractable lower bound on the maximum-entropy objective. Despite these measures, backpropagation through the full sampling trajectory incurs substantial computa-tional and memory costs and poses a nonnegligible risk of training instability. 

Value-guided sampling. QSM (Psenka et al., 2024) trains a diffusion score network using critic gradients at noisy actions, while QFlex (Wei et al., 2026) performs critic-gradient ascent from a learned source and fits a flow model to the resulting endpoints. Unlike our method, QSM and QFlex do not establish an exact characterization that uniquely identifies both the prescribed Boltzmann den-sity and its velocity from CFM construction. Their guidance relies on repeated action-refinement steps, and evaluating the critic at noisy actions can expose it to inputs where its estimates are unreli-able. We instead derive regression targets from score-calibrated optimality conditions and evaluate the critic gradient at clean actions. Each target requires only one endpoint-score evaluation, and the continuous-time formulation is independent of the number of sampling steps, without requiring an additional multi-step guidance procedure. 

Weighted regression. QVPO (Ding et al., 2024) trains a diffusion policy with Q-dependent weights and entropy regularization. Related approaches include reward-weighted flow match-ing (Fan et al., 2025) and diffusion policy mirror descent (Ma et al., 2025). These methods use importance sampling to construct weighted regression objectives and therefore remain susceptible to high-variance training signals when the importance weights become concentrated. Generating and evaluating multiple candidate actions further increases computational cost. Moreover, their in-duced training densities depend on the proposal distribution and weighting rule and generally differ from the prescribed Boltzmann target πQ, so even exact optimization may not recover the desired policy. 

Boltzmann targeting. MaxEntDP (Dong et al., 2025) and SDAC (Ma et al., 2025) use Q-weighted noise signals to train noise-prediction and score networks, respectively. DQS (Jain et al., 2025) adapts iterated denoising energy matching (Akhound-Sadegh et al., 2024) to train a score net-work using Q-weighted averages of critic gradients. RFM (Li et al., 2026) identifies these noise-expectation and gradient-expectation methods as instances of a shared posterior-mean estimation problem, and provides a principled way to combine both families through Stein control variates for variance reduction. These formulations target the Boltzmann density at the ideal population opti-mum, but their practical estimators rely on importance sampling, incurring computational overhead and estimator variance. SCF instead enforces score-calibrated self-consistency through regression on self-generated endpoints, without explicitly estimating the target posterior mean. A.2 LEARNING TO SAMPLE FROM UNNORMALIZED DENSITIES 

Learning generative samplers from unnormalized densities has been extensively studied, with rep-resentative approaches based on normalizing flows (No´ e et al., 2019; Papamakarios et al., 2021) and diffusion models (Zhang & Chen, 2022; Vargas et al., 2023; Akhound-Sadegh et al., 2024). We focus on recent matching-based methods that avoid both importance-weighted training targets and backpropagation through sampling trajectories. Adjoint Sampling (AS) (Havens et al., 2025) derives an endpoint-gradient regression objective from stochastic optimal control. Adjoint Schr¨ odinger Bridge Sampler (ASBS) (Liu et al., 2025) extends this construction beyond memoryless reference processes by alternating between learning a diffusion drift and a terminal corrector. Bridge Matching Sampler (BMS) (Blessing et al., 2026) derives 12 Preprint diffusion-matching targets from a fixed-point iteration perspective. Flow Sampling (FS) (Havens et al., 2026) constructs regression targets by combining the conditional velocity with an endpoint-score correction. SCF grounds its training rule in optimality conditions that uniquely characterize the target density and ideal flow velocity under mild regularity requirements. BMS and FS adopt a fixed-point it-eration perspective on training with self-generated samples, but do not establish that the desired sampler is the unique fixed point (Blessing et al., 2026; Havens et al., 2026). AS establishes fixed-point uniqueness only under the additional assumption of reciprocal-projection invariance (Havens et al., 2025). This is closer in spirit to our density optimality condition, where the flow is prescribed by the candidate density. Our velocity optimality condition goes further: it rules out spurious fixed points without presupposing a density–flow consistency. ASBS establishes convergence of its alter-nating updates under the assumption that each stage reaches its critical point, but does not establish uniqueness of the joint critical points of its coupled drift–corrector matching equations (Liu et al., 2025). SCF instead derives necessary and sufficient conditions directly for the terminal density and trainable velocity. Their unique solutions are the target density π and the ideal velocity vπ that CFM would learn from target samples. Moreover, the four methods above learn the drifts of stochas-tic differential equations (SDEs). Their sampling procedures rely on stochastic simulation, which may require a large number of function evaluations (NFEs), increasing computational costs during both training and inference. FS illustrates a structural limitation of this formulation: its target-score correction is proportional to the squared diffusion coefficient, so setting this coefficient to zero elim-inates explicit target-score supervision (Havens et al., 2026). The remaining objective reduces to CFM on self-generated endpoints, which reproduces the current density without identifying the pre-scribed target. Thus, simply removing the diffusion noise does not yield a training objective that ensures the learned velocity field generates the prescribed target density through ODE sampling. In contrast, SCF derives its velocity regression objective from score-calibrated optimality conditions, retaining target-score supervision without relying on stochastic sampling dynamics.

## D ABLATION STUDIES 

In this section, we analyze how different design choices affect the performance of SCF. We con-duct the experiments on the h1-run task from HumanoidBench. All variants follow the settings of Appendix C.2 and are trained with five seeds; the training curves are shown in Figure 4. First, we vary the NFEs used to sample actions during both training and evaluation. SCF uses 10 

NFEs by default; we compare with 6 and 20 NFEs. As shown in Figure 4 (left), the three settings perform similarly, indicating that the algorithm is robust to the choice of NFEs. Second, we study the coefficient choices in the regression target (18). Our default choice (44) combines the conditional velocity, current velocity, and score-based term, with λt = ηα 2 

> t

canceling the inverse powers of αt in the score-based contribution (Proposition B.6). As an ablation, we set 

λt = 1 and ζt = 0 , leaving only the score-based term. Since this term is unbounded as t → 0, we sample t uniformly from [0 .05 , 1] to avoid the singularity. Nevertheless, this variant fails to learn: for every seed, the regression loss exceeds 10 24 within the first 2 × 10 6 environment steps, and the return never improves (Figure 4, right). These results confirm that the singularity cancellation developed in Appendix B.4 is both theoretically well motivated and practically essential for stable training. 27
