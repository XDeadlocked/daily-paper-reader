Abstract —Vision-language-action models (VLAs) inherit strong semantic grounding from pretrained vision-language backbones but are typically optimized for predicting actions rather than future observations. They can see and act, but they do not imagine the future before acting. World action models (WAMs) built on video diffusion backbones can imagine but treat language as frozen conditioning on a continuous latent space. Unified models bring these modalities into one architecture, but they either decode autoregressively, one token at a time, or keep video continuous with an auxiliary action head. In this work, we present SUAVE, a Single vocabulary Unified Action-Video modEl in which a masked diffusion transformer generates video and actions conditioned on language, with all three modalities represented as discrete tokens in a shared sequence. Choosing which tokens to mask at inference turns the same network into a world model, a robot policy, or a video-action model. For action-free co-training, the action positions of unlabeled video are filled with mask tokens and excluded from the loss. Simulation and real-world experiments demonstrate two findings. First, a single SUAVE model predicts long-horizon video and acts as a policy, competitive with dedicated world models and specialized action policies on static and dynamic manipulation tasks. On a real robot, our model generates subgoal images and an action chunk spanning one second of motion in 1,030 ms on an RTX 5090 GPU, sustaining closed-loop control at 2.5 actions per second. Second, pretraining on robot video and co-training on human video substantially improves policy performance and zero-shot robustness to distribution shift. Together, these results show that masked diffusion is a practical and versatile foundation for unified video-action modeling.

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Robot learning has recently experienced a paradigm shift towards end-to-end policies trained on large datasets. This shift has been driven by two complementary model fam-ilies. Vision-language-action models (VLAs) [1]–[3] adapt pretrained vision-language backbones to output actions and inherit strong semantic grounding. World action models (WAMs) [4] adapt pretrained video diffusion backbones to output actions alongside predicted frames and inherit an under-standing of physical dynamics. Both families have shown that a pretrained backbone plus robot actions is a viable recipe for general-purpose control. However, each family imposes architectural assumptions that limit its suitability for robotics. A VLA sees and acts, but it is not trained to anticipate. It maps the current observation to an action chunk without explicitly predicting the visual consequences. Because the standard VLA objective is defined only over actions, it cannot learn from video that lack action labels, which represent a large portion of all video that exists. A WAM is trained to anticipate as a product of its future video prediction, but it does so in a continuous latent space. Although video prediction and control are often jointly trained, many architectures retain separate representations or prediction heads for the two outputs, making them inherently decoupled. Recent work on visual chain of thought [5] shows that predicting a subgoal image before arXiv:2610.04009v1 [cs.RO] 2 Oct 2026 2

acting improves VLA performance, which suggests that the two families are converging on the same idea: a policy should be able to imagine the future before acting. This motivates a single generative model over language, video, and actions, rather than separate modules for each. We model the three modalities in a shared sequence using masked diffusion, a method that is analogous to continuous diffusion but in the discrete space. If every video frame, action, and language token is drawn from one vocabulary, then a transformer trained to unmask tokens can learn to fill in video and actions across a few denoising steps. Which conditional the model samples at inference time is decided entirely by which positions are masked: mask the future frame and it becomes a world model, mask the actions and it becomes a robot policy, mask both and it becomes a video-action model that generates a visual subgoal and the actions needed to reach the subgoal. Excluding masked action positions from the loss allows video without action labels to enter the same batch as labeled demonstrations, enabling robot video with actions and human video without actions to share one training stage and one unified objective. Because all masked positions are predicted in parallel, decoding at inference only takes a handful of steps rather than one step per token. This is in contrast to fully autoregressive methods, which make generating a subgoal image alongside actions prohibitively slow for closed-loop control, as each image and action token needs to be predicted sequentially. The discrete formulation enables strong versatility, allowing the same model weights to control the robot, roll out long-horizon video, and learn from human video all in one objective. We propose SUAVE, a Single-vocabulary Unified Action-

Video mod El built on a masked diffusion transformer. SUAVE arranges a video context, a language instruction, a predicted goal frame, and an action chunk as one token sequence. Because every token is predicted by the same objective, the mask at inference selects whether the model acts as a world model, a robot policy, or a video-action model. Experiments in simulation and on a real robot support two findings: 1) One model is a competitive world model and robot policy. A single SUAVE checkpoint predicts long-horizon video at a quality comparable to dedicated world models. As a policy, it is competitive with specialized action policies on static manipulation tasks and exceeds every baseline on dynamic manipulation tasks. We show our results for real world tasks, LIBERO, LIBERO-PLUS, and the DOMINO benchmark. 2) Pretraining and co-training on video substantially improve performance and zero-shot robustness. Pre-training on robot video and co-training on action-free human video more than double the real robot task scores of models trained from scratch and roughly triple them under distribution shift, with the gap statistically significant in nearly every task and condition. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Vision-language-action models 

VLAs adapt a pretrained vision-language backbone to robot control by treating actions as an additional output modality. RT-2 [6] and OpenVLA [1] discretize actions into tokens and decode them autoregressively. OpenVLA-OFT [7] replaces sequential decoding with parallel regression over an action chunk. π0 [2], π0-FAST [8], and π0.5 [3] attach flow-matching heads or compressed action tokenizers to pretrained VLM backbones. All of these map the current observation and language instruction to action. CoT-VLA [5] extends this by predicting a single subgoal image before acting and shows that this visual chain of thought approach improves success through better grounding. However, because the subgoal is generated token by token, the authors report a 7 × slowdown in control. These models generate actions and at most one frame, however none model multi-step video. As their objectives are defined over actions, none can train on video that lacks action labels. 

B. World models and world action models 

A parallel line of work starts from video generation. iVideoGPT [9], Interactive World Simulator [10], and Cos-mos [11] learn action- or latent action-conditioned video pre-dictors. Recent world action models, such as DreamZero [4], adapt pretrained video diffusion backbones to output actions alongside predicted frames. These models can imagine the future well, but they typically operate in a continuous latent space: when language is present, it enters only as conditioning from a frozen text encoder, and actions are produced by a separate head. Video, actions, and language are therefore handled by different components with different objectives. SUAVE takes the opposite approach: all three are represented as discrete tokens and processed by one network under a shared objective. 

C. Unified video-action and unified multimodal models 

Several recent works generate video and actions jointly. UWM [12] and UVA [13] are continuous diffusion models over frames and actions. UWM samples independent noise levels for the two modalities so that one network can act as a policy, a forward model, or an inverse dynamics model. It is the closest continuous analog to our approach, and our independent per-modality mask rates are the discrete counterpart of its independent time steps. UniVLA [14] unifies vision, language, and action as discrete tokens but decodes them autoregressively, one token at a time. On the language-model side, LLaDA [15] showed that masked diffusion scales to LLM-sized text models, and MMaDA [16] extends it to a shared text and image vocabulary with parallel decoding. SUAVE brings this backbone to robotics by adding video and actions. Existing designs fall into three families: models that decode autoregressively over discrete tokens, models with continuous diffusion over frames and actions, and hybrid models that combine a language model with diffusion heads. SUAVE presents a fourth design: one generative mechanism over one discrete vocabulary for every modality, so that all cross-modal interaction happens under a single objective, with subgoal frames and the action chunk decoded in parallel.

III. M ETHOD 

SUAVE is a single transformer that models language, video, and robot actions as one sequence of discrete tokens, as shown in Figure 2. Attention is bidirectional within each block of the sequence and causal across blocks, and the model is trained with a masked diffusion objective [21]–[23]. In this section, we review masked diffusion (§III-A), describe how each modality is sequenced (§III-B), state the training objective (§III-C), and explain how the model is decoded as a world model, a policy, and a video-action model (§III-D). 

A. Background

Masked diffusion [15], [21], [24] defines a forward process that pro-gressively replaces tokens with [M] and a reverse process that restores them. In the forward process, each token of x0 is masked independently with probability t, where t ∈ (0 , 1] is the noise level, resulting in a corrupted sequence xt. Because the corruption process replaces a token with [M] , xt contains a mixture of clean tokens and mask tokens. The mask state is absorbing, where a token that is masked at level t remains masked at every higher level, and at t = 1 every token is masked. The reverse process runs from t = 1 to t = 0 and is driven by a mask predictor pθ (xi 

> 0

| xt), a network that predicts the clean token at every masked position from the partially masked sequence. The generation process alternates between predicting mask tokens and re-masking a fraction of them at the next noise level until no mask tokens remain. The mask predictor is trained with cross-entropy on the masked positions, which we state for SUAVE in §III-C. Two properties make this objective suitable for robotics. First, the mask predictor predicts every token in parallel in a single forward pass, where a full sequence is generated in a small number of denoising steps rather than one step per token. Second, the model can denoise a sequence with any combination of mask tokens, so the conditional it samples at inference is chosen by which tokens are masked. LLaDA [15] trained this objective and released open weights, while indus-try language model systems such as Gemini Diffusion [25] and Mercury [26] remain closed. MMaDA [16] extends this objective to a shared text and image vocabulary, and SUAVE extends that to video and robot actions. 

B. Tokenization and Sequence Layout 

Backbone. SUAVE is initialized from MMaDA-8B [16], a 32-layer transformer built on the LLaDA architecture [15]. We make two changes. The attention mask is replaced by the block-causal mask described below, and the vocabulary is extended with 256 action tokens placed directly after the vision codebook. No modality-specific encoders, adapters, or action heads are added. 

Tokenizers. Every modality is mapped into one vocabulary composed of three disjoint sets, V = Vtext ∪ V video ∪ V action ,that share a single embedding table and output head. Language 

uses the LLaDA text tokenizer. Video is encoded by MAGViT-v2 [27], a convolutional image tokenizer with lookup-free quantization. With this, a 256 × 256 pixel image becomes a 

16 ×16 grid of tokens, where each frame occupies 256 tokens. The tokenizer is frozen and shared by all datasets. Actions 4

are tokenized per dimension with uniform bins [1], [6]. Each dimension is linearly rescaled to [−1, 1] using its q1 and q99 quantiles in the training dataset, clipped to that range, and assigned to one of 256 bins. All dimensions and time steps share these 256 ids and are distinguished by their position in the action block. At inference, decoding returns the bin center.

Block-causal attention. Every token carries an index within a block, where we have one block for the instruction, one per video frame, and one for the action chunk, in sequential order. Attention is bidirectional within a block and causal across blocks, as shown in Figure 2. The goal frames attend to the context and language instruction, and the action chunk attends to the goal frames. The policy is therefore goal-conditioned, where actions depend on the imagined future. Within a video frame, all 256 tokens attend to one another.

Action-free video. A demonstration without action labels, such as a clip of human video, is laid out as shown in Eq. (1), with the action block padded with mask tokens. Those positions have no labels, so they are not in P, and the example is supervised only through its goal frames. Its context frames and language instruction are still attended to. This is our co-training mechanism: action-labeled robot demonstrations and action-free human video share a batch, a loss, and a training stage, with no auxiliary objective. 

D. Inference 

Modes. At inference time, the context video and the lan-guage instruction are given clean, and the target blocks are masked according to the desired mode. All policy results in this work use the video-action mode. In closed-loop control, the generated goal frame is discarded, and the next context frame comes directly from the camera observation. 

Long-horizon video rollout. Video beyond one goal frame is generated block-autoregressively, where the decoded goal frame becomes the newest context and the oldest context frame is dropped. Each step advances one second of video, and in our experiments, the rollout continues until the end of the held-out episode, up to 40 frames on the real tasks. Rollouts are seeded with a cold-start from a single observed frame, repeated across all eight context steps. During training, the same rule is applied to context indices that fall before the start of an episode. 

Prefix key-value cache. Under the block-causal mask, tokens preceding the first masked position never attend to other masked tokens, so their keys and values are identical across the denoising steps. They are computed once per action chunk and reused, and each denoising step runs a forward pass over the masked suffix only. The cache is exact, producing bit-identical outputs, and reduces decode time per chunk by up to 2.2×. With 4 denoising steps, which we use across all of our experiments, the video-action mode generates front and wrist subgoal images and a chunk of five actions spanning one second of motion in 1,030 ms on an RTX 5090 GPU, sustaining closed-loop control at about 2.5 actions per second on a real xArm7 robot. A. Experimental Setup 

Model variants. All SUAVE models share the same ar-chitecture and hyperparameters, differing only in training data. SUAVE-Scratch initializes from MMaDA-8B and trains directly on the target benchmark’s demonstrations. SUAVE-Pretrain first trains on a robot video corpus with actions, then finetunes on the target demonstrations. SUAVE-Cotrain pre-trains on the same robot corpus mixed one-to-one with action-free human video from Something-Something v2 (SSv2) [28], then finetunes in the same manner. Co-training here refers to a single pretraining stage where labeled and unlabeled videos share batches, as explained in Section (III-C). The robot corpus is chosen per benchmark so that pretraining stays in-domain. For real world tasks, we pretrain on DROID [29]. For LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus, we pretrain LIBERO-90, the 90 tasks that are disjoint from the four evaluation suites. For DOMINO, we pretrain on non-Franka embodiments, where evaluation only uses the Franka arm tasks. Each model is trained with a single seed, and we use the final checkpoint. Every policy decodes an action chunk of five actions per query at 5 Hz with 4 denoising steps and a prefix KV cache. 

Real Setup. With the xArm7 robot, we use a wrist-mounted RealSense D405 and an over-the-shoulder front view with a RealSense D455. We collect three tasks with 100 demon-strations each. PickVegetables : pick the vegetable named in the instruction from four candidates, lemon, green pepper, eggplant, or carrot. OrganizeShoes : correctly orient a fallen shoe and align it with the other shoe. SweepPile : sweep three debris piles into a dustpan with a brush held in the gripper. For each task, we train the three model variants and evaluate each on 24 initial configurations under nominal conditions, and then on the same 24 configurations under a task-specific distribution shift, which includes novel distractor objects for PickVegetables, an unseen shoe for OrganizeShoes, and altered lighting for SweepPile. Each episode receives a Task Score ,defined as the unweighted mean of ten components assigned by a reviewer who is blind to the model and condition. The components measure progress in engagement, attempts, partial completion, collateral contact, timeout, and speed. Specific component definitions are task-specific and are released with the code. C. DOMINO for Dynamic Manipulation 

Setup. DOMINO [20] evaluates the manipulation of moving objects in simulation. We use its 25 single-arm Franka tasks and evaluate 100 episodes per task at each of the three dynamics levels: Level 1 where objects move at a constant velocity, Level 2 where objects move along polynomial tra-jectories with varying curvature, and Level 3 where objects move along piecewise segments with stochastic, discontinuous velocity. DOMINO reports three metrics: success rate (SR), a manipulation score (MS) that credits partial progress, and route completion (RC), which is MS before safety penalties. Every method is trained on all 18,231 Franka demonstrations for these tasks, covering clean and randomized scenes at all three levels, and the same checkpoint is evaluated at each level. All baselines are retrained by us using their authors’ recipes. SUAVE-Cotrain is pretrained on DOMINO’s non-Franka embodiments mixed with SSv2 and finetuned on the Franka demonstrations. We presented SUAVE, a masked diffusion transformer that represents language, video, and actions as discrete tokens in one sequence. Choosing which tokens to mask at inference turns the same weights into a world model, a robot policy, or a video-action model with no change to the architecture or training objective. On a real robot, the joint model generates two visual subgoals and an action chunk at a rate sufficient for closed-loop control. Our model is competitive with specialized policies on both static and dynamic manipulation benchmarks, and the same weights predict long-horizon video comparably to dedicated video models. A controlled study on a real robot shows that pretraining on robot video and co-training on action-free human video more than doubles task scores and preserves that gain under distribution shift. 

Limitations. At our scale, co-training yields higher task scores than pretraining alone, however the difference is not statistically separable. More diverse action-free corpora would provide a clearer signal. We use a frozen MAGViT-v2 tok-enizer, which bounds the reconstruction quality of predicted frames. Finetuning it directly on robot data could improve visual quality. Our token sequences have a fixed length. Variable length decoding would allow the model to adapt its horizon to the task.
