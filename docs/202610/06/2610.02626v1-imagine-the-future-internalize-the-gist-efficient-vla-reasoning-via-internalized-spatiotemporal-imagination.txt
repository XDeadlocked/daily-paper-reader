## ABSTRACT 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models increasingly incorporate intermediate rea-soning to improve robotic manipulation, yet existing approaches primarily reason about observed states without explicitly anticipating future scene evolution. Ex-tending such reasoning to explicit future rollouts at every inference step, however, introduces substantial computational overhead. We propose IG-VLA , a VLA reasoning framework that enables models to imagine the future and internalize the gist . Our Latent Spatiotemporal Reasoning learns to imagine task-relevant future scene evolution directly in visual representation space, guiding action pre-diction without costly pixel-level video generation. To further reduce inference overhead, we introduce Scene Gist Memory , which internalizes reasoning-derived scene–behavior associations into a compact Scene Gist Token, preserving the bene-fits of future reasoning while bypassing explicit future imagination at inference. Extensive experiments on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and VLABench demonstrate the effectiveness and efficiency of IG-VLA. On the LIBERO-Plus Language suite, both the reasoning and gist policies outperform the strongest baseline by nearly 6% in success rate. The gist policy also achieves up to 6.38 × speedup over base-lines, reducing inference latency from 1,081 ms to 169.5 ms per action chunk on a single NVIDIA A6000 GPU. These results demonstrate that future spatiotemporal reasoning can be effectively internalized for efficient VLA deployment. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models enable general-purpose robotic manipulation by mapping multimodal observations and language instructions to actions Guan et al. (2025); Li et al. (2025a;b); Sapkota et al. (2025); Wang et al. (2025). However, learning an effective end-to-end mapping from multimodal perception to robotic actions requires extensive, high-quality demonstration data Liu et al. (2025a); Ma et al. (2026). To address this challenge, recent VLA models adopt a “think-before-acting” paradigm through Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning Guo et al. (2025b); Ye et al. (2025); Yin et al. (2025).By reasoning over task goals, visual observations, and object relations before execution, these models decompose complex instructions and adapt action plans to environmental feedback. Existing VLA paradigms include language-based reasoning Zawalski et al. (2024); Lee et al. (2025); Qu et al.; Huang et al. (2026a), which carry out reasoning in language domain and reasoning in vision ,exemplified by CoT-VLA’s decomposition of predicted goals into visual subgoals Zhao et al. (2025a) and VLA-Thinker’s repeated incorporation of intermediate state images Wang et al. (2026). As shown in Figure 1(a), language-based reasoning loses fine-grained visual details and achieves lower success rates. In general, existing approaches face two limitations: (i) reliance on observed states rather than future predictions, and (ii) substantial computational overhead from repeated reasoning rollouts. Specifically, existing approaches primarily reason over observed states and plan subsequent actions based on the current environment, without explicitly anticipating how the environment may evolve under different decisions. Meanwhile, reasoning in VLA models need to perform full spatiotemporal reasoning rollouts repeatedly at every inference step, introducing substantial computation overhead. To address these issues, we propose a visual reasoning framework for VLA models with two core mechanisms: latent spatiotemporal reasoning and scene gist memory . Latent spatiotemporal reasoning enables the model to imagine future scene evolution in visual representation space, ex-ploring plausible action trajectories and their potential outcomes. These imagined futures guide action selection, as shown in Figure 1(b). It is inspired by human reasoning process, which imagines how a scene might unfold under different candidate actions and makes decisions based on expected consequences Atance & O’Neill (2001). For example, when crossing a street, we consider multiple possible futures conditioned on our own actions such as crossing, waiting, or speeding up, then choose the safest action. This anticipation evaluates candidate behaviors through the imagined consequences rather than merely extrapolating scene evolution independently of actions Conti & Irish (2021). To reduce computational overhead, our proposed Scene Gist Memory internalizes reasoning-derived scene–behavior associations into a compact Scene Gist Token . This token captures general scene context and corresponding action patterns rather than preserving the complete future reasoning trajectory. During inference, the gist encoder predicts the Scene Gist directly from the current context, allowing the model to bypass the explicit future-reasoning pathway while retaining reasoning-derived behavioral guidance (Figure 2). It’s inspired by human behavioral psychology that repeated experience in familiar environments helps consolidate learned behaviors into procedural memory involving the basal ganglia Wulf et al. (2010); Wood & Rünger (2016). This memory supports rapid responses with less deliberation, reducing cognitive effort and decision time. For example, familiarity with a home allows a person to head directly to the kitchen for coffee without searching the entire house. Repeated experience reinforces the association between the familiar scene and the corresponding navigation behavior. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • Our framework incorporates Latent Spatiotemporal Reasoning , enabling VLA models to learn to imagine task-relevant scene evolution in visual representation space. These antici-pated scene dynamics guide action prediction without costly pixel-level video generation. • We further introduce Scene Gist Memory , which internalizes reasoning-derived scene– behavior associations into a compact Scene Gist Token. It enables the model to reuse this knowledge in corresponding scenarios, preserving reasoning benefits while bypassing explicit future imagination and full reasoning rollouts at inference. • Extensive experiments on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and VLABench demonstrate the effec-tiveness and efficiency of our framework. ## 2 RELATED WORK 

VLA Models. Recent advances in embodied AI have shifted robot learning toward foundation VLA models that directly map visual observations and language instructions into executable actions Brohan et al. (2022); Mees et al. (2024); Nvidia et al. (2025); Yang et al. (2025); Pertsch et al. (2025); Driess et al. (2026). OpenVLA Kim et al. (2024) adopts an end-to-end policy learning framework for language-conditioned robotic manipulation. π0 Black et al. (2024) extends this paradigm with scalable policy learning across more diverse robotic tasks. HAMSTER Li et al. (2025c) strengthens spatial reasoning through structured visual representations, while TraceVLA Zheng et al. (2025) further incorporates 2D visual trajectories to enhance the alignment between perception and action. 

VLA Reasonings. CoT improves LLM performance in mathematical reasoning, code generation, and question answering Wei et al. (2022); Yeo et al. (2025), motivating explicit reasoning in VLA models Huang et al. (2026b). Existing approaches mainly follow several directions. Prompting-based methods such as EcoT Zawalski et al. (2024) use supervised reasoning traces to decompose tasks into intermediate subgoals before action execution. Data-centric methods, including RAD Clark et al. (2025), combine human videos with LLMs to construct reasoning-action demonstrations for policy learning. Another line of work, represented by CoT-VLA Zhao et al. (2025b), replaces textual reasoning with visual subgoal representations to better align planning with robot perception. Recently, reinforcement learning Shao et al. (2024); Guo et al. (2025a) has been adopted to optimize reasoning policies under verifiable rewards, where GRPO encourages more effective reasoning trajectories Feng et al. (2026); Tan et al. (2025); Liu et al. (2025b). However, current methods often require costly reasoning supervision and sophisticated reward engineering Huang et al. (2026a).

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Dataset. We use LIBERO Liu et al. (2023) as the primary training benchmark and evaluate success rates on the Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long suites. We further evaluate robustness on LIBERO-Plus Fei et al. (2026) supervised fine-tuning. Finally, we evaluate on VLABench Zhang et al. (2025a) following Zhong et al. (2026), where the Non-Latent Spatiotemporal Reasoning baseline and our latent spatiotemporal reasoning module are fine-tuned on identical training data and evaluated on five public tracks: in-distribution, cross-category, commonsense, semantic-instruction, and unseen-texture. 

Baselines. We compare our method with representative VLA and world-model approaches, including ACoT-VLA Zhong et al. (2026), Octo Mees et al. (2024), OpenVLA Kim et al. (2024), π0 Black et al. (2024), π0.5 Physical Intelligence et al. (2025), CoT-VLA Zhao et al. (2025a), WorldVLA Cen et al. (2025), DreamVLA Zhang et al. (2025b), Fast-WAM Yuan et al. (2026), and ImageWAM Zhang et al. (2026). The complete comparison sets for each benchmark are reported in Tables 1–3, with reproduced results indicated in the corresponding table captions. 

Implementation Details. We use 8 NVIDIA H100 GPUs to train the reasoning module and use 4 NVIDIA A6000 GPUs to train the gist memeory module, while we only use a single NVIDIA A6000 GPU for end-to-end inference latency evaluation. We build on the PaliGemma-based backbone Zhong et al. (2026), using SigLIP So400m/14 as the vision encoder, Gemma-2B as the multimodal prefix model, and Gemma-300M modules as action experts. Latent spatiotemporal reasoning module predicts K = 10 future SigLIP latent states, supervised by latent MSE and cosine direction losses, while the auxiliary pixel reconstruction head is used only during training. For LIBERO, we train for 100K steps with a batch size of 16 and a peak learning rate of 5 × 10 −6 (cosine decay to 1 × 10 −7). For VLABench, we follow the configuration of Zhong et al. (2026) with 61K training steps and a batch size of 128 to ensure a controlled comparison. 4.2 SIMULATION EXPERIMENTS 

Main Results. Tables 1–3 present the simulation results on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and VLABench. On LIBERO, both IG-VLA (Reasoning Policy) and IG-VLA △ (Gist Policy) achieve the highest average success rate of 98.9%, outperforming the strongest baseline ACoT-VLA of 98.5%. In particular, IG-VLA △ achieves 100.0% and 97.8% success rates on the Object and Long suites, respectively, demonstrating that Scene Gist preserves strong manipulation performance without explicit future reasoning rollout. On LIBERO-Plus, our supervised fine-tuning achieves the best average success rate of 88.5%, exceeding best baseline ACoT-VLA by 0.5 percentage points, with a notable improvement on language variation from 79.7% to 85.6%. ## 5 CONCLUSION 

In this work, we present IG-VLA to address two key limitations of existing VLA reasoning: the lack of explicit anticipation of future scene dynamics and the high inference cost of repeated reasoning rollouts. By reasoning over future dynamics in latent visual space, IG-VLA provides task-relevant spatiotemporal guidance for action generation, while Scene Gist Memory further converts the acquired reasoning knowledge into compact behavioral guidance for efficient inference. Experiments across LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and VLABench consistently demonstrate improved manipulation performance and generalization, with particularly strong gains under challenging language and semantic variations. Meanwhile, the gist policy retains competitive reasoning performance while substantially reducing inference cost, showing that explicit future reasoning need not be repeatedly executed at deployment. Real-world robotic experiments further validate the effectiveness of the proposed framework beyond simulation. Overall, our results suggest that anticipating future scene dynamics and subsequently internalizing such reasoning provides an effective direction toward more capable and efficient VLA policies. 9Imagine the Future, Internalize the Gist: Efficient VLA Reasoning via Internalized Spatiotemporal Imagination

Algorithm 1 summarizes the overall training and inference pipeline of IG-VLA. During training, we first encode the current and future observations into the latent visual space and construct their latent transitions as supervision. The Latent Spatiotemporal Reasoning module learns to predict these future transitions from the current multimodal context, enabling the reasoning policy to anticipate task-relevant scene evolution and use the resulting future information for action prediction. We then internalize the acquired future reasoning into Scene Gist Memory. Specifically, the reasoning-enhanced representation is compressed into a compact Scene Gist target, whose associated actions are aligned with those produced by the reasoning policy. A lightweight gist encoder subsequently learns to predict this reasoning-derived Scene Gist directly from the current multimodal context, with both representation- and action-level supervision. In this way, the Scene Gist captures the behavioral knowledge distilled from explicit future reasoning without requiring the future rollout itself. 14 Imagine the Future, Internalize the Gist: Efficient VLA Reasoning via Internalized Spatiotemporal Imagination 

At inference time, only the current observation and instruction are required. The gist encoder directly retrieves the corresponding Scene Gist from the current context, which conditions the gist policy to generate the action chunk. Therefore, the expensive Latent Spatiotemporal Reasoning module and future-state rollout are completely bypassed during deployment, while their reasoning benefits are retained through the learned Scene Gist. A.2 MORE DETAILS ABOUT REAL -W ORLD DEPLOYMENT 

Setup. To evaluate our method beyond simulation and on an embodiment different from the training platform, we deploy the fine-tuned policy on a dual-arm UR3 setup (Figure 7). The hardware consists of two 6-DoF UR3 arms with parallel-jaw grippers, yielding a 14-dimensional state/action space, and observations from three RGB cameras (one top-down scene camera and two wrist cameras), each resized to 224 × 224 before the SigLIP encoder. We consider a pick-and-place-a-screwdriver task: starting from the robot home pose, the right arm grasps a screwdriver and places it at a target location (a single-arm task; the left gripper is disabled during deployment). All policies are fine-tuned from 

pi05_base on 77 teleoperated demonstrations (15 Hz). 

Deployment. Since the UR3 is joint-controlled while the policy predicts actions in a delta end-effector (delta-EEF) space, we integrate each predicted delta-EEF chunk onto the current end-effector pose (obtained by forward kinematics from the measured joints) and map it back to joint targets with a numerical damped-least-squares inverse-kinematics solver (warm-started from the current joints); the robot then executes the resulting joint chunk under real-time joint-position control. This conversion is performed entirely server-side, so the joint-space robot client is unchanged. Actions are executed with a near-synchronous chunking scheme: each 10-step delta-EEF chunk is executed in full at 5 Hz before the next inference, with a joint-velocity cap of 2.5 rad/s. 

Comparison and protocol. Under identical deployment conditions we compare three policies: π0.5,ACoT-VLA (the prior baseline), and our method (with latent spatiotemporal reasoning internalized through Scene Gist Memory). All three share the same fine-tuning configuration (delta-EEF action space, action horizon 10, pi05_base initialization, batch size 16, constant learning rate 5 × 10 −5)and differ only in the method itself. For each policy we run 16 trials with randomized initial screwdriver placements drawn from the same distribution across methods, and report the binary task-success rate. 15
