## Abstract 

Learning control from action-free recordings is challenging because intervention effects are unobserved and policies may exploit errors in reconstructed dynamics. We present a hi-erarchical model-based reinforcement learning framework that uses shared structure across related systems to learn system-specific control policies from action-free recordings. A hi-erarchical dynamical system reconstruction model captures shared dynamics and individ-ual variation through low-dimensional embeddings. These embeddings are then reused to parameterize shared policy and value networks, linking differences in reconstructed dynam-ics to differences in control. Policies are trained entirely via simulation under an explicit intervention model with additive latent perturbations. Piecewise-linear recurrent neural networks enable mechanistic analyses of the controlled dynamics, while decoder-based con-straints make the immediate effects of interventions interpretable in observation space and permit interventions on one modality while protecting another from direct manipulation. On Lorenz-63 and double-pendulum systems, hierarchical policies improve transfer over in-dependently trained policies. On Lorenz-63, they also achieve a higher mean reward than repeated planning with the same reconstructed models, perform comparably to methods trained with controlled interactions, and generalize to systems absent from policy training after embedding inference alone. Applications to neural–behavioral recordings demonstrate suppression of predicted movement under constrained neural perturbations. Together, these findings show how shared dynamical representations support transferable control and mech-anistic hypothesis generation from action-free recordings. 

## 1 Introduction 

Controlling nonlinear dynamical systems (DSs) is central to many fields of science and engineering. In neuroscience, control may involve selecting inputs that steer neural dynamics toward a desired state or behavioral outcome. Yet limited data and safety or ethical constraints often restrict opportunities to ex-perimentally probe the effects of such inputs (Krauss et al., 2021; Chandrabhatla et al., 2023). Neural and behavioral recordings may nevertheless be available across individuals or repeated sessions, providing in-formation about shared dynamics and individual variation. We call these recordings action-free when they contain no responses to the control actions considered here, even though task cues and behavior may be recorded. We study how to learn control policies from these recordings using reconstructed dynamics and an explicitly specified action model, without further interaction with the underlying systems during training. Having a model of the dynamics provides a basis for designing control policies. Two major approaches use such a model to select actions: repeated planning during deployment and prior learning of an explicit policy 1arXiv:2610.03065v1 [cs.LG] 2 Oct 2026 that maps the current state to an action. Model predictive control (MPC) follows the first approach, using the model to optimize a finite sequence of actions at each time step, apply its first action, and repeat the optimization from the next observed state (Richalet et al., 1978; Mayne et al., 2000). While MPC relies on computationally heavy trajectory optimization at every step, model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL) can amortize this cost by using the transition model to train a policy (Sutton, 1990; Moerland et al., 2023). In particular, latent world models allow a policy to be trained entirely on simulated trajectories and deployed with minimal latency (Hafner et al., 2019; 2020; 2025a). Although policy learning occurs in simulation, these methods typically learn action effects from controlled interactions with the real system. Learning from action-free recordings creates two difficulties. First, policies optimized in a learned simulator may exploit reconstruction errors, particularly when actions move the system into regions poorly constrained by the recordings. Without recorded control actions and their outcomes, there is no empirically supported intervention behavior toward which the policy can be regularized (Yu et al., 2020; 2021). Reliable dynamics reconstruction is therefore essential for generating the long simulated trajectories on which policy learning depends, alongside regularization that limits exploitation of model errors. This is particularly challenging for chaotic systems, where small errors can amplify rapidly (Lorenz, 1963). Second, even a model that perfectly reproduces the system’s dynamics without intervention does not tell us how a physical actuator would change its state. This relationship must therefore be specified through prior knowledge or an explicit modeling assumption. To address the reconstruction problem, we build on hierarchical dynamical system reconstruction (DSR). DSR seeks models that recover attractor geometry and characteristic temporal structure beyond short-term prediction accuracy, and excels particularly at learning dynamical systems (Durstewitz et al., 2023; Hess et al., 2023). Hierarchical DSR shares parameters across related systems while representing individual variation through low-dimensional embeddings, improving data efficiency and generalization when per-system data are limited (Brenner et al., 2025; Vermani et al., 2025). We extend this hierarchy to control by reusing reconstruction embeddings to parameterize shared policy and value networks, trained with soft actor-critic (SAC) entirely on simulated transitions (Haarnoja et al., 2018). This incorporates learned dynamical structure into control, with differences between related systems determining corresponding variations within a shared policy family. In the absence of an empirical action distribution toward which policies could be regularized, hierarchical parameter sharing therefore provides an alternative constraint. It reduces the number of trainable parameters and limits specialization to individual world models. Moreover, the controller can be applied to systems not encountered during policy training. Fitting a new embedding to their uncontrolled recordings yields a system-specific policy while shared actor and critic parameters remain fixed. The second problem remains unresolved by observational data alone, since we cannot identify how a physical actuator couples to the system. Here, we therefore ask what changes in measured variables could achieve a desired outcome. To study this question, we model interventions as additive actions that additively perturb the latent state, applied before the learned transition. Linear decoders make their immediate effects explicit in observation space, allowing us to characterize which variables the controller changes and by how much. They also permit modality-specific constraints. For example, interventions can alter decoded neural activity while preserving the immediate behavioral output, so that subsequent behavioral changes arise through the reconstructed dynamics (Emonds et al., 2026). To understand how these changes achieve control, we use Almost Linear Recurrent Neural Networks (AL-RNNs) with piecewise-linear latent dynamics (Durstewitz, 2017; Koppe et al., 2019; Brenner et al., 2024a). Analytic access to fixed points and stability properties allows us to examine how interventions shift dynami-cal mechanisms. Together, these analyses generate mechanistic hypotheses about which changes in observed variables can achieve control and the dynamical mechanisms through which they act. In sum, our contributions are: (i) a formulation for learning control policies from action-free recordings that combines DSR with MBRL under an explicit latent intervention model; (ii) a hierarchical controller that reuses reconstruction embeddings to share SAC across related DSs, enabling adaptation to unseen systems without retraining; (iii) decoder-based action constraints that make interventions interpretable in observation space and permit control across distinct intervention and target modalities; (iv) a mechanistic analysis of 2learned control strategies using regional fixed points and local stability properties; and (v) experiments demonstrating improved transfer to the underlying Lorenz-63 and double-pendulum systems relative to independently trained SAC controllers, embedding-based adaptation to unseen Lorenz-63 systems, and in-silico control of behavior in models fitted to neural–behavioral recordings. 

## 2 Related Work 

Model-predictive control MPC is a standard framework for controlling nonlinear systems by repeatedly solving finite-horizon optimization problems under dynamical and input constraints (Richalet et al., 1978; Mayne et al., 2000). For nonlinear and hybrid systems, piecewise-affine formulations provide an important compromise between expressivity and tractability, since nonlinear dynamics can be represented through collections of locally affine regimes with region-dependent control laws (Mayne & Raković, 2003). This is conceptually close to the piecewise linear (PWL) latent dynamics used in our work. However, classical MPC typically assumes a reliable controlled model and often relies on online optimization at deployment time. Recent learning-based extensions retain the receding-horizon principle while relaxing the requirement of analytically specified dynamics: CEM-MPC optimizes action sequences by sampling and refining them in a learned predictive model, whereas TD-MPC jointly learns a latent dynamics model and value function from controlled interaction data and performs short-horizon latent-space planning with terminal value estimates (Pinneri et al., 2021; Hansen et al., 2022). Our CEM-MPC baseline uses the same reconstructed ALRNN as the proposed method but plans anew at every time step. 

Model-based and offline RL MBRL learns a world model and uses it for planning or policy optimization (Sutton, 1990; Moerland et al., 2023). Latent world models such as the Dreamer framework show that compact state-space models can support policy learning through simulated rollouts (Hafner et al., 2019; 2020; 2025a;b). These methods deal well with high-dimensional observations, but usually assume either online interaction or sufficient logged state-action data. Offline MBRL addresses the resulting distribution-shift problem by penalizing model rollouts or policies that move away from the empirical state-action distribution (Yu et al., 2020; 2021). In our setting, however, no actions are observed, so there is no measured action distribution against which policy behavior can be regularized. We therefore use hierarchical parameter sharing as an implicit regularizer. Multitask and hierarchical reinforcement learning (RL) exploit shared structure across related control problems by sharing policy and value function parameters or conditioning them on task embeddings, thereby improving data efficiency and generalization. Hierarchy here refers to a parametric decomposition into shared and subject-specific components, not to temporal scales, levels of abstraction or option-based control (Dayan & Hinton, 1992; Barto & Mahadevan, 2003; Bacon et al., 2017). 

Reconstruction of families of dynamical systems DSR seeks generative models that learn the true underlying DS by reproducing attractor geometry and long-run temporal structure from time series (Durste-witz et al., 2023; 2026). ALRNNs retain a PWL structure with a parsimonious symbolic code, making local dynamical analysis feasible (Brenner et al., 2024a; Brenner & Koppe, 2026). Hierarchical DSR represents a family of systems through shared parameters and low-dimensional embeddings, which can improve estima-tion from limited per-system data and enable adaptation to unseen systems (Brenner et al., 2025; Vermani et al., 2025). We extend this framework from reconstruction to control by conditioning the policy and value function on the same embedding. 

Control in neuroscience and cross-modal interventions Adaptive neurostimulation provides a chal-lenging application that motivates our setting. Common methods optimize control policies directly, rather than learning a world model of the neural dynamics (Chandrabhatla et al., 2023; Krauss et al., 2021; Horn & Neumann, 2025). Closed-loop, personalized stimulation has to be designed under safety and data constraints that limit collecting interventional data (Ravivarapu et al., 2025). Recent work therefore learns stimulation policies in simulated neurophysiological environments (Kuzmina et al., 2025), or fits state-transition models to observational recordings and applies control theory to them for mechanistic insight (Emonds et al., 2026). In each case the effect of an action is supplied by a simulator or by an assumed perturbation model, as it is here. Our neural experiments constrain that assumption further: actions are restricted to neural decoder directions, so any change in predicted behavior must pass through the learned latent dynamics. 33 Methods 

3.1 Preliminaries and problem setting 

MBRL requires an environment model p(zt+1 , r |zt, a t). We define the reward r by penalizing distance from a target state. Our goal is therefore to construct the transition dynamics p(zt+1 |zt, a t) from short recordings of related systems in which no control actions at were observed. We learn each system’s dynamics without control actions, specify how actions perturb its state, and combine these components into an environment for policy learning.

Together, these components provide the environments in which we train the policy (see also figure 1a).

## 4 Experiments 

We first applied our approach to Lorenz-63 (Lorenz, 1963) and double-pendulum systems, where we could transfer the learned actions directly to the true underlying equations. We then examined whether a trained controller could generalize to unseen Lorenz systems using only action-free observations. Finally, we used neural and behavioral recordings to test in-silico whether the model could predict actions that suppress movement without directly changing the decoded behavioral variables. 

4.1 Evaluation and comparison methods 

For the synthetic benchmarks, we distinguished performance in the learned (world) model from performance on the true system. To test this transfer, we encoded the current true-system observation, obtained an action from the policy, and mapped it back to the permitted system coordinates. We then applied this perturbation to the true system and advanced its equations numerically. We used three comparisons to examine different parts of the approach. First, independent ALRNN-SAC 

learns a separate policy and value function for each system, using the same reconstructed environments, action constraints, and reward as the hierarchical controller. This comparison tests whether hierarchizing SAC parameters and reusing subject embeddings improves performance beyond learning each policy indepen-dently. Second, ALRNN-CEM-MPC uses the same reconstructed dynamics but chooses actions through online planning (Pinneri et al., 2021). This comparison asks how a policy learned in simulation performs against repeated online planning. Neither approach uses action-conditioned transitions from the true system for training. Third, we compared learning without actions against learning with actions, using two estab-lished baselines: DreamerV3 , which learns an explicit policy through imagined trajectories from controlled interactions (Hafner et al., 2025a), and TD-MPC , which selects actions through repeated planning in a learned model during deployment (Hansen et al., 2022). Generalization to unseen systems We next asked whether the controller could be reused when the underlying dynamics changed. We generated additional 64 Lorenz-63 systems by shifting parameters: (ρ, σ, β ) ∈ { 51 , 81 , 111 , 141 } × { 8.5, 9.5, 10 .5, 11 .5} × { 1.5, 2.5, 3.5, 4.5}. For each new system, we fitted only its subject vectors S(j) to action-free observations while keeping the shared dynamics parameters fixed (fig-ure 1b). As a baseline, we reused the embedding of the corresponding unshifted training system, without fitting a new embedding (one of its nearest neighbors on the parameter grid). On the true system the model achieved −0.036 ±0.002 using the newly fitted embeddings, and −0.046 ±0.005 using the training embeddings, yielding a significant improvement (paired t-test; t(9) = 6 .59 , p < 0.001). Thus, the hierarchical policy gener-alized to unseen systems through newly fitted embeddings, outperforming the unshifted-embedding baseline and achieving control performance comparable to that on training systems. 

Dynamics exploited by the policy To gain insights into the mechanisms underlying control, we exam-ined how the policy changed the dynamics within the reconstructed Lorenz-63 systems. Without actions, trajectories visited a wide range of LSRs (median of 50). Under control, in contrast, trajectories concentrated on a small subset of regions. Across subjects, 90% of time steps were spent in at most four (figures 2d and 8a), although this concentration was not imposed by the reward structure (since the decoder reads out from units that are not subject to the PWL partitioning), and thus rather represents a dynamical preference induced by the control. To characterize this preference, we analyzed the local linear dynamics within the occupied regions. For each LSR, we compute the FPs implied by the linearized controlled system (equation 12) and measure their distance to the target. We find that the LSRs preferentially occupied under control are those whose local dynamics admit FPs close to the target (figure 8b) and exhibit predominantly stable eigenvalues, indicating saddle-type structure with largely attractive manifolds (figure 8c). Double-pendulum benchmark We further evaluated the framework on a challenging family of 16 double-pendulum systems with varying masses and lengths. The state of this system consists of two angles and their angular velocities. Without damping or control, the dynamics are Hamiltonian and conserve energy, while exhibiting both regular and highly chaotic motion. To ensure continuity, we represented each angle by its sine and cosine and retained both angular velocities, yielding O = 6 observation dimensions. The models once more achieved low median KL divergence DKL = 6 .40 and Hellinger distance DH = 0 .22. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) projections of the subject embeddings revealed structure associated with mass and length ratios, which the world model extracted solely from the action-free time series (fig-ure 3a). This structure emerged although the equations of motion depend nonlinearly on these ratios. The control objective was to stabilize the double-pendulum in the upright position with both arms pointing up, an unstable FP of the uncontrolled dynamics. Actions were constrained to affect only the angular velocities. Hierarchical SAC again transferred better than independent SAC ( −9.74 ± 2.42 vs. −16 .82 ±7.78) and stabilized the true pendulum in 49 percent of the trials on average, from random initial states, trained on action-free recordings alone (figure 3b). CEM-MPC achieved higher rewards using the same reconstructions, suggesting a benefit of online planning on this task. Results for methods trained through interaction with the true system were mixed: DreamerV3 outperformed hierarchical SAC, whereas TD-MPC performed worse (Table 1). In contrast to Lorenz-63, all methods performed better on the true systems than in the world models. This suggests that the policies transfer successfully despite imperfect long-horizon world-model fidelity. A likely explanation is that the true system supplies physically consistent states at every step, whereas free-running world-model predictions accumulate model error that lowers the reward attainable in simulation. On unseen double-pendulum systems, controllers using newly fitted embeddings did not significantly differ in performance from controllers using the embeddings of the nearest training systems (paired t(9) = −1.30 , p =0.208). The shared policy thus maintained its performance on systems absent from policy training, although fitting system-specific embeddings yielded no additional benefit, unlike on Lorenz-63.

Control across recording sessions We first used 12 sessions from the longitudinal LINK dataset (Tem-mar et al., 2025), which contains neural recordings and finger kinematics from a macaque performing a center-out-reach task, over a period of more than three years. Spiking-band power was measured from 96 channels of Utah arrays in placed motor cortex (see Appendix B.5 for more details on the dataset). In this task, the macaque makes finger movements to match visually presented target positions. We treated each session as a subject and learned a shared reconstruction with four-dimensional subject vectors. The embeddings correlated with recording day ( r = 0 .85 , p < 0.001; figure 4a), suggesting that they captured systematic changes across recording time. The policy was trained to suppress finger movement toward the instructed goal position. Without actions, the model successfully reproduced finger movements in held-out recordings (figure 4b and Appendix B.6). With actions, it predicted reduced movement across sessions, achieving a median reward of −0.090 ± 0.002 (figures 4b and 12). The comparison shows that, in simulation, the shared controller can suppress predicted behavior across session-specific models while respecting the behavioral constraint. Because actions reach the recorded channels through the decoder, the model also determines how many independent directions are available for intervention. Only 10 of the latent dimensions are decoded, so all 96 channels lie in a 10-dimensional subspace, and two of its directions are reserved for the protected finger outputs. Each channel on which the action is forced to zero removes one of the remaining eight directions. Accordingly, action magnitude first increased as randomly selected channels were excluded, and control failed once eight channels were excluded (figure 4c). The rank of the decoder and the set of protected outputs thus determine in advance how many constraints on the intervention the system tolerates.

## 5 Discussion 

This work presents a hierarchical MBRL framework for learning control policies from action-free recordings, without controlled trajectories or direct interaction with the underlying systems. By combining hierarchical DSR with subject-conditioned SAC policies, the framework shares structure across related systems while preserving subject-specific variation through low-dimensional embeddings. The PWL world model further permits mechanistic analysis of control through occupied LSRs, induced FPs, and local stability properties, 12 extending previous uses of PWL recurrent models for interpreting nonlinear dynamics (Sussillo & Barak, 2013; Linderman et al., 2017; Smith et al., 2021). The Lorenz-63 and double-pendulum experiments demonstrate that policies trained strictly on uncontrolled, autonomous trajectories, within a hierarchical framework, can transfer to true systems. In both bench-marks, the hierarchical policy transferred more reliably than independently trained policies, although these performed as well or better in simulation. This suggests that parameter sharing reduces overfitting to subject-specific world-model inaccuracies. Beyond the gains in data efficiency and generalization known from hierarchical and multitask RL (Dayan & Hinton, 1992; Barto & Mahadevan, 2003; Bacon et al., 2017), parameter sharing plays an additional role here. Since action-free recordings provide no empirical action distribution to constrain the policy (Yu et al., 2020; 2021), the reduced capacity of the hierarchical policy may act as an implicit regularizer that limits specialization to inaccuracies of individual world models. The results on unseen Lorenz systems, that is, subject embeddings the controller was never trained on, further indicate that hierarchical training can help controllers generalize, and that learned embeddings capture dynamical variation relevant to control. On unseen double-pendulum systems, the shared policy maintained its performance, although newly fitted embeddings yielded no gain over those of the nearest training systems. The benefit of embedding-based adaptation may therefore depend on how densely the training systems sample the family of dynamics. Repeated online planning partly improved performance, which makes combining the hierarchical policy with planning at deployment a promising extension. The fact that the policy and value function remain fixed while only the subject-specific representation is adapted indicates that the learned embedding provides a common coordinate system in which both DSR and control can be performed, in line with prior work on transferring control policies on learned representations of system dynamics (Yu et al., 2017; Killian et al., 2017). The PWL structure also clarifies how the policies achieve control. Under control, trajectories concentrate within a small subset of the LSRs visited during autonomous dynamics. These target regions harbor FPs near the goal and exhibit locally attractive dynamics, revealing the mechanistic basis of policy behavior far better than reward values alone. In sum, beyond standard closed-loop neural control (Chandrabhatla et al., 2023; Horn & Neumann, 2025; Krauss et al., 2021; Emonds et al., 2026), our framework handles multimodal systems with mismatched intervention and target modalities. By projecting latent actions onto the behavioral decoder’s null space, we prevent direct behavioral manipulation, forcing changes to emerge entirely through learned neural–behavioral couplings. While learned policies successfully modify predicted hand trajectories with minimal shifts in neural summary statistics, these results reflect world-model simulations. They should be interpreted as proof-of-concept for model-based feasibility rather than validated physiological control. 

Limitations Four points limit the generality of our approach. First, the hierarchical parameterization places subject-specific dynamics in linear subspaces, which regularizes the model but may be too restrictive when systems differ nonlinearly. Second, reusing reconstruction embeddings for control assumes that the factors distinguishing subjects’ dynamics also determine appropriate control. Although supported by the present experiments, this correspondence need not hold in other settings (Lambert et al., 2020). Third, adaptation to unseen systems likely requires sufficient coverage of the system family and reconstruction performance. Finally, representing actions as additive latent perturbations permits decoder-based constraints when no actuator data are available, but leaves open how physical interventions couple to the true system, which distinguishes this work from most MBRL frameworks (Hafner et al., 2025a; Moerland et al., 2023).

22 B DSR Training Protocol 

The DSR model is trained by minimizing the mean squared error between predicted and ground truth tra-jectories using the RAdam optimizer (Liu et al., 2020), with subject-specific variance used for normalization. Training uses generalized teacher forcing, which linearly interpolates generated latent states with data-inferred states after loss computation. Since the latent state can be expanded with additional dimensions, the input dimension of the decoder does not necessarily correspond to the latent dimension and is therefore called the forcing dimension(Hess et al., 2023). For non-identity observation models, losses are computed in both latent and observation space, and an additional penalty enforces invertibility of the observation model (Brenner et al., 2022). Parameters are subject to L2-regularization that pushes selected entries of A toward 1 and entries of W and h toward zero (Schmidt et al., 2021). Subject vectors are initialized uniformly on [−1, 1], and group-level parameters use Xavier initialization (Glorot & Bengio, 2010). Reconstruction metrics are reported for the world model subsequently used for control. Further details on hierarchical DSR training are given in Brenner et al. (2025). 

B.1 Lorenz-63 data 

The Lorenz-63 system is a three-dimensional model of atmospheric convection that can exhibit stable fixed points, periodic orbits, and aperiodic chaotic dynamics depending on its parameters. Transitions between these regimes occur through bifurcations, including Hopf and homoclinic bifurcations. The system is defined by the coupled nonlinear ordinary differential equations ˙x = σ(y − x) (13) ˙y = x(ρ − z) − y

˙z = xy − βz 

where σ, ρ and β are positive parameters. For DSR training and testing, and for applying control to the true system, we integrate these equations using odeint from scipy.integrate at a step size of 0 .01 yielding 5000 training and 100000 test time steps per subject. Gaussian noise with a variance of 5% of the subject-specific data variance is added to the training data. The 64 subjects correspond to the parameter configurations (ρ, σ, β ) ∈ { 36 , 66 , 96 , 126 } × { 8, 9, 10 , 11 } × { 1, 2, 3, 4} (figure 13). The time series for all subjects were normalized together. For control, this transformation is reversed for the integration with the ODE.

The Long-term Intracortical Neural activity and Kinematics dataset (Temmar et al., 2025) contains record-ings from a single nonhuman primate performing a dexterous, two-degree-of-freedom finger movement task. It includes 312 sessions recorded on 303 days over a period of about 3.5 years (1242 days), with data organized by sessions containing around 375 trials each. With a sampling frequency of 50 Hz and recordings from 96 motor cortex channels of a Utah array, the observation dimension is 98 when including the two-dimensional finger positions. We only considered trials with randomly selected targets. The spiking band power was first low-pass filtered using a 5th-order Butterworth filter with a cutoff frequency of 8 Hz. Both finger position and velocity signals were smoothed using a Gaussian filter with a standard deviation of 8 time steps. Following filtering, all signals — both neural and kinematic — were normalized. Finally, the continuous time-series data was segmented into non-overlapping time series of 200 time steps each, forming the final input for our models. 

B.6 LINK training 

We randomly selected 12 of the 312 sessions and treat them as subjects for our hierarchical approach. For each subject, the world model is trained and validated on average on 100 time series per subject and tested on 25. Both the neural data (spiking band power) and the kinematic variables are modeled using a Gaussian decoder. A causal convolutional encoder which performs a sequence of one-dimensional convolutions and whose inputs are padded on the left so that future observations do not influence the current latent state, infers the mean and variance of the latent state (Brenner et al., 2022). In principle, encoders, decoders, and 

C can also be learned hierarchically, although in our case they are learned for each subject. The external input to the latent ALRNN is the target position for both finger groups. The target state for SAC is 0 for both the index and the MRS finger group. On the test set, the median mean squared error across subjects is 0 .81 for the neural data and 0 .09 for the kinematic data (cf. 4b).

In the delayed center-out reach task of Pei et al. We consider only mazes without barriers. Spikes are binned at 50 ms and hand and eye positions are normalized to [ −1, 1], giving an observation dimension of 186. 

B.8 Macaque delayed center-out reach training 

The model receives the go cue and the goal position as external inputs to the latent ALRNN: the go cue as a single pulse and the target position during its presentation. ## C SAC implementation 

The SAC implementation largely follows Haarnoja et al. (2018). The Q-functions and policy π are ReLU feedforward networks whose weights and biases are hierarchized as described in the main text. Two indepen-dent Q-networks are trained, and the minimum of their predictions enters the gradient computation. Each 

Q-network has a target network updated by Polyak smoothing. Since all rewards are non-positive, target values are clipped at a maximum of 0, which prevents divergence of the value function. The policy is a Gaussian distribution, which permits the reparametrization trick. The replay buffer is filled exclusively with trajectories generated by the world model (so-called imaginations ), and RL gradients do not propagate back into the world model. To improve robustness on the true system, Gaussian noise is added to the latent state at each generated time step, with its magnitude treated as a hyperparameter (Table 2). During evaluation, the expected values of both policy and the decoders are used. The temperature parameter is kept fixed and not optimized. The expectation over the decoder in the reward function (equation 5) is approximated with a single sample. To prevent actions from directly changing the outputs of goal decoders or other protected dimensions, a subject-specific projection maps latent actions onto the joint null space of the corresponding decoder weights. The relevant rows of the decoder mean weights are stacked, their null space is obtained via singular value decomposition (SVD), and an orthogonal projector onto this null space is formed. Actions are first tanh-squashed to [ −1, 1] and then projected. In experiments with external inputs, the policy additionally receives the current external input, which makes it time-dependent. 

## D Batching protocol 

We implement the training protocol as a measurement-aware batching hierarchy where each batch consists of multiple subbatches, with each subbatch containing samples exclusively from a single subject. For DSR, all subbatches of a batch pass through the encoder and latent dynamics in a single forward pass. Losses are computed per subbatch, averaged, and combined with the global regularization terms. A single backward pass optimizer step then update all model parameters jointly. For SAC, training alternates between two phases. The actor phase collects exactly one batch per epoch, generates actions and rewards through environment interaction, and stores transitions in a replay buffer with-28 out gradient updates. The critic phase then samples multiple batches from this replay buffer and performs separate gradient updates for Q-networks (minimizing 1-step Bellman errors) and policy (minimizing KL divergence) using distinct optimizers, followed by Polyak averaging of target networks after each batch, effec-tively decoupling exploration from exploitation while maintaining the measurement-aware subbatch structure throughout both phases for hierarchical parameter learning. 

## E Baseline implementation 

We compared the proposed hierarchical controller with three baselines that differed in how they use learned dynamics and access to controlled interactions. To ensure comparability, all methods were instantiated independently for each subject and used the same reward function, initial-state distribution, 500-step evaluation horizon, and projected action space. Raw actions were clipped and projected before execution. In the Lorenz-63 environment, only the projected y

and z components were injected into the physical dynamics; in the double-pendulum environment, only the projected components corresponding to the two angular velocities were applied. Thus, all baselines respected the same constrained physical action interface as the proposed method. CEM-MPC (Pinneri et al., 2021) was implemented as a derivative-free receding-horizon planner that operates exclusively in the pretrained subject-specific ALRNN world model. At each evaluation step, candidate projected action sequences were simulated in the ALRNN and ranked by the same reward function as the proposed method, only the first action of the selected sequence was applied to the true system. Thus, CEM-MPC required no additional controller training and did not use controlled true-system trajectories for planning. For CEM-MPC, seeds were planner seeds controlling the stochastic sampling of candidate action sequences; no model or controller parameters were trained. TD-MPC followed the task-oriented latent-dynamics framework of Hansen et al. (2022). For each subject, TD-MPC learned an encoder, latent transition model, reward predictor, twin Q-functions, and policy prior from controlled interactions collected in the true environment. Short-horizon action sequences were optimized in the learned latent model using an MPPI-style planner and evaluated with a learned terminal value estimate. The true environment was used only to execute the first selected action and provide subsequent observations; future true-system trajectories were never available to the planner. The DreamerV3 RSSM agent (Hafner et al., 2025a) was implemented with categorical stochastic latents, observation, reward, and continuation prediction heads, and actor-critic learning through imagined trajecto-ries. Like TD-MPC, DreamerV3 learned exclusively from controlled true-environment interactions and was evaluated in closed loop, with each action selected from the learned model before being applied to the true system. The reported configurations were fixed before final evaluation. DreamerV3 included a restricted validation-search procedure, whereas TD-MPC and CEM-MPC used fixed configurations.
