Abstract 

LLM agents that orchestrate frozen vision-language-action (VLA) policies improve across episodes through text memory, which records what the agent did but not how the task is done. A demonstration video shows it, but fits poorly into an agent’s context. The full video slows every turn, fixed keyframes lose the contact detail that decides whether a grasp holds, and what the agent needs shifts from the task’s struc-ture while planning to the frames around each contact. We introduce Recursive Video In-Context Learning (RV-ICL) , a training-free method that turns a demonstration into a hier-archy the agent navigates rather than a prompt it receives. The hierarchy is built from the sub-events of the demonstration, such as grasps and releases. Its levels grow finer, from keyframes of the whole task to phases, moments and short clips, and are exposed through read-only tools. The agent reads the coarse levels before plan-ning. During execution it re-enters the hier-archy whenever a step needs more detail and loads only the clip of its current sub-goal. One demonstration per task is enough. Built on RPent, RV-ICL raises success from 92.6% to 96.5% on LIBERO-PRO and from 86.7% to 95.8% on LIBERO-Plus. 

1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as the deliberative layer of robot systems. They decompose instructions, localise objects, and call learned visuomotor policies for the contact-rich steps they cannot perform analytically (Ahn et al., 2022; Liang et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2022). Recent agentic harnesses push this design further. HarnessVLA (Zhang et al., 2026c), released as the RPent framework, exposes a frozen VLA as one retryable primitive among a few analytic ones. A coding agent orchestrates them and improves

across episodes through text memory : recipes, lessons and failure rules. Under distribution shift, this design outperforms end-to-end VLAs by a wide margin. How far the agent can improve, however, is bounded by what text can record. The agent writes its memory from its own execution traces, so the memory describes its calls and their outcomes rather than the task itself. It can record that a grasp failed, but not what a successful execution looks like: which object is taken first, from which side the hand approaches, how high the object is lifted, when the gripper opens. Such knowledge is spatial and temporal, and it is hard to put into words. What is hard to put into words is easy to show: a video of the task captures all of it, frame by frame. Such a video is also cheap to obtain. A human demonstration only requires recording a person actually performing the task, with no ac-tion labels or other annotation. Large models can learn from demonstrations placed in their context, in text (Brown et al., 2020) and with interleaved images (Alayrac et al., 2022). This suggests letting the agent watch a demonstration of its task, much as a person watches a how-to video before trying something new. But in what form should the video enter the agent’s memory? The obvious answers fail for three reasons. (i) Length. A demonstration spans hundreds of frames, and the agent re-reads its whole context at each of its tens to hundreds of turns. Long contexts also degrade how well models use their content (Liu et al., 2024). (ii) Resolution. 

A few keyframes are affordable, but they drop the frames around each contact, which decide whether a grasp holds or a placement registers. (iii) Timing. 

What the agent needs changes over the episode: the task’s structure before it acts, and the frames around a particular contact once it reaches that step. Since each moment of the episode calls for a different part of the demonstration, why not let the agent watch just that part, and only at that moment? Methods for long documents and long videos al-ready take this route: instead of reading everything, the language model navigates a hierarchical in-dex (Sarthi et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Packer et al., 2023). We bring this idea to embodied agents, which decide from their own progress when to look closer: before a delicate contact, at the start of a new sub-goal, or after a step goes wrong. We build such an index over a demonstration and call the method Recursive Video In-Context Learning (RV-ICL) . The index is a tree whose levels grow finer: event-driven keyframes of the whole task, the phases between sub-events, the mo-ments around each sub-event, and short clips from any camera (Figure 1a). The agent reads the coarse levels before planning and does not collect clips up front. Whenever it needs more detail during exe-cution, the agent re-enters the tree at the node of its current sub-goal and pulls only the relevant clip (Figure 1b). The demonstration is thus consulted recursively, at the level of detail and the moment the agent’s progress calls for. 

RV-ICL requires no training, no change to the VLA, and one demonstration per base task. The hierarchy is built from the sub-events of the demon-stration, the instants at which one sub-task ends and the next begins. The same construction therefore applies to any task that decomposes into sub-tasks. We make the following contributions: • We introduce video in-context learning to tool-using robot agents: their text memory cannot record what a successful execution looks like, but a demonstration video shows it frame by frame (§3.1). • We propose Recursive Video In-Context Learning ( RV-ICL ), which lets the agent look only at the part of the demonstration its cur-rent step needs, by giving it recursive access 

to a hierarchy built from the demonstration’s sub-events (§3). • On LIBERO-PRO, RV-ICL raises the success rate of HarnessVLA from 92.6% to 96.5%, above every baseline, and on LIBERO-Plus from 86.7% to 95.8% across seven kinds of perturbation. On the long LIBERO-10 tasks it gains up to 12 points, where keyframes or the whole video of the same demonstration gain at most 2 (§5). 

2 Related Work 

2.1 LLM agents for robot manipulation 

Language models entered robotics as planners over pre-trained skills. SayCan (Ahn et al., 2022) scores LLM proposals by learned affordances, ProgPrompt (Singh et al., 2023) and Code as Poli-cies (Liang et al., 2023) write programs over per-ception and control APIs, and VoxPoser (Huang 2et al., 2023) composes 3D value maps for a mo-tion planner. Inner Monologue (Huang et al., 2022) feeds execution feedback back to the planner, and Voyager (Wang et al., 2023) accumulates a library of verified skills. A recent line of agentic harnesses puts a coding agent in charge of a frozen VLA. Har-nessVLA (Zhang et al., 2026c) exposes the VLA as a retryable primitive and learns its operating range from text memory; Robo-Harness K1 (Li et al., 2026) augments such agents with perception; CaP-X (Fu et al., 2026) benchmarks coding agents for manipulation; RATS (Zhang et al., 2026a) learns through play; and RAPID (Liu et al., 2026) synthe-sises agent programs from demonstrations. The knowledge these systems carry across episodes is textual or programmatic. RV-ICL 

keeps the harness unchanged and adds a visual record of how the task is performed, together with a protocol for consulting it. 

2.2 Learning from demonstrations in context 

In language models, a few demonstrations in the prompt suffice to specify a task (Brown et al., 2020), although models draw mostly on their format and label space rather than the exact input–label mapping (Min et al., 2022). Flamingo (Alayrac et al., 2022) extends in-context learning to interleaved images. In robotics, one-shot imitation has been learned by meta-learning from human videos (Yu et al., 2018) and by video-conditioned policies (Jain et al., 2024). In-context imitation has been achieved by tokenising demonstrations as keypoints for an off-the-shelf LLM (Di Palo and Johns, 2024), by train-ing a policy to continue interleaved demonstration trajectories (Fu et al., 2024), and by conditioning a VLA on structured demonstrations (Xu et al., 2026). When an LLM acts directly as the pol-icy, one-shot demonstrations yield no aggregate gain (Zhang et al., 2026b). All of these present the demonstration once, as a prefix for the next actions. Our agent makes tens to hundreds of decisions per episode, so the question is not only whether to show a demonstration but 

which part, and when .

2.3 Recursion in language-model reasoning and retrieval 

Recursion lets a bounded model handle inputs and problems that exceed its context. Recursive sum-marisation condenses a book into a tree of sum-maries read top-down (Wu et al., 2021), and RAP-TOR (Sarthi et al., 2024) retrieves from such a tree at the level of abstraction a question requires. For long videos, VideoAgent (Wang et al., 2024) and VideoTree (Wang et al., 2025) likewise inspect only the frames or branches a question needs. 

Recursive decomposition splits a problem as needed. ADaPT (Prasad et al., 2024) decomposes a sub-task further only when the executor fails on it, THREAD (Schroeder et al., 2025) spawns child threads that return only what the parent needs, and Recursive Language Models (Zhang et al., 2025) call themselves over slices of a context too long to read at once. Tree of Thoughts (Yao et al., 2023) searches over such trees of intermediate steps. A related family decides when to fetch infor-mation. FLARE (Jiang et al., 2023) retrieves when upcoming tokens are uncertain, and Self-RAG (Asai et al., 2024) learns to request retrieval on demand. Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023) and Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023) feed the outcome of an attempt into the next one, and MemGPT (Packer et al., 2023) pages information between a bounded context and external storage. 

RV-ICL brings recursion to an embodied agent. The demonstration is a tree read at the level each decision requires, and the agent descends whenever its progress in the physical task calls for more de-tail: before a delicate contact, at a new sub-goal, or after a step goes wrong.

3.2 The demonstration hierarchy 

A demonstration D = ( o1: T , x 1: T , g 1: T , s 1: T ) con-sists of multi-camera frames ot, end-effector poses 

xt, a binary gripper command gt and, when avail-able, object and joint states st. We segment D

at its sub-events E, the instants at which one sub-task ends and the next begins. Every task that decomposes into sub-tasks has such instants, so the construction below applies to it unchanged. Manipulation tasks expose their sub-events in the recorded state. An object is grasped when the gripper closes and released when it opens, and a drawer or door is opened or closed when its joint moves. We therefore take E to be the gripper events 

Eg = {t : gt̸ = gt−1} together with the instants at which an articulated fixture opens or closes. The hierarchy has four levels (Figure 1a). 

L0: task keyframes. A fixed number K of frames that span the whole task. They are placed around every grasp and release, and uniform sam-ples fill the rest. Each keyframe shows the third-person and wrist views and is captioned with its time fraction, gripper state, end-effector position and event label. 

L1: phases. The intervals pj = [ ej , e j+1 ) be-tween consecutive gripper events ej ∈ Eg, ren-dered as text . Each phase records its duration, the gripper state, the end-effector travel and lift, and, when object states are recorded, what it achieved: the object it moved and the region it ended in, or the fixture it opened or closed. A fixture event does not split a phase and is reported as part of what that phase achieved. The task’s goal predicates are listed with the phases, so each predicate can be matched to the phase that satisfies it. 

L2: moments. A text index of short windows around every sub-event and at the end of the episode. Each window is named by kind and order (grasp 1 , release 1 , close microwave ). 

L3: clips. A short sequence of frames sampled evenly from any phase or moment, from the third-person view, the wrist view or the two stacked. The hierarchy conveys how the task is done, not 

where . The demonstration was recorded in another scene, so the tool descriptions tell the agent to re-localise every object in its own cameras rather than read positions from the demonstration. 

3.3 Recursive access 

TD exposes the hierarchy through three calls. 

view_task_video (page ) returns L0 one page of keyframes at a time, view_task_map () returns L1

and the L2 index, and view_demo_clip (n, cam )

returns the L3 clip of a phase or moment n. The system prompt tells the agent how to use the three calls, and the agent decides when to make them. Access is recursive: the agent reads the coarse lev-els once and then, at each step that needs detail, returns to the hierarchy for one clip (Algorithm 1). 4Coarse levels before planning. Before its first action the agent reads L0 to L2. It writes its sub-goals from the phase map, with the keyframes as the picture of each stage. It loads no clip yet, be-cause the prompt asks for clips at the point of action rather than up front. 

Re-entry during execution. The agent returns to the hierarchy whenever its current sub-goal calls for more detail. It does so before it closes the gripper on an object or opens it, and when a stretch of motion is unclear. It also does so when a step does not go as planned, such as a grasp that does not close, an object that does not move, or a placement whose goal predicate does not fire. In that case it watches the clip before it changes any parameter or primitive. It does not reread the coarse levels. It maps the current sub-goal g to its phase pj and loads the clip of pj or of the moment it needs. It compares the clip with its current views and adjusts its next action. A re-entry adds one clip to the context, not the whole video. 

Bounding the recursion. The harness can bound the recursion with a viewing budget B per phase or moment. Further requests for the same phase or moment are refused with a message that sends the agent back to acting. A finite budget stops the agent from re-watching a step that a clip cannot fix, as in the failed episodes of §5.3.

4 Experimental Setup 

We first test whether RV-ICL improves a strong agentic system under distribution shift (§5.1) and whether recursive access beats showing the agent keyframes or the whole video (§5.2). We then examine how agents use the hierarchy (§5.3) and whether the gains hold on a second benchmark with other kinds of perturbation (§5.4). 

Benchmarks. Our main benchmark is LIBERO-PRO (Zhou et al., 2025), which perturbs the four LIBERO suites (Spatial, Object, Goal, LIBERO-10; Liu et al., 2023) by instruction redirection (T) and position swap (S). Each of the eight cells con-tains 10 tasks. We follow the evaluation protocol of Zhang et al. (2026c), whose reported baselines use 10 seeds per task. We evaluate RV-ICL with 5 seeds per task, so each of our cells aggregates 50 episodes. To test other kinds of perturbation we use LIBERO-Plus (Fei et al., 2025), which per-turbs the LIBERO tasks along seven dimensions: object layout, camera viewpoint, robot initial state, language, lighting, background and sensor noise. LIBERO-Plus contains 10,030 perturbed variants, so we evaluate a stratified subset of 120. It takes three variants of each of the 40 base tasks and ro-tates the seven dimensions over them, which gives 17 or 18 variants per dimension. Variants are drawn from the hardest difficulty levels available, and 105 of the 120 come from the two hardest of the five levels. Both methods use the same initial state for every variant. The agent receives the text memory of the unperturbed base task. On LIBERO-Plus we run HarnessVLA ourselves with the same agent. Since this protocol differs from the official one, these numbers compare the two methods with each other, not with the public leaderboard. 

Demonstrations. We build one hierarchy per LIBERO base task from the first of its human tele-operation demonstrations. The keyframes are re-rendered at 512 px from the recorded simulator states, and the clips use the 128 px frames recorded during teleoperation. Both are shown in the ori-entation of the agent’s own cameras (third-person and wrist), so that demonstration and live views are directly comparable. Replaying the recorded states also gives the object and joint states that L1 uses to state what each phase achieved. Both bench-marks use K=16 keyframes, and a clip holds at most 20 frames (§B). One hierarchy serves every perturbed variant of its base task in LIBERO-PRO and LIBERO-Plus. In the instruction-redirection (T) cells, whose goal differs from the demonstrated one, the agent is told which parts of the demonstra-tion no longer apply.

Metrics. The primary metric is task success rate, read from the simulator’s own goal check. On LIBERO-PRO the baseline numbers come from separate runs, so we test the gap in success rate pooled over the eight cells with a two-proportion z-test (§5.1). To study how agents use the hierarchy, we log every demonstration call with its level, the phase or moment it shows, its camera and its turn. We also measure the cost of an episode in wall-clock time and tokens (§D). 

Implementation. The agent is gpt-6-astra at low reasoning effort running in the Codex CLI, as in the strongest HarnessVLA configuration. 

RV-ICL does not depend on this choice. It only adds read-only tools and a prompt section to the harness and changes nothing specific to the model, so any agent the harness supports can use the same demonstration hierarchy, for example Claude Code with Claude Opus 5.5 (§C). The VLA primitive is 

π0.5 fine-tuned by RLinf on LIBERO and served locally, and SAM 3 (Carion et al., 2026) provides segmentation. Each episode is a single attempt.

6 Conclusion 

We presented Recursive Video In-Context Learning (RV-ICL ), which gives an LLM robot agent one demonstration of its task as a hierarchy it navigates: coarse levels ground the plan, and during execution the agent returns to the demonstration at the node of its current sub-goal whenever it needs more detail. The approach needs no training and no change to the policy. The hierarchy is built from the sub-events of the demonstration, so it applies to any task that decomposes into sub-tasks. On LIBERO-PRO it raises the success rate of HarnessVLA from 92.6% to 96.5%, most of all on the long LIBERO-10 tasks, where keyframes or the whole video add little. Agents re-enter the demonstration in 97% of episodes, mostly to see a grasp or a release. On LIBERO-Plus RV-ICL holds up under all seven 8kinds of perturbation, and it carries over to a second planner without change. More broadly, treating demonstrations as an environment to be queried, rather than as a prefix, is a natural fit for agents whose information needs change as they act. 

Limitations 

RV-ICL assumes one demonstration per base task; when a benchmark rewrites the goal of a task, parts of the demonstration become invalid and must be flagged to the agent. On LIBERO we detect sub-events from the gripper and from fixture joints. Tasks whose sub-tasks leave no such trace in the recorded state, such as pushing, wiping or pour-ing, need another detector, for example one that reads the end-effector trajectory or asks a vision-language model. When contact is executed by a learned skill whose motion differs from the demon-strator’s, the finer levels carry little that the agent can act on. Each demonstration view stays in context for the rest of the episode, so cost grows with the number of re-entries. All our experiments use LIBERO tasks with a single robot and a single VLA, so other embodiments and demonstration sources re-main untested. Our agents are proprietary models accessed through third-party endpoints, whose be-haviour may change over time. Our LIBERO-PRO cells hold 50 episodes and each LIBERO-Plus di-mension 17 or 18 variants on one seed, so a single episode moves a cell by two to six points. The base-lines in Table 1 come from separate runs, so they cannot be compared with ours episode by episode. 

Ethics Statement 

All experiments are conducted in simulation. The LLM agents run shell commands and could read files outside their task; we isolated conditions by restricting directory permissions and audited every shell command afterwards. Deploying such agents on physical robots requires stronger sandboxing and human oversight. Demonstrations are taken from public benchmark datasets.
