## ABSTRACT 

Practical Reinforcement Learning (RL) algorithms learn to solve Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) through iterative policy improvement in the presence of approx-imate evaluation. We study policy improvement from first principles, defining optimal policy improvement as producing the best policy attainable in a single update under specified constraints. We show that optimal improvement restricted to a set of states is equivalent to solving an induced MDP, characterizing planning with an explicit or implicit model as a path towards optimal policy improvement. Because practical methods commonly solve such induced problems through iter-ative improvement in the form of greedification, we take steps towards optimal greedification under the central practical constraint of approximate evaluation. We formulate greedification under this constraint as probabilistic decision-making under uncertainty and derive a novel operator that is optimal with respect to the resulting objective. Empirically, the operator and its practical gradient-based ap-proximations improve aggregate performance across GumbelAlphaZero, SAC, ReBRAC and Generalized Policy Iteration, in experiments spanning discrete and continuous actions, model-based and model-free, online and offline RL. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

The objective of Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents is to find well-performing policies for se-quential decision problems, formalized as Markov Decision Processes (MDPs, Bellman, 1957). The performance of the policy is measured as its expected return , also called the policy’s value . To find such policies, practical algorithms rely on the (approximately) monotonic learning process of iterative 

Policy Improvement (Sutton & Barto, 2018). Examples of algorithms that can be abstracted to this process are (Deep) Q-learners (Watkins & Dayan, 1992; Mnih et al., 2015), Actor-Critics (Konda & Tsitsiklis, 1999), learners-in-the-model (Hafner et al., 2023), planning-based learners (Silver et al., 2018; Schrittwieser et al., 2020) and many others. To drive policy improvement, RL algorithms use 

Policy Improvement Operators which take the agent’s current policy and produce a better policy (Oren et al., 2025b; Chan et al., 2022). Prominent examples of policy improvement operators are Policy Gradient (Sutton et al., 2000), Monte Carlo Tree Search (Kocsis & Szepesv´ ari, 2006) and the greedy operator used by Q-Learning. A central design question in RL is which policy improvement operator best uses the information available to the agent under practical constraints. Approximate evaluation is particularly important 1arXiv:2610.01566v1 [cs.LG] 1 Oct 2026 Preprint because of both its prevalence and its adverse effects on learning (Baird, 1995; Tsitsiklis & Roy, 1997; Thrun & Schwartz, 2014; van Hasselt, 2010; van Hasselt et al., 2016; 2018). Popular algorithms mitigate the effects of evaluation error on policy improvement by constraining policy updates through different forms of regularization (Schulman et al., 2017; Haarnoja et al., 2018; Abdolmaleki et al., 2018; Danihelka et al., 2022; Hafner et al., 2023). These methods implicitly encode decisions about how uncertain evaluation should affect improvement. Making those decisions explicit and deriving operators that optimize the resulting objective remains an important avenue for research. In this work we take steps towards optimal policy improvement under practical constraints such as approximate evaluation, in two central parts. First, we characterize optimal improvement under state-restricted policy updates. We show that constrained optimal improvement is equivalent to solving an induced MDP. These results connect optimal policy improvement to planning with explicit models such as world models and implicit models such as replay buffers. To solve the MDP in the model, practical RL algorithms rely on greedification for policy improvement (Sutton & Barto, 2018; Oren et al., 2025b). This motivates the second part of our analysis: optimal greedification under approximate evaluation. We model this problem explicitly as a problem of optimization under uncertainty formulated as Maximum a Posteriori optimization, and derive Iopt , a novel greedification operator which is optimal for the derived optimization objective. Iopt generalizes an operator arising from Regularized Policy Optimization (Neu et al., 2017), Control as Inference (Levine, 2018), Mirror Descent (Tomar et al., 2022), and Trust Region Policy Optimization (Schulman et al., 2015), and used by popular algorithms such as GumbelAlpha/MuZero (Danihelka et al., 2022) and TDMPC variants (Hansen et al., 2024). Theoretically, we investigate the soundness of Iopt for driving the RL process by establishing convergence in a Dynamic Programming setup underlying the general learning process of RL (Oren et al., 2025b). Empirically, Iopt and its practical gradient-based approximations improve performance across experiments encompassing RL and Dynamic Programming, discrete and continuous action spaces, model based and model free, classical and modern, online and offline algorithms. These include GumbelAlphaZero (Danihelka et al., 2022) in 9x9 Go and Chess , Soft Actor-Critic (Haarnoja et al., 2018) in 10 classical DeepMind Control (Tunyasuvunakool et al., 2020) continuous-action environments, ReBRAC (Tarasov et al., 2023) in 4 classical offline RL domains of D4RL (Fu et al., 2020) and Policy Iteration algorithms (Sutton & Barto, 2018) across infinite horizon grid environments. These results support the design of improved policy improvement operators as a promising basis for better performing RL algorithms, and in addition, the construction of explicit, uncertainty-aware decision objectives as a promising basis for designing stronger policy improvement operators.

## 5 CONNECTIONS TO PRIOR WORK 

Iopt generalizes the widely used shift-invariant Ireg operator parameterized by η:

Ireg (π, Q )( a|s) ∝ π(a|s) exp ( Q(s, a )/η ) ∝ π(a|s) exp ( A(s, a )/η ) .

Ireg itself emerges independently from several seminal directions: Regularized Policy Optimization 

(Neu et al., 2017); Control as Inference and “Optimal Control” (Levine, 2018; Kappen et al., 2012), which interpret π′(s) = Ireg (π, Q π′

> sof t

)( s) as a “soft-optimal” policy and motivate SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2018), MPO (Abdolmaleki et al., 2018) and TDMPC (Hansen et al., 2022; 2024); The Natural Policy Gradient (Kakade, 2001), Trust Region Policy Optimization (Schulman et al., 2015) and Mirror Descent (Tomar et al., 2022) whose improvement steps have all been shown to connect to Ireg (Neu et al., 2017; Xiao, 2022). For additional detail see Appendix C. In view of Iopt , the regularization term used by Regularized Policy Optimization induces a prior over candidate policies. From the perspective of Control as Inference, Ireg is most directly interpreted as a form of optimal improvement , rather than as approximating a globally optimal policy . Moreover, 

1/η (s, a ) can be interpreted as an action-dependent step size which accounts for the uncertainty in the improvement signal. Conversely, when we do not have any information about the evaluation error we can model the uncertainty as uniformly bounded by a constant, use σ2ˆA(s, a ) = σ2 conservatively, reducing η(s, a ) to η and Iopt to Ireg , which thus can be viewed as optimal with respect to the same decision objective as Iopt , when we are uninformed about error. Gumbel-AlphaZero/MuZero (GA/MZ) (Danihelka et al., 2022) use Ireg within MCTS, where the coefficient 1/η (s) increases with N (s, a ), the number of evaluations at each node in the search tree: 1/η (s) = ( cvisit + max a N (s, a )) cscale . This heuristic mechanism is inspired by the original MCTS assumptions which induce N (s, a ) ∝ 1/σ 2ˆQπ (s, a ) and increases as evaluation error reduces (Kocsis & Szepesv´ ari, 2006). Rather than using N (s, a ) directly which does not guarantee policy improvement, GA/MZ use max a N (s, a ) ∝ max a 1/σ 2ˆQπ (s, a ): a per-node surrogate for a quantity that is inherently per-action . Iopt can circumvent this heuristic and use N (s, a ) per action directly. Several previous works incorporate uncertainty into policy optimization for specific algorithms or sources of uncertainty. POMBU (Zhou et al., 2020) uses Q-value uncertainty to make PPO updates more conservative. Queeney et al. (2021) (UA-TRPO) make TRPO robust in the estimated policy gradient to uncertainty sourced from finite sampling. UWAC (Wu et al., 2021), specifically addressing offline RL, uses epistemic uncertainty to down-weigh the contribution of out-of-distribution predic-tions in actor-critic training. Hui et al. (2023) model Q-function evaluation error probabilistically and derive a modified Q-learning objective. In contrast, we study policy improvement at the operator level rather than within a particular algorithmic framework. We formulate greedification under uncertain evaluation as an explicit optimization problem and derive an operator that is optimal for the resulting uncertainty-aware objective under the stated probabilistic modeling and approximation choices. The operator admits convergence guarantees in the analyzed GPI setting and can be incorporated modularly into algorithms with different policy-improvement mechanisms (see Section 6). 

## 6 EXPERIMENTS 

Iopt exactly solves the decision objective derived in Equation 16. Our experiments investigate whether optimizing this local decision objective indeed facilitates improvement in the broader 7Preprint iterative learning process. We ask two questions. First, does Iopt improve sample efficiency in a controlled Policy Iteration setting that matches its evaluation assumptions ? Second, do Iopt and its gradient-based practical approximations improve established RL algorithms when uncertainty must be estimated during training ? We study these questions across Dynamic Programming, model-based and model-free RL, discrete and continuous actions, and online and offline learning.

Ireg requires an estimate of Qπ (s, a ) for each state and action, which traditional MCTS architectures do not provide. GumbelMCTS instead uses the completed Q-values mechanism, which substitutes a prediction of V π (s) for Qπ (s, a ) for unsearched actions, composed of the predictions of all searched actions weighted under π. This substitution removes action-specific information, since every unsearched action receives the same value prediction. When action-wise prediction errors contain independent or weakly correlated components, this averaging reduces their variance. Moreover, 8Preprint actions with greater policy support are generally searched or played, and consequently trained, more frequently; weighting by π therefore tends to emphasize better-supported estimates while assigning less weight to weakly supported, higher-error actions. Consistent with this reasoning, using the empirical proxy described in Appendix F.3, we observe lower estimated error variance for the V π -based predictions in our experiments. We therefore hypothesize that Iopt , which is designed to exploit action-specific Qϕ predictions while accounting for their estimated uncertainty, will benefit more than baseline GAZ from incorporating a Qϕ critic. Such a critic is inexpensive to incorporate as a small additional head on the shared-body DNN of GAZ. ## 7 CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

In this work, we took steps towards practical RL algorithms driven by optimal policy improvement operators. We characterized optimal policy improvement, showing that optimal improvement is itself equivalent to solving MDPs and fundamentally connecting optimal policy improvement to planning with an explicit and implicit model. To solve the MDP in the explicit or implicit model, practical methods rely on greedification , which lead us to focus on optimal greedification under practical constraints. We formulated greedification under approximate evaluation as probabilistic decision-making under uncertainty and derived a tractable decision objective. Solving for this objective, we derived Iopt , a novel greedification operator which is optimal with respect to the probabilistic decision objective. We established convergence for algorithms driven by Iopt in a Generalized Policy Iteration setting and shown that Iopt generalizes a popular greedification operator in literature, Ireg , extending it with explicit accounting for evaluation uncertainty. Empirically, Iopt 

and its practical gradient-based approximations improved aggregate performance over the respective GumbelAlphaZero, SAC, and ReBRAC baselines in board games, online continuous control, and 9Preprint offline continuous control, respectively. In a noised Policy Iteration experiment which coincides with the assumptions under which Iopt was designed, the novel operator outperformed all other tested operators. Iopt presents a new, attractive choice for greedification, unified across different algorithms, and lays a path towards optimal policy improvement through the design of operators which are optimal with respect to practical RL constraints. Rational Choice Theory is a foundational framework widely popular in the fields of economics, political science, and behavioral sciences for modeling complex, goal-oriented decision-making (Becker, 1976). Rational Choice Theory posits that decision-makers consistently select actions that maximize their subjective preferences (Arrow, 2012). Under conditions of uncertainty, this is formalized by Expected Utility Theory (EUT), which demonstrates that a rational agent’s preferences can be mathematically represented by the expectation of a scalar utility function over possible outcomes (von Neumann & Morgenstern, 2007; Savage, 2012). To operationalize EUT within probabilistic frameworks, deterministic utility maximization is standardly relaxed into probabilistic choice models. Rooted in Luce’s choice axiom (Luce et al., 1959) and random utility theory (McFadden, 1973), these models construct likelihood functions where the probability of selecting an alternative is proportional to its exponentiated utility. This approach allows us to model policy optimization as reasoning over which policy π′ is pre-ferred based on its expected utility Aπ′,π (s). In this section we further discuss the suitability of a Gaussian distribution to model the evaluation error, beyond its advantages for analytical tractability, and how we estimate the error in practice. As discussed in Section 4, Hui et al. (2023) motivate Gaussian TD error through the Central Limit Theorem and empirically find a moment-matched heteroscedastic Gaussian to provide a suitable approximation throughout training. More generally, evaluation error in modern RL can arise from stochastic returns, bootstrapped targets, sampled actions and function approximation. These sources need not be independent or exactly Gaussian, so we use the heteroscedastic Gaussian as an approxi-mation rather than an exact description of modern evaluation error. The better we can estimate the resulting state-action evaluation uncertainty, the better Iopt can be expected to perform. Uncertainty estimation remains an active and major field of research for AI, ML and RL, and we expect the potential of Iopt to grow with the quality of the estimator. To strengthen the significance of the contribution of Iopt to RL, in this work we incorporate Iopt into algorithms that already use some form of uncertainty estimation, and rely on this source - even when it is rudimentary. In Policy Iteration, we noise the evaluation with Gaussian noise with known variance, and provide the agent with this exact variance. As expected, Iopt is the best performer in this setting. In model-based RL, we incorporate Iopt into MCTS. Classical MCTS estimates action values from sample means of bounded Monte-Carlo returns (Kocsis & Szepesv´ ari, 2006). Under fixed-distribution independent sampling, these means are unbiased, their variance decreases as 1/N (s, a ), and their error becomes asymptotically Gaussian. Modern DNN-based MCTS (Silver et al., 2018; Schrittwieser 28 Preprint et al., 2020) departs substantially from this idealized sampling setting yet remains highly successful, suggesting that exact satisfaction of these assumptions is not necessary for the corresponding design principles to remain useful. We therefore use σ2ˆQπ (s, a ) ∝ 1/N (s, a ) as a simple variance proxy and directly examine the resulting DNN residuals in Figure 6. In model free RL (SAC and ReBRAC) we use the two-critic value ensemble. Value ensembles are a standard approach for value-uncertainty estimation in RL (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017; Gawlikowski et al., 2023) and as we see, they are sufficient to enable Iopt to provide improvement over the baseline, both in offline as well as online RL (Figures 1 and 7, and Table 1). B.7 Iopt AND EXPLORATION 

The operator Iopt can be expected to have an effect on the exploration of RL algorithms along multiple lines. Due to the complexity of exploration as its own independent research field, we divide the discussion into three parts. First, we will motivate why exploration does not need to be considered by Iopt at all. Second, we will motivate what effects Iopt could have on exploration. Last, we will discuss how exploration should be incorporated into algorithms utilizing Iopt .B.7.1 WHY WE NEED NOT WORRY ABOUT THE EFFECTS Iopt HAS ON EXPLORATION 

Although the simplest and most popular approach is to use trained exploitation policy πθ to act in the environment, and implicitly to explore, there is no principle necessitating that. πθ need not be used for exploration at all. Oren et al. (2025a) showed that one could use in a principled manner a policy trained explicitly for exploration to benefit the exploitation policy learned by πθ .Due to this principled separation between policies, we need not even consider explicitly the effects 

Iopt has on exploration - because the policy it is used to train πθ need not be used for exploration at all. B.7.2 WHAT EFFECTS Iopt could HAVE ON EXPLORATION 

Of course, in practice, the simplest approach would be to replace the existing greedification operator used by an RL algorithm with Iopt and act with the trained exploitation policy πθ in the environment (which is indeed how many popular, practical algorithms work in practice (Danihelka et al., 2022; Hansen et al., 2024; Haarnoja et al., 2018)). Despite not being the only alternative, it is popular in practice for several reasons. Not only is it simpler, but in complex environments such as Chess or Go it is not immediately clear which principled exploration policy is best suited, and acting with the trained policy is known to work well in practice (Silver et al., 2018; Danihelka et al., 2022). From this perspective, it may still be interesting to reason over what effects Iopt could have on exploration. We provide a detailed set of conjectures below: 1. Over-estimation bias should reduce, because the operator is more stable, because it prop-agates less error, because it favors high-error values less. As a result of reduced over-estimation bias, exploration may reduce. 2. During acting, exploration may also be reduced as a result of not favoring high-error, high-value actions. 3. However, because high -error, low -value actions are less unfavored, exploration can overall 

increase , as well. Due to these conflicting possible effects, it is hard to predict whether acting with πθ trained with Iopt 

increase, decrease or have other effects on exploration in aggregate. However, due to the last point in this list, we would expect Iopt in practice to simply induce a better learning behavior over algorithms that do not employ explicit directed exploration in the majority of domains. This result is supported by the results of our experiments in online RL (GumbelAlphaZero and SAC). B.7.3 HOW TO UTILIZE Iopt FOR PRINCIPLED EXPLORATION 

As shown by Oren et al. (2025a), a principled approach to training an exploration policy πe is using policy improvement operators. However, instead of the improvement being with respect to Q or 29 Preprint

The random variable is the model M , and the uncertainty stems from the model not necessarily being aligned with reality. We refer to Oren et al. (2025a) for more detail. E.2 DISCRETE -A CTION MODEL BASED RL (G UMBEL ALPHA ZERO ON BOARD GAMES )GumbelAlphaZero uses GumbelMCTS to drive policy improvement and action selection in RL. The GumbelMCTS algorithm is a modern variant of the MCTS algorithm which uses greedification operators directly within the search to drive policy improvement at the root. In broad strokes, MCTS is an iterative algorithm that constructs a search tree and uses statistics to direct its search towards increasingly more promising directions. In each iteration, the algorithm traverses the tree from the root using a search policy πi = Ireg (πθ , Q i), where πθ , often called the prior policy , the policy trained by the RL algorithm, and Qi is the aggregated value observed during search. When search arrives at an as-of-yet un-expanded action a at a node sT (a leaf in the tree), the node is expanded, evaluating 

sT +1 ∼ P (·| sT , a ), Q i(sT , a ) = rT + γV ψ (sT +1 ). The reward and transition models are both 32 Preprint usually assumed to be deterministic. After the expansion step, the new information rT + γV ψ (sT +1 )

observed in the expansion is backpropagated throughout the entire search trajectory all the way back to the root, the iteration has completed, and the next iteration starts from the top. The number of iterations, which is also the number of expansions, M , is the common compute budget considered for MCTS, as in many cases the DNN forward-pass-based expansion is the cost’s bottleneck. The GumbelAlphaZero (GAZ) algorithm is a natural test-bed for Iopt : its visit counts provide a natural state-action evaluation-variance estimator under the classical sample-mean interpretation of MCTS and its DNN-based evaluations test Iopt in fully the modern deep-RL setting. Finally, it already has a clear interpretation as using iterative greedification within the tree for policy improvement outside the tree, with Ireg .

Evaluation in board game, a standard, hard, benchmark domain for RL and MCTS, is very expensive: the agents take relatively large number of steps before they have well functioning policies. To achieve high-quality training curves, the evaluation requires comparing every checkpoint to every other checkpoint across a large number of games, where each agent uses MCTS variants with the checkpoint’s DNN. Due to the high cost of this evaluation, we run two different experiments, answering two faces of the same question - how does GumbelAlphaZero perform when Ireg is replaced with Iopt ?

In the first experiment we compare 4 agents: with and without a Qϕ critic, and with and without Iopt 

in place of Ireg . This experiment allows us to evaluate which operator, in and out of the presence of the additional critic, produces better learning? This is the experiment presented in Figure 1. This answers the question what is the direct contribution of each operator to action selection - playing strength - when all else is equal? We also repeat this experiment across different planning budgets M and in two environments: chess and 9x9 Go, allowing us to answer this question much more comprehensively. Each searched action is weighted by p1 + N (s, a ), i.e. by inverse standard error rather than inverse variance. Precision weighting (1 + N ) is maximum-likelihood only for unbiased targets, and these are not: an action is most-visited partly because its early estimates looked good, so its backup carries a winner’s curse that does not shrink like 1/N . Bootstrapped actions get unit weight; their noise is that of Vψ (s′) and is not on the √1 + N scale. The weighted regression constrains only actions that received a target, so we add one scalar constraint per state: the improved policy at the root πsearch -weighted mean of Qϕ must match the same mean over the tree’s completed values, with the target treated as a constant, providing some learning signal to all actions. The total loss is L = Lπ + Lv + λq Lq , with Lπ the cross-entropy against the improved policy over legal actions, Lv the squared error on the value head, and Lq the sum of the weighted regression and the calibration term. ## G HYPERPARAMETERS 

G.1 HYPERPARAMETER TUNING 

To keep the comparisons as meaningful as possible, we use each baseline’s pre-tuned hyperparameters and network architecture unchanged for both the baseline and its +Iopt variant, apart from the additional Iopt hyperparameters. This controls the comparison by changing only the operator. It also leaves open the possibility that the +Iopt variants could perform better if the baseline hyperparameters were retuned jointly with τ 2 and β.As a result, outside of the policy iteration experiments, where all hyperparameters were tuned from scratch, the only hyperparameters that were tuned in this work are Iopt ’s τ 2, β . To further adhere to each algorithm’s standard tuning conditions τ 2, β are tuned per environment for algorithms which are tuned per-environment (ReBRAC), and one set of hyperparameters τ 2, β is tuned and used for an entire domain for the algorithms which are tuned in this way (all the rest: SAC, GumbelAlphaZero and the Policy Iteration experiments). In the Policy Iteration experiments, all hyperparameters for all algorithms were tuned with an extensive grid search across the 20 environments and the single set of hyperparameters that performed best was chosen. The experiment was then repeated for 100 seeds in all environments and only the repeated seeds are shown. GumbelAlphaZero uses the same set of GumbelMCTS hyperparameters across different board games, for which it was tuned. Following this choice, we have used one set of hyperparameters in all environments. To tune τ 2 and β we have chosen parameters that induce growth 1 

> η(s,a )

with N (s, a ),

We’ve then conducted an elo evaluation sweep across hyperparameter combinations around these values with M = 64 in 9x9 Go, chosen one set of uniformly well performing hyperparameter, and repeated all experiments with them. The SAC baseline uses one set of hyperparameters across all environments. In accordance with this choice, we’ve done a sweep across hyperparameter combinations for Iopt across a subset of the environments. The single set of dominating hyperparameters was chosen and then the experiment was repeated for 10 seeds in all environments. In the offline RL experiments, the baseline’s hyperparameters were tuned per environment. Following the baselines choice, τ 2, β were tuned using a sweep across hyperparameter combinations per environment. The best performing hyperparameter combination was chosen per environment and the experiments were repeated for 20 additional seeds for this set of hyperparameters.
