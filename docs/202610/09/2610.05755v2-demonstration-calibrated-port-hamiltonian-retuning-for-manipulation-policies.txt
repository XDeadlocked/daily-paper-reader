Abstract 

Diffusion and VLA policies for manipulation are often deployed through downstream impedance controllers. The stiffness and damping gains of these controllers affect task success, yet are commonly inherited from data collection rather than selected for the deployed policy. Although empirical gain sweeps can improve performance, they require repeated evaluation rollouts. We introduce PHRetune, an offline method that derives controller gains for a frozen policy without evaluation rollouts or gain search. Our approach learns a port-Hamiltonian model from demonstrations to estimate the effort and energy associated with the policy’s predicted actions. The policy is applied to recorded demonstration observations, and its predictions are assessed against demonstration-derived effort and energy budgets. From this comparison, we derive a single gain scale in closed form, adjusting the downstream controller while preserving the policy and its action representation. The gains are fixed before evaluation, without requiring a prior manipulator model, task rewards, policy retraining, or additional runtime computation. Across LIBERO suites, PHRetune improves Diffusion Policy success by up to 9.4 percentage points, with the derived gains achieving the highest observed success rates in empirical gain sweeps. On all four real-world manipulation tasks, PHRetuned Diffusion Policy outperforms the nominal policy, alternative gain-tuning methods, and a policy-retraining baseline. The same procedure improves success with SmolVLA and OpenVLA-OFT on every task, while reducing acceleration and jerk for both VLA backbones. 

# 1 Introduction 

The behavior of a learned manipulation policy depends not only on its predicted actions, but also on the controller that executes them. Diffusion and vision-language-action (VLA) policies often command end-effector targets through a downstream impedance or operational-space controller [1, 2, 3]. The controller’s stiffness and damping therefore shape how policy predictions are realized as motion and interaction forces [4, 5]. Yet these gains are commonly inherited from teleoperated data collection or simulator defaults rather than selected for the deployed policy [6, 7, 8]. Recent studies show that this choice materially affects policy learning and execution, and that more compliant settings can improve task success [9, 10]. Prior work addresses this issue either by incorporating compliance into policy learning or by tuning the downstream controller. Variable-impedance actions, explicit compliance predictions, and learning from intent–execution mismatch modify the action representation or training procedure rather than retuning the controller of an unchanged policy [10, 11, 12]. Controller-side approaches instead optimize tracking through a dynamics model, prescribe stiffness from demonstration variability, or search directly over evaluation rollouts [9, 13, 14]. These approaches respectively rely on a model-based tracking objective, omit the deployed policy’s predictions, or require execution feedback to select gains. Our goal is to derive a fixed gain tailored to a frozen policy using its task demonstrations, without requiring a prior manipulator model, policy retraining, or gain-selection rollouts. We introduce PHRetune (pronounced “free-tune"), an offline method that derives controller gains for a frozen policy from its existing task demonstrations, without additional robot interaction for gain selection. # 2 Related Work 

Controller Gains in Policy Learning. Visuomotor manipulation policies, including diffusion policies and VLA models, commonly predict joint- or end-effector targets that are executed by lower-level controllers [1, 2, 16, 17]. Their closed-loop behavior therefore depends on controller gains, although benchmark environments typically treat these gains as fixed parameters rather than quantities selected for the deployed policy [6, 7, 8, 18, 19, 20]. One line of work incorporates compliance into policy learning. VICES jointly commands Cartesian motion and impedance [11], while related methods learn variable-impedance profiles from task experience or demonstrations [21, 22]. Adaptive Compliance Policy predicts compliance alongside diffusion-generated motion [12], and Mind the Gap learns implicit impedance from intent–execution mismatch [10]. These approaches modify the action representation or training procedure to learn compliant behavior, rather than retuning the downstream controller of an unchanged policy. A complementary line tunes the downstream controller. DiffTune differentiates a tracking objective through a dynamics model [13], while demonstration-based rules map motion variability to stiffness [14]. Tune-to-Learn empirically evaluates how controller gains affect policy learning and execution, demonstrating that gain selection can substantially alter task performance [9]. These approaches select gains through tracking objectives, demonstration statistics, or evaluation rollouts. PHRetune instead uses a demonstration-calibrated energy model to assess the frozen policy’s predictions on recorded observations against demonstration-derived effort and energy budgets. This assessment determines a fixed gain scale while preserving the policy, its action representation, and the controller structure. Gain selection therefore requires no policy retraining nor robot or simulator rollouts. 

Impedance, Passivity, and Port-Hamiltonian Foundations. Impedance control specifies the dynamic relationship between motion and interaction force around a virtual equilibrium [4], while operational-space control formulates motion and force control directly in task coordinates [5]. When a learned policy commands this equilibrium, its setpoint updates can supply energy to the controlled system rather than serving solely as kinematic targets. Port-Hamiltonian modeling describes this interaction through stored energy, dissipation, and power-conjugate ports [15]. Under the linear impedance model considered here, spring effort and setpoint velocity form a power-conjugate pair. This representation provides a common basis for relating action attenuation and controller softening through their effects on the energy associated with setpoints. These foundations also underpin energy-shaping and passivity-based control. Interconnection and damping assignment passivity-based control (IDA-PBC) shapes the closed-loop storage and dissipation structure through feedback synthesis [23], whereas energy-tank methods regulate energy exchange during online variations in stiffness or interaction [24, 25]. PHRetune addresses a different design problem. It neither synthesizes a new feedback law nor enforces a runtime energy budget, but uses a demonstration-calibrated energetic model to derive fixed gains for 2an existing impedance controller before deployment. The port-Hamiltonian formulation thus supports an offline retuning rule tailored to a frozen policy, with gain selection remaining blind to evaluation outcomes. 

# 3 Method 

Given a frozen diffusion or vision-language-action policy, its task demonstrations, and a downstream operational-space impedance controller, our goal is to derive fixed controller gains without policy retraining or evaluation rollouts. PHRetune uses two complementary port-Hamiltonian models to connect offline assessment of the policy’s actions to controller retuning. An action-port surrogate fitted to demonstration transitions provides model-relative effort and energy estimates. We apply the frozen policy to recorded demonstration observations and assess its predicted action chunks against demonstration-derived budgets to determine an admissible action scale. A linear impedance setpoint-port model then relates action attenuation to controller softening through energy matching, providing a closed-form map from this scale to controller gains. The resulting gains are fixed before evaluation and introduce no additional runtime computation. 

# 3.1 Port-Hamiltonian Surrogate 

Conservative Hamiltonian models provide an energy-based description of dynamics but do not account for dissipation or external inputs [26, 27]. We therefore adopt a learned port-Hamiltonian representation to model end-effector dynamics with dissipation and external forcing [15, 28, 29, 30]. For a system with state x and input u, let the Hamiltonian be H = T + V where T and V denote the system’s kinetic and potential energy terms. The port-Hamiltonian dynamics therefore takes the form 

˙x = [ J (x) − D (x)] ∇H (x) + G (x) u. (1) Here, J is the power-preserving symplectic operator, D is the dissipation matrix, and G maps the input into the state dynamics, with constraints J (x) = −J (x)⊺ and D (x) = D (x)⊺ ⪰ 0. In the learned surrogate, the required matrix constraints are enforced directly through parameterization rather than penalty terms. PHRetune restricts the learned representation to end-effector motion rather than explicitly reconstructing the full joint-space dynamics of the manipulator. This reduced-order representation aligns with the end-effector action interface of the diffusion and VLA policies considered here. Its Hamiltonian serves as a model-relative storage function for comparing demonstrations with policy-generated motion. We distinguish two models within this framework. The learned action port model describes the action-to-motion dynamics and defines the demonstration-derived effort and energy envelopes. The analytical impedance setpoint port model then translates the resulting admissible action scale into fixed controller gains through virtual spring-energy matching.

# 4 Experiments 

We evaluate PHRetune through a series of experiments designed to test whether a controller gain computed from demonstrations can improve a frozen Diffusion Policy without empirical gain search. We compare the derived gain against nominal and principled tuning baselines, assess whether it lies on the empirically optimal gain plateau, and examine its consistency across data regimes. Unless otherwise stated, all gains are computed once before evaluation and applied only to the downstream controller; the policy, action representation, and inference procedure remain unchanged.

4.1.3 PHRetuned Energy Consistency 

The method rests on the premise that a policy which over-drives its demonstrations can be returned to their energy manifold, and that a compact port-Hamiltonian plant identified from those demonstrations suffices to determine the required gain. We verify this against energy measured from the true dynamics rather than from the learned plant used to derive the dose. The frozen policies are rolled out on all 30 tasks before and after retuning, five episodes each from the canonical initial states. For each eight-step (0.4 s) action chunk, we measure net task-space controller work in joules by integrating the controller force dotted with the end-effector velocity. We report controller work relative to each task’s demonstration envelope, with values above 1 indicating excess energy delivery. The average per-chunk energy falls from 1.47 to 0.93 on Spatial, 1.37 to 1.00 on Goal, and 2.11 to 1.26 on Object after retuning. Consequently, the retuned policies operate near the energy level of the demonstrations they were trained on. 

4.1.4 Shadow Mode Derivation 

The gains in Table 1 are derived in shadow mode. The frozen policy is applied as a pure function to the demonstration observations, and the effort and energy of its predicted action chunks are scored through the fitted plant against the demonstration budgets, with no hardware or simulator execution. Shadow mode requires only the demonstrations and one forward pass of the policy over them, which is what makes the derivation practical on hardware. To verify that the demonstration frames are a sufficient proxy for the policy’s own state distribution, we also derive the gains from closed-loop telemetry, rolling the frozen policy out for five log-only episodes per task and scoring its executed chunks the same way. As demonstrated in Table 3, the closed-loop route yields β∗ within 0.05 of the shadow-mode values. Furthermore, both derivation modes achieve equal success rate with minor fluctuations between task suites. A single scalar gain derived from the fitted plant lifts the success rate of every LIBERO suite, recovers most of the gap between 20 and 50 demonstrations, and achieves the highest observed success in an independent gain sweep. These improvements accompany reduced controller work toward demonstration levels. Since the derivation needs only the demonstrations and one forward pass of the frozen policy, the same procedure transfers unchanged to hardware execution and other policy backbones.

# 5 Conclusion 

We introduced PHRetune, an offline method that derives controller gains for frozen manipulation policies from their task demonstrations. A demonstration-calibrated port-Hamiltonian surrogate assesses policy predictions against effort and energy budgets, while a linear impedance model maps the resulting admissible action scale to fixed stiffness and damping gains. Gain selection remains blind to evaluation outcomes, requiring neither gain search nor additional robot or simulator rollouts. The resulting controller retuning preserves the policy, action representation, and inference procedure, introducing no additional runtime computation. Across LIBERO suites, PHRetune improved Diffusion Policy success, with the independently derived gains achieving the highest observed success rates. On four real-world manipulation tasks, PHRetuned Diffusion Policy outperformed the nominal policy and all evaluated baselines. The same procedure improves SmolVLA and OpenVLA-OFT success on all tasks, while reducing acceleration and jerk. Together, these findings show that task demonstrations can inform not only policy learning but also the controller that executes the learned behavior, providing a practical route to improved manipulation without changing the policy itself.
