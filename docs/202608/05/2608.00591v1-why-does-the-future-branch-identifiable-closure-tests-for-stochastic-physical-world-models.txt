## ABSTRACT 

Stochastic world models are usually judged by whether their predicted futures are accurate and calibrated. These criteria leave a decision-relevant ambiguity: the same conditional future distribution can arise because an observation aliases differ-ent physical states, or because dynamics remain random after the declared full state is fixed. We prove that this attribution is not identifiable from ordinary transition data, even with an optimal probabilistic predictor. We then introduce ClosurePairs ,an interventional evaluation protocol that crosses compatible microstates with re-peated exogenous disturbances. A two-way variance decomposition identifies state aliasing, noise, and their nonlinear interaction; an independent-repeat variant works when disturbances cannot be reused. On likelihood-equivalent Gaussian systems, paired supervision reduces alias-fraction error by 15 .96 × at identical test NLL. Across 18 nonlinear Langevin conditions, it reduces attribution MAE from 0.372 to 

0.051 and sensing regret from 0.0138 to 0.0003 without changing NLL. On a pixel-conditioned recurrent model, a frozen shared-state probe reduces alias-fraction MAE against a deep ensemble from 0.584 to 0.130 ID and from 0.630 to 0.170 

OOD over ten seeds. Finally, in a matched-total-variance REFINE/BRANCH test, a total-variance router reaches 66 .48% ± 1.06% accuracy, whereas ClosurePairs reaches 99 .99% ± 0.02% and improves selected NLL from −2.087 to −2.717 over five seeds. ClosurePairs therefore measures information about why futures branch that proper forecast scores cannot identify. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

World models support prediction and planning by representing a distribution over possible futures (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al., 2025). In physical settings, multiple futures are not an implementation nuisance: partial observation hides velocities and material parameters, coarse-graining creates memory, and thermal or randomized dynamics remain stochastic. A model should therefore predict a distribution rather than a single trajectory. Yet a predictive distribution does not explain its own width. Consider two action-conditioned environments. In the first, the observation omits a microstate variable that deterministically controls the future. In the second, the observation is a complete state but fresh process noise controls the future. The two environments can induce exactly the same p(y | z, a ). Every proper scoring rule gives an optimal predictor the same expected score in both (Gneiting & Raftery, 2007), although the correct intervention differs: acquire a finer observation in the first environment and preserve stochastic branches in the second. This is not the standard epistemic–aleatoric distinction (Kendall & Gal, 2017); it concerns two sources inside the environment’s conditional randomness, relative to a declared state and intervention boundary. We study this ambiguity as an identification problem . Let X be a full simulator state, Z = gr (X)

its observation at resolution r, A an action, E an exogenous disturbance, and Y = F (X, A, E ) afuture outcome. The law of total variance splits forecast uncertainty into variation of the microstate-conditional mean and residual variation at fixed microstate. We call these state aliasing and process stochasticity. Ordinary samples (Z, A, Y ) reveal only their sum. 1arXiv:2608.00591v1 [cs.AI] 1 Aug 2026 ClosurePairs augments evaluation data with two paired interventions: vary compatible microstates while holding (Z, A ) fixed, and repeat disturbances while holding (X, A ) fixed. In a simulator, crossing the same sampled disturbances with every compatible microstate enables a functional-ANOVA decomposition (Sobol, 1993). Crucially, shared random numbers alone are insufficient in nonlinear systems: a state–noise interaction must be estimated and assigned to the process component. When a simulator cannot replay a disturbance, independent nested repeats remain sufficient. Our contributions are: • We prove observational non-identifiability of uncertainty-source attribution and show that the ambiguity survives perfect likelihood and calibration. • We give unbiased nested and crossed estimators. The crossed estimator separately identifies state, noise, and state–noise interaction effects. We also prove that expected aliasing contracts under nested observation resolutions. • We implement ClosurePairs for Gaussian and mixture-density stochastic predictors and eval-uate it on analytic systems, nonlinear controlled Langevin dynamics, stochastic Pendulum dynamics, and a pixel-conditioned recurrent model with a shared predictive representation. • We connect attribution to two decisions: whether to acquire a refined observation and whether to retain multiple futures. Under matched total variance, source-aware routing separates REFINE from BRANCH while a magnitude-only router cannot. To our knowledge, this is the first world-model evaluation protocol to jointly use microstate-fiber interventions, repeated-noise counterfactuals, nested physical observations, and controlled actions to identify the mechanism of forecast branching. We do not claim to introduce stochastic closure, adaptive resolution, or the distinction between reducible and irreducible uncertainty.

## 5 LEARNING AND DECISION EVALUATION 

ClosurePairs can score a frozen model or supply auxiliary training targets. Our Gaussian MLP and mixture-density network (MDN) (Bishop, 1994) contain two parameter-disjoint branches. The predictive branch minimizes marginal NLL and outputs a mean/distribution and total variance bV .The attribution branch outputs ˆρ(z, a ) ∈ [0 , 1] and is trained only on paired labels: 

[Valias = ˆ ρ bV , [Vproc = (1 − ˆρ) bV . (10) Parameter separation guarantees that paired attribution cannot improve NLL by construction; this isolates information from predictive capacity. We also test a stronger shared-representation setting: a CNN encodes pixels, an action-conditioned GRU rolls out a latent state, and a five-component MDN emits the future. Lightweight attribution probes read that same recurrent state after the predictive network is frozen. The exact NLL control is therefore retained while testing whether paired labels make the mechanism decodable from a predictive representation. We report NLL, RMSE, total/component variance error, alias-fraction MAE, and a sensing decision. If a refined sensor removes aliasing variance ∆ at cost c under squared-error utility, the Bayes action is “refine” iff ∆ > c . The regret of the opposite action is |∆ − c|.

Corollary 1 (Decision significance) . The optimal refinement action is not identified from the observed kernel in Proposition 1. Plugging consistent ClosurePairs estimates into the rule is decision-consistent whenever |∆ − c| is bounded away from zero. 

For probabilistic prediction, the corresponding decision is whether to spend the next unit of computa-tion on a more informative observation (R EFINE ) or on multiple futures (B RANCH ). ClosurePairs supplies a mechanism label for this choice. The controlled routing experiment below gives both routers the same paired rollout matrix and the same predictive heads; it therefore tests whether variance composition , rather than magnitude, selects the appropriate head. It does not count paired rollouts as free computation or claim an end-to-end speedup. The pixel experiment instead tests distillation into a frozen predictive representation that does not require paired rollouts at evaluation time. 

## 6 EXPERIMENTS 

6.1 QUESTIONS AND PROTOCOL 

We ask: (Q1) Can observationally identical systems have arbitrarily different attribution? (Q2) Do paired labels recover it without changing forecast scores? (Q3) Does the result survive nonlinear multi-step dynamics and OOD actions? (Q4) Does the decomposition behave physically under a 4standard control system? (Q5) Does it survive pixel input, recurrent prediction, standard observational uncertainty baselines, and a downstream sensing decision? (Q6) When total variance is matched, does the decomposition select REFINE versus BRANCH? The earlier learned experiments use three seeds; the pixel study uses ten. All tests use held-out intervention groups. Confidence intervals bootstrap the independent seed-level differences; Wilcoxon tests are two-sided and paired. We construct two 12-step stochastic-ball mechanisms. In the state mechanism, a single frame aliases velocity but four frames identify it, so the appropriate operation is R EFINE . In the noise mechanism, the current state is concentrated but a future random horizontal impulse creates two modes, so the appropriate operation is B RANCH . We tune the impulse amplitude on training data so that the two mechanisms have matched mean total predictive variance. For every example, four compatible microstates are crossed with four disturbances. A total-variance threshold and a small ClosurePairs probe receive the same intervention matrix; the former uses only its total and the latter uses the three variance components and their total. Both select between the same four-frame Gaussian trajectory head and one-frame, five-component mixture head. Thus only the routing statistic differs. Across five seeds, the noise-to-state total-variance ratio is 1.021 ± 0.021 . Physical inference and paired interventions. Scientific-ML closure work learns unresolved effects (Sanderse et al., 2024); MemNO demonstrates benefits of memory under low-resolution projection (Ruiz et al., 2025); stochastic multiscale models explicitly couple macro and latent micro states (Ilersich & Nair, 2025). We do not propose another closure architec-ture. We provide an experimental contract for distinguishing the portion resolvable by state from randomness at a declared full-state boundary. 

## 8 LIMITATIONS AND CONCLUSION 

ClosurePairs requires more access than passive video: microstates must be reset or sampled within an observation fiber, and repeated disturbances require either independent trials or simulator seed control. Fiber sampling must match the target q(X | z, a ); otherwise estimates answer a shifted question. Reset drift biases the process term upward. Variance captures only second-order branching, not all distributional or topological differences. Our pixel recurrent experiment is a controlled 32 ×32 setting that predicts a scalar physical summary, not a large free-form video generator; scaling the protocol to video simulators is important future work. Finally, “process” is relative to the declared state and can shrink if that state is expanded. The matched-variance router is deliberately synthetic and receives paired interventions at evaluation time; its near-perfect accuracy validates a decision consequence of the estimand but does not establish passive online routing or net computational savings. The observation-only pixel probe is a first distillation result, but validation on a public pretrained video world model remains necessary. 8Within these boundaries, the conclusion is sharp: predictive accuracy and calibration cannot determine why futures branch. Paired interventions supply the missing information, yield unbiased estimators even with nonlinear state–noise interaction, and expose consequential sensing errors invisible to NLL. A stochastic physical world model should therefore report not only how broad its future distribution is, but whether that breadth closes when the world is observed more completely. AI USE STATEMENT

Learned Gaussian experiment. Each system uses 192 train groups with 16 microstates and eight disturbances, and 256 test groups with 20 microstates and ten disturbances. Matched-variance routing. Each of seeds 2027–2031 uses 6,000 training and 2,000 test trajectories at a 12-step horizon. ClosurePairs uses four compatible microstates crossed with four reusable disturbances. The REFINE head is a two-layer Gaussian trajectory MLP conditioned on four frames; the BRANCH head is a two-layer five-component mixture trajectory MLP conditioned on the current frame. The threshold is selected on training data. All three networks train for 1,800 minibatch steps. The animation uses a held-out matched pair, but all reported values aggregate the complete test sets.

## D PROTOCOL ASSUMPTIONS AND DIAGNOSTICS 

• Fiber validity: verify that all Xi map to the same declared observation and report the fiber sampler or importance weights. • Reset validity: run deterministic full-state repeats. Any positive variance diagnoses reset drift or hidden simulator state. • Randomness validity: distinguish independent nested trials from replayable crossed seeds. Never apply the naive shared-noise row estimator to a nonlinear system. • Resolution validity: observations must be nested for the monotonicity claim; compare the same microstate/action population. • Finite samples: report raw estimates and uncertainty intervals. Clipping and isotonic display projections must not be used to manufacture a hypothesis-test result. 13
